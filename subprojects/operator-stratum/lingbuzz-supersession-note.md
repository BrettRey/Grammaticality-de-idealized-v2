# Why I am withdrawing the operator-stratum claim
## A supersession note for LingBuzz/009706
In January 2026, I posted _Why clause structure is judged like tense and agreement: a coordination account of grammaticality_ at LingBuzz/009706. This note replaces its argument. The original file remains part of the version record, but I no longer claim that it identifies a new component of grammar, and I do not plan to submit that theoretical paper elsewhere.
## Summary
The January paper asked why speakers treat some readily understood departures as errors of grammar but treat others as matters of accent, register, or word choice. It proposed that many familiar targets of grammaticality judgments—such as clause type, polarity, agreement, tense, evidentiality, and switch-reference—belong to a common _operator stratum_. The proposal had two parts. These contrasts were said to form relatively closed paradigms, and their values were said to help determine what kind of move an utterance makes, who is committed to what, or how participants and referents are linked. The paper also claimed that this combination explained distinctive patterns of judgment, repair, processing, and preemption.

I no longer regard that theoretical architecture as original. Work by Lars Heltoft and his collaborators already brings together language-specific paradigms with grammatical content involving speech acts, viewpoint, responsibility, and relations among interlocutors.

The closest structural predecessor is Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft's account of grammar as organized in bounded, domain-specific paradigms. Heltoft's earlier work supplies the interactional side of the architecture. My paper gave these ideas a new label and attached broader predictions to them, but it did not establish a new stratum. Those claims are withdrawn. A narrower question about where repair is directed may still be worth testing, but it is an untested hypothesis for a new empirical paper, not a result of LingBuzz/009706.
## 1. What the paper claimed
The paper began from a selective pattern in grammaticality judgments. A wrong agreement marker, a mis-selected question particle, or an illicit clause pattern may be rejected as simply ungrammatical even when the intended message is easy to recover. By contrast, an accent that sounds wrong for the setting may be fully understood and criticized as pretentious, inauthentic, or inappropriate. A poor lexical choice may prompt negotiation over what the speaker meant. The paper asked why these intelligible departures are treated differently.

Its answer was not that morphology and syntax constitute grammar while sound, word choice, and social meaning do not. Languages can express grammatical contrasts through tone, intonation, particles, word order, signs, or inflection. The paper instead classified a form by the work its contrast performs.

The paper called the resulting difference _public accountability_. Some contrasts were said to provide mutually recognizable instructions for how an utterance is to be taken up. Accent, register, and open-class vocabulary could have powerful social or conceptual effects without normally serving as such instructions. On that basis, the paper proposed three strata: expression-shape constraints that make a token recognizable, operator constraints that organize coordination, and payload resources that contribute conceptual or indexical content. The proposed operator stratum was the middle category.

It called a contrast an _operator_ when two conditions held. First, its values belonged to a restricted conventional system: same subject versus different subject, affirmative versus negative, declarative versus interrogative, rather than an open list of possible descriptions. Second, selecting a value helped interlocutors determine how to treat the utterance: as a question rather than an assertion, as committing one participant rather than another, or as continuing one referent rather than introducing another. The paper called this contribution a _public-update role_. _Operator stratum_ was the proposed name for contrasts that combined the restricted system with that role.

This proposal was intended to explain {==why clause structure often patterns with tense and agreement==}{>>this was just the headline, not the complete goal<<}{id="c1" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:36:37.293Z"}. The claim was not that syntax is autonomous or that word order is intrinsically grammatical. It was that clausal resources frequently express values for clause type, argument linking, scope, and reference tracking. Where they do, they help tell interlocutors how to interpret and respond to the utterance. Tone, gesture, or a particle could perform the same work in another language.

The proposed explanation extended from systems to individual errors. Consider three cases. In Amele, dependent verbs in a clause chain distinguish whether a relevant participant continues or changes; selecting the wrong switch-reference value can change who is understood to perform the next event (Roberts 1988). English _goed_ still expresses past time, but it is not the conventional past-tense form of _go_. An accent or register mismatch can leave both the event and the kind of move clear while changing the speaker's social presentation. The paper predicted that responses would tend to target participant linking in the first case, the word form in the second, and the speaker's social positioning in the third.

The paper made several stronger claims on top of that comparison. It suggested that operators work like {==protocol headers==}{>>if you're not into computation, you probably don't know what a protocol header is<<}{id="c2" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:37:39.077Z"}{>>I've made this point before<<}{id="c3" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:38:01.195Z" re="c2"}: a small error could disrupt the interpretation of material in their scope. It predicted that categorical judgments, characteristic processing responses, and strong preemption would cluster around operator contrasts. It also treated forms such as _depend of_ and French _le hiver_ for _l'hiver_ as boundary cases between constructional or form licensing and operator value. These claims were theoretical proposals. The paper reported no experiment or corpus study demonstrating the predicted clustering or repair differences.
## 2. What Heltoft's work had already established
I missed the closest precedent because the relevant argument is distributed across several works, much of it outside the literature in which I first framed the problem. {--That explains the error; it does not preserve the originality claim.--}{id="s1" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:38:33.206Z"}
### 2.1 Grammar and the public organization of an utterance
Heltoft's 1981 article already asks how conventional linguistic form indicates {==what aspect of a communicative act is open to doubt==}{>>this seems rather distant from what my paper was doing<<}{id="c4" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:38:57.542Z"}. Its framework distinguishes questions about understanding, truth, sincerity, and the correctness of an inference or norm. The linguistic analysis shows that focus placement and verb class help select among those possibilities. _Did you_ **_SEE_** _that the accused threw stones?_ can ask for the perceptual basis of the claim. **_DID_** _you see that the accused threw stones?_ can instead question the claimed seeing. The words describing the event remain largely constant, but the conventional form helps identify what is being challenged (Heltoft 1981).

Heltoft's 2005 account of Danish dialogic particles makes a related point without reducing every public contribution to clause type. The proposition ‘he failed his exam’ can remain constant. The particle _jo_ presents it as something the speaker does not expect to be contradicted about; _vel_ invites the other's agreement; _vist_ attributes the information to another source; and _da_ rejects a contrary position. Heltoft analyzes these particles as organizing relations among speaker and interlocutor positions. {==They do not merely add descriptive content, and they do not all perform the same illocutionary work==}{>>and they don't seem particularly closely related to what counts as grammatical, though I can start to see some vague distant relevance<<}{id="c5" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:40:02.228Z"} (Heltoft 2005: 94–99).

_Grammatik over det Danske Sprog_ (Hansen and Heltoft 2011; GDS) incorporates {==this approach==}{>>What approach? I don't see anything so far about grammaticality<<}{id="c6" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:40:43.144Z"} into a comprehensive grammar. GDS {==treats grammar as conventional relations between expression and content, where the content may include illocutionary distinctions, feedback, metacommunication, and textual organization==}{>>this is standard functionalist fare<<}{id="c7" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:41:20.025Z"}. Its analyses include Danish word-order contrasts that contribute to the difference between declaratives and polar questions. They also include a two-member complementizer paradigm: _om_ ‘whether’ marks alternative truth value, while _at_ ‘that’ is neutral in that respect (GDS XIV §4.2). {==This is very close to the kind of _that/whether_ contrast my paper treated as an operator.==}{>>um, only on a very surface level<<}{id="c8" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:41:46.695Z"}
### 2.2 Grammar as language-specific paradigms
The other half of my proposal was the claim that the relevant values belong to restricted paradigms. GDS already treats grammatical forms as systems of contrasting expression and content, and it extends such organization beyond ordinary inflection.

Jakobsen and Heltoft (2012) make the point through the history of German mood. They argue that modern German has separate mood paradigms. One contrasts assertive with directive force. Another distinguishes whether the speaker vouches for the represented reality. A third distinguishes whether the current speaker is responsible for an utterance or merely reports someone else's. The analysis separates a paradigm's conventional value from contextual effects that a value may often have.

{==Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft (2015)==}{>>this finally starts to seem relevant<<}{id="c9" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:43:03.509Z"} state the general architecture explicitly. On their account, a grammatical paradigm has a bounded set of members, a recurring domain in which the contrast applies, and a language-specific content frame: the meaning dimension along which its members contrast. A value must be selected whenever the domain applies, and the members stand in a marked–unmarked relation. Paradigms need not be inflectional. They can be realized through word order and constructional syntax. Grammaticalization can therefore create or reorganize a paradigm rather than merely turn a lexical item into a grammatical morpheme.

Heltoft's recent analysis of Danish case continues the same programme. It keeps apart the content of a case contrast, the constructions in which it applies, the forms that express its values, and variation in how speakers distribute those forms (Heltoft 2025). {==That separation matters directly to examples such as _goed_ and _le hiver_: an intended grammatical value may remain recoverable even when its conventional expression is wrong.==}{>>I'm not sure if this is relevant to my point or not; it's far too compressed to be helpful<<}{id="c10" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:43:43.859Z"}

Read together, these works already contain the central architecture of my proposal. {==They treat grammar as conventional form–content organization. Grammatical content may concern speech acts, viewpoint, responsibility, and interlocutor relations==}{>>I never saw this as new or original. This is the standard functionalist view. The point is that a word is also a form–value correspondence, but it's not grammar and choosing the wrong one doesn't typically get you an ungrammatical construction.<<}{id="c11" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:44:58.403Z"}. Grammatical alternatives form language-specific paradigms, and a value is distinguished from both its expression and its contextual effects. {--Heltoft does not use the label _operator stratum_, and he does not make my proposed repair prediction. But a new label and an untested consequence do not make the underlying architecture mine.--}{id="s2" by="user" at="2026-08-23T22:46:20.456Z"}

Heltoft does not divide linguistic resources into my three strata. That taxonomy was my formulation. But its middle category was identified by combining paradigmatic organization with grammatical content bearing on utterance and interaction. That combination was not new, and my paper did not show that the three-way division predicted anything beyond the distinctions used to define it.

The attribution issue is easy to miss outside Danish and Scandinavian functional linguistics. GDS is a major reference grammar of Danish, while some of the English-language articles most relevant here have circulated modestly in the wider literature. Uneven visibility does not alter priority.

Adopting this precedent also does not require treating Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft's paradigm criteria as the only possible definition of grammar. Boye and Harder (2012), for example, argue that closure and obligatoriness are consequences rather than defining properties of grammatical status. That disagreement remains important. For the present correction, the decisive point is narrower: the combination I presented as new already has a substantial and integrated precedent.
## 3. What I am withdrawing
I withdraw the claim that _operator stratum_ identifies a newly discovered component of grammar. The label redescribed a region of grammatical organization for which Heltoft's work already supplied much of the theory.

I also withdraw the claim that combining restricted paradigms with a role in public coordination was itself the paper's theoretical contribution. Heltoft's work connects paradigms with distinctions involving speech acts, responsibility, viewpoint, and interlocutor relations. My formulation was broader and used different terminology, but breadth and terminology do not establish priority.

The proposed explanations of judgment, processing, preemption, and repair were also too strong for the evidence. Identifying a paradigm and describing its content does not show why a particular speaker corrected a particular token. _Goed_ may be corrected because English conventionally requires _went_ as the past form; _depend of_ because _depend_ conventionally selects _on_ or _upon_ in that complement. An accent may be corrected because of social sanction. A wrong switch-reference value may be corrected because it changes participant linking, because it violates a taught norm, or both. Those causes have to be distinguished empirically.

Finally, I withdraw the information-theoretic explanation. The protocol-header comparison was a metaphor, not a measured model. The paper did not quantify downstream uncertainty or derive the claimed effects.

This reassessment was not produced by a referee report. The paper's desk rejection from _Functions of Language_ concerned journal scope. The change follows from the later examination of Heltoft's work and from separating a useful description from an untested causal explanation.
## 4. What may remain worth testing
One question survives the theoretical correction. Suppose a speaker selects the wrong value in a paradigm—for example, a same-subject marker where a different-subject value is intended. Compare that with a case in which the intended value is clear but its form is wrong, as with _goed_. Does the first case more often prompt clarification of who did what, while the second prompts replacement of the form?

That contrast alone would not be surprising, because the examples are defined by what they preserve. The stronger question is comparative. Before examining repair, identify several paradigms and independently measure how much changing their values changes clause type, commitment, or participant and reference linking. Then ask whether paradigms with larger effects show a larger difference between value-directed and form-directed repair. Category placement, the communicative measure, and the repair categories would all have to be fixed before the outcomes were examined.

Existing work connects grammar with the organization and repair of conversation (e.g. Fox, Hayashi, and Jasperson 1996), but the literature reviewed for this note has not identified that particular comparison. If the independently assigned measure failed to predict the repair difference after construction, frequency, processing, schooling, and social sanction were accounted for, the explanatory hypothesis would fail. Descriptions of the individual paradigms could remain correct.

As of August 2026, I have not identified a corpus that can support this test. If suitable archival interaction data are available, the study may be possible without recruiting participants. If it is undertaken, it should begin as a new empirical paper with its own title, preregistration, and record. It should not be presented as confirmation of the operator-stratum theory.
## 5. Consequences for the version record
LingBuzz/009706 should now be cited only as a superseded proposal. This note is its current endpoint.

My companion preprint _Grammaticality de-idealized_ (Reynolds 2026) has used the operator-stratum proposal when discussing the possible relation between grammatical values and repair. It cannot treat LingBuzz/009706 as an established foundation. Any retained repair claim must be presented as the open empirical question described above and must acknowledge the Heltoft programme.

Correcting the originality claim does not show that the remaining empirical question is unimportant. It changes what an answer could contribute. The contribution would be evidence for a specific repair contrast beyond the theory already available—not a new name for that theory.
