# Five levels, six explanatory questions
The word “grammaticality” is used for objects at several explanatory levels. Here “level” marks a difference in what is being explained, not a hierarchy in which one object is more fundamental than another. A formal theory licenses a representation; a community sustains a linguistic norm; a token conforms or fails to conform; a speaker issues a judgment; and an experimenter records a rating. These claims aren’t interchangeable. A formal grammar may fail to generate a community-licensed form because the model undergenerates. A speaker may reject a licensed form because it belongs to an unfamiliar dialect. And an experimenter may record a low rating caused by processing difficulty rather than by any grammatical fault. Treating all three outcomes as measurements of one latent yes–no {++grammaticality++}{#s31} property hides the dependencies that need explanation. Table [1](#tab:levels) separates the five kinds of object introduced above.

## Practice-dependent, response-independent standards
The answer has two parts: coordinated linguistic practice explains how a relation supplies a standard of correctness, while participation in recurrent constructional assembly explains why that standard is grammatical.

A linguistic norm is more than a frequent event type. It supplies a standard against which novel or disputed tokens can conform or fail to conform. Low-token- frequency constructions can be securely licensed, while frequent innovations can remain restricted or unstable. A norm needn’t be explicitly represented as a rule, prescribed by an institution, morally obligatory, or identically stored by every speaker. It’s socially sustained when production and interpretation converge on a relation, participants rely on it in forming and understanding new tokens, and counterfactual substitutions or omissions make systematic differences within a scoped repertoire.

Coseriu ([1952](#ref-coseriu1952)) supplies a precedent. His distinction among system, norm, and speech separates functional possibilities, customary social realization, and individual production. Later presentations also distinguish congruence, idiomatic correctness, and situational appropriateness ([Pardo Llibrer 2021](#ref-pardollibrer2021); [Soto Vergara 2022](#ref-sotovergara2022)). This account inherits the idea that correctness relative to a historical language and appropriateness in a situation are different evaluative relations. It adds a question that Coseriu’s distinctions don’t settle on their own: which contribution to expression formation makes the relevant norm grammatical?

The paper explicates public repertoire status for conventional linguistic classification and descriptive grammar. Other legitimate targets for “grammaticality” remain. Psycholinguistic accounts can model individual competence and innovations not yet sustained more widely. A formal grammar can model an idiolect or a public variety. In each case, derivability establishes status relative to that model and target; it doesn’t by itself establish public licensing. Conversely, speakers can reliably follow a public relation without consciously representing, correcting, or institutionally enforcing it. Individual, model-relative, and public claims can inform one another, but none is a proxy for the others ([Poulsen 2012](#ref-poulsen2012grammaticality); [Leivada and Westergaard 2020](#ref-leivadawestergaard2020)).

The first question, norm constitution, receives a functional answer. A grammatical norm relates already identifiable {==signs==}{>>"constructions"?<<}{#c14}, exponents, positions, or values within a recurrent construction. Subject–verb agreement, for example, relates the number value expressed by a subject noun phrase {++(construction 1)++}{#s36} to the form of a finite verb {++(construction 2)++}{#s37}. French article elision relates a selected determiner value {++(C1)++}{#s38} to its realization in a conditioned position {++(C2)++}{#s39}. A complement construction can relate a head {++(C1)++}{#s40}, a complement function {++(C2)++}{#s41}, and the phrase that realizes that function {++(C3)++}{#s42}. These relations differ in generality, but each helps determine how linguistic material is assembled as an instance of a recurrent construction{==.==}{>>I'm not sure about numbering the constructions, but I think some way of making them more salient *as constructions* is important<<}{#c15}

“Assembly” here names a functional relation, not a claim that speakers mechanically join a sequence of pre-existing pieces{==.==}{>>add a footnote explicitly distinguishing from "Merge"<<}{#c16} An assembly relation can hold simultaneously across a construction, be expressed prosodically or gesturally, or have no overt exponent. The term says only that the relation helps determine whether a performance conforms to a recurrent expression type{==.==}{>>should we have more comparison with how CxG would talk about this?<<}{#c17}

Other conventions govern different aspects of the same performance. A lexical convention establishes that _winter_ is a word with a particular content. English phonotactics constrains the possible sound shape of that word. Orthography specifies its written representation. A register convention can make the word or construction more appropriate in one activity than another. Words, {==sounds==}{>>segments? something that doesn't exclude signed languages<<}{#c18}, and social meanings can participate in grammatical assembly, but merely being conventional or meaningful doesn’t make a relation grammatical. The question is whether the relation organizes how independently identifiable linguistic resources combine or realize a constructional value.

The English double-copula pattern in _The thing is, is that we left early_ illustrates the difference between recognizability and grammatical analysis. The sequence is readily understood, and it recurs in conditioned spontaneous-speech environments ([Coppock and Staum 2004](#ref-coppockStaum2004double)). Those facts make it a candidate construction rather than random repetition. Its grammatical status depends on whether the two copulas occupy independently supported positions in a recurrent construction, not simply on the fact that speakers understand it or that editors dislike it. The same reasoning applies to the disputed examples throughout this paper.

Defective performances require three objects to remain distinct. First{++,++}{#s43} there is the utterance or inscription itself. Second{++,++}{#s44} there is its recoverable affiliation with a construction: listeners can recognize *_le hiver_ as an attempted definite noun phrase. Third{++,++}{#s45} there is its conformity or defect relative to each relation in that construction. The noun, definiteness, gender, and number can be preserved even when article realization fails. Calling the performance defective doesn’t require denying that it is recognizably an instance of the broader construction.

This functional distinction must be identified without using the response it{++'++}{#s47} {--i--}{#s46}s later meant to explain. One useful counterfactual holds the intended words, constructional values, and situation constant while changing the disputed relation. If the change determines whether the material conforms to the recurrent construction, the relation contributes to grammatical assembly. If it changes only possible {==sound shape==}{>>phonetic realization<<}{#c20}, spelling, lexical fit, or suitability for the occasion, a different norm is at issue. Recurrent social reliance supplies the proposed difference between a norm and an accidental one-off pattern.

Miller ([2021](#ref-miller2021words)) provides a near precedent, though not a complete argument. He treats word instances as governed by community-relative local performance standards, sustained by external social mechanisms while internal mechanisms enable the production of new instances. On Miller’s account, the standards are conditions on conforming word instances, not summaries of observed frequencies. Miller leaves their nature open. I draw the further conclusion that stable clustering alone can’t supply normativity: a regular distribution becomes a standard only when the linguistic practice relies on it in producing and interpreting further instances.

I call that dependence _constitutive reliance_. Appropriately situated participants are disposed to produce and interpret novel performances through the relation. Their dispositions are sufficiently coordinated to stabilize new instances of the same recurrent constructional organization, and counterfactual substitutions or omissions systematically preserve or disrupt that organization. Corpus distributions, elicitation, and responses provide evidence about this pattern of reliance without constituting it.

The reliance matters normatively because participants use the relation not merely to predict which forms will occur, but to produce and interpret performances as instances of the same constructional type. A departure can therefore convey its intended message while failing a practice-internal condition for a conforming instance. This is the thin sense in which the performance is defective relative to the repertoire.

Correctness is thin and practice-internal. A performance is correct or defective relative to a repertoire insofar as it conforms or fails to conform to a standard for constructing or realizing expressions. This needn’t amount to moral obligation, institutional approval, actual correction, majority preference, or statistical expectedness. Transmission, teaching, correction, and repair can maintain the norm through time without being part of what sustains it at a given time. A token-level judgment may misidentify the defect, and the absence of correction doesn’t remove it. An innovation can be surprising while conforming to an existing construction, whereas a familiar shibboleth can attract condemnation without any assembly failure. Participation in constructional assembly supplies the proposal’s criterion of grammar-specificity: the standard governs relations among signs, exponents, or values in recurrent expression formation. This is a functional explication of grammatical status, not a conclusion forced by recurrence or social coordination alone.

Not every performance has to fit one box. Lexical valence can help determine whether a complement construction is licensed, and item-specific allomorphy can realize a grammatical value. Such performances contain more than one relevant relation. But the analysis can’t posit a grammatical relation merely because some respondents call the problem “grammar”, or a lexical relation merely because others call it “wording”. Sign identity, conventional value, contribution to construction formation, and repertoire scope {~~must~>have to~~}{#s48} be identified independently of those labels.

By contrast, an English pitch contour can distinguish a statement from a questioning use of the same words ([Ladd 2008](#ref-ladd2008)). One performance can contain multiple independently supported relations of different types. These intersections don’t make every linguistic norm grammatical. They show why status belongs to a particular relation as it operates in a repertoire, not to a sound, word, or social meaning considered in isolation.
## Individuating the assembly relation
The preceding subsection said that grammar relates “already identifiable” signs, exponents, positions, or values. “Identifiable” doesn’t mean already classified as lexical or grammatical. The point is to avoid inventing a relation solely because a disputed performance has received a grammar label. The analysis proceeds in three stages.

First, the proposed parts need independent support. A sign is supported when its form and contribution recur across combinations. A bound exponent can be identified by its contrast with other realizations in the same conditioned position. A construction is supported when a distribution and interpretation recur under substitution, omission, or embedding. These patterns identify the possible signs, values, and constructional sites without yet deciding which norm type governs them.

Second, a relational analysis must explain more than the stored identity of one large expression. Its proposed parts should recur or contrast independently, and varying one should reveal a dependency over an independently motivated position, value, or class. The analysis should also predict behaviour in substitutions, derived forms, or constructional environments not used to posit it. Productivity and structural separability can provide that evidence, but neither is required: an item-restricted construction can still contain a determinate assembly relation.

A whole-sign analysis is favoured when the supposed parts don’t preserve their contributions outside the expression and the proposed internal relation predicts nothing beyond an item list. The appropriate unit of analysis is determined by comparison. Competing whole-sign and relational analyses are evaluated by how much independently observed distribution and interpretation they capture without adding a dependency solely for the disputed case. If the evidence supports both analyses equally, the unit of analysis is epistemically indeterminate. If different groups or activities sustain different organizations, the repertoire itself is unsettled at the declared scope. Neither result licenses an automatic verdict of “partly grammatical”.

Third, an analysis containing more than one relation has an additional burden. Each proposed relation must be attested outside the response being explained and must make a distinct counterfactual prediction. Inflection inside an idiom, for example, can be separated from the idiom’s lexical identity only where the inflectional relation recurs elsewhere and predicts changes within the idiom. Respondents dividing between “grammar” and “wording” isn’t enough to establish two relations.

Simple contrasts show what the three stages are meant to distinguish. In _These books is useful_, the words and proposition are recoverable, but the number relation between subject and finite verb fails. In _strong rain_, ordinary adjective–noun modification succeeds, but the lexical combination is unusual compared with _heavy rain_. A novel word beginning with /ŋ/ can conflict with ordinary English word shape without disrupting a constructional value. And a Japanese plain-style verb addressed {++by a young student++}{#s49} to a professor can be linguistically well formed while being unsuitable for the relationship. The four performances may all attract negative reactions, but the independently identified relation at issue differs.

English complementation and collocation provide a harder contrast. In _depend on an answer_, the meaning of _depend_ introduces a semantic dependency relation. In the clause, a PP realizes the complement function within the VP, and _on_ heads that PP. The verb, the complement function, the PP realization, and possible complements of the preposition recur independently. Related forms such as _dependence on_ and _dependent on_ preserve the head-specific preposition. This supports a set of dependencies connecting semantic relation, syntactic complement function, phrasal category, and an item-restricted choice of preposition.

Not every recurring part of that dependency set is thereby grammatical. Collocations and morphological families also recur across forms. The relevant evidence includes preservation of the semantic relation across different complements, participation in complement alternations, recurrence across derived forms, contrasts with non-complement uses, and whether the preposition realizes a constructional relation rather than merely accompanying a lexical family. The semantic-to-syntactic and complement-to-PP dependencies clearly contribute to grammatical assembly. On the evidence considered here, the final choice of _on_ remains indeterminate between lexically indexed grammatical licensing and lexical collocation – in more traditional terms, between grammar and lexis.

The comparison with _heavy rain_ clarifies the distinction. Replacing _heavy_ with _strong_ can leave the adjective–noun construction grammatical while producing a lexically odd combination. If _depend on_ turned out to be stored only as a larger lexical construction whose proposed parts made no independent predictions, the item-specific grammatical analysis would likewise lose support; the final preposition restriction would then be lexical rather than grammatical. The opposite result is also possible. If _heavy_ developed into a conventional intensity exponent over an independently supported class of nouns, its selection would become part of grammatical assembly. These conclusions are fixed from distribution and interpretation before corrections or grammar labels are examined.
## Paradigms, constructional dependencies, and discursive status
Strict paradigms provide a strong case of grammatical assembly. Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft ([2015](#ref-norgardsorensenheltoft2015)) characterize a grammatical paradigm through a recurring domain, a language-specific semantic frame, a bounded set of alternatives, obligatory choice in the domain, and marked–unmarked organization. Their account extends beyond inflection to word order and constructional syntax. Hansen and Heltoft ([2011](#ref-hansenheltoft2011gds)) likewise allow prototype and radial organization within their account of Danish grammar. These analyses show how a set of values and their realizations can be organized within a repertoire without reducing grammar to segmental morphology.

Paradigmhood doesn’t define all grammar under the assembly-norm proposal. Constructional dependencies can be productive without a closed set of values, and item-specific relations can depend on more schematic licensing relations. The assembly-norm account places these relations by asking what they contribute to the formation of constructional tokens. Bounded alternatives, obligatoriness, and markedness are powerful ways of organizing grammatical assembly, not universal conditions on it.

Boye and Harder ([2012](#ref-boyeharder2012)) offer a different answer to norm constitution. On their view, a grammatical expression is conventionally ancillary: it supports another expression rather than carrying the main point of the utterance. Grammatical meaning is consequently _discursively secondary_, whereas lexical meaning is capable of becoming primary. Closed paradigms and obligatoriness may correlate with that status, but they don’t define it.

Boye and Harder diagnose discourse status through focusability and addressability. In _Jones is gonna call her tomorrow_, for example, a reply such as _How?_ can ask how Jones will call, but it can’t independently question the meaning expressed by _gonna_. Nor does ordinary sentence stress make _gonna_ the principal point. A lexical verb such as _call_ is more readily focused or questioned. These contrasts support their analysis because they show what can and can’t become discourse-primary.

The assembly-norm proposal agrees that substance and frequency don’t define grammar, but it treats discourse status differently. The disagreement becomes concrete in Boye and Harder’s classification of English demonstratives. Boye and Harder ([2012, 20–21](#ref-boyeharder2012)) classify free-standing _that_ as lexical because it can be selectively focused and addressed, unlike grammatical _it_. In _I want THAT, not THIS_, for example, the demonstrative can plainly {==carry the contrastive point==}{>>but can't "I want it not them" do the same? Contrastive it is rare, but contrastive him isn't<<}{#c22}.

That discourse behaviour coexists with independently described constructional organization. English _this_, _these_, _that_, and _those_ form a system crossing proximal–distal deixis with singular–plural number, and demonstrative determinatives function as determiner or fused determiner–head (standing alone as the nominal head) in nominal constructions ([Huddleston and Pullum 2002](#ref-huddleston2002)). Under the assembly criterion stated above, the deictic–number and nominal-construction relations are grammatical even when the demonstrative expression is discourse-primary. Focusability and grammatical assembly cross-classify in an attested case; the disagreement isn’t merely a possibility built into the two definitions.

This contrast also reveals a difference in {==bearer==}{>>this is the only token of bearer<<}{#c24}. Boye and Harder assign lexical or grammatical status to expressions and meanings. This proposal assigns grammatical status to a relation in which an expression participates. It can accept their observation that _that_ carries the principal point of a token while denying that this prominence makes the demonstrative’s deictic–number and nominal-construction relations lexical. Conversely, an ancillary parenthetical or discourse marker isn’t grammatical merely because it normally remains in the background; it {~~must~>has to~~}{#s59} participate in an independently supported assembly relation.

The disagreement is direct but doesn’t make Boye and Harder’s diagnostics irrelevant. Conventional discourse-secondary status may explain attention, processing, and the behaviour of many familiar grammatical expressions. The constitutive question is whether it can be the sole identity condition for grammar once a discourse-primary expression participates in independently established constructional relations. The comparison {~~must~>has to~~}{#s50} examine both kinds of evidence without defining either property from the grammar labels to be explained.

## Multiple relations and epistemic profiles
One performance can have different statuses relative to different relations without making evidence, reality, and judgment equally fuzzy. The relations can have different functions {~~– for example,~>(e.g.,~~}{#s60}  a grammatical complement relation together with a lexically restricted preposition{++)++}{#s62} {--–--}{#s61} and different activities can sustain different organizations. Item restriction, low productivity, and narrow distribution don’t by themselves make an assembly relation less grammatical. A lexeme-specific relation can be determinately {==grammatical==}{>>we have, I think, two meanings for grammatical: 1) having to do with grammar 2) being acceptable within the grammar. Is this a problem, and if so, what's the solution?<<}{#c25} if it determinately realizes a grammatical value. Such properties weaken generality or evidential support only when they also leave the contribution to expression formation uncertain or the relation unsettled across the declared repertoire.

Evidence and judgments vary separately. The analyst may have strong or weak evidence that a relation exists, and a respondent may apply a categorical or hesitant label. A sharp schoolroom correction doesn’t prove a sharply grammatical relation, just as uncertain corpus evidence doesn’t prove a fuzzy norm.

An _assembly profile_ is the analyst’s estimate of norm organization. It records evidence about whether form and value are coupled at a constructional site, how alternatives and selection are organized, and where the relation is licensed in the declared repertoire{==.==}{>>cite Pullum's suggestion to use Goodman's reflective equilibrium?<<}{#c26} Production, interpretation, substitution, omission, productivity, and opportunity-normalized distribution bear on those questions. A descriptive grammar contributes through the patterns it documents, not by lending the analysis its prior category label.
# How tokens fail linguistic norms
Token application, the second of the six questions, begins only after a candidate norm and repertoire have been identified. It asks which relation, if any, a particular performance fails. The asterisk in *_These books is useful_ doesn’t answer that question. It tells us that someone has classified the example negatively; the analyst {--must--}{#s51} still {++has to++}{#s52} locate the conflict between plural subject and singular finite verb.

An analyst can make a prior epistemic error by applying a norm from the wrong community, activity, medium, or period. That mistake concerns repertoire scope; it isn’t a defect in the performance. Once the repertoire is specified, Table [4](#tab:token-failures) lists possible sites of token failure. It{++'++}{#s64} {--i--}{#s63}s a diagnostic menu, not a sequence through which every performance passes. The entries also separate two senses of “form error”. A nonce word can violate ordinary English phonotactics, in which case sound shape is at issue. Or a repertoire can exclude one phonological form as the realization of a grammatical value, as when *_goed_ uses the productive past exponent where the lexeme-specific realization is _went_. In the second example, phonological material is the surface of a grammatical realization fault.

Value errors require particular care. Three cases have to remain separate.

1. An _internal value incompatibility_ arises when linguistic material in the token supplies values that conflict: agreement features disagree, a temporal construction conflicts with an overt temporal specification, or a switch-reference marker conflicts with the subjects supplied by its clause chain. *_These books is useful_ is straightforward: plural _these books_ and singular _is_ supply incompatible number instructions. This can be a grammatical failure.

2. In _external value misapplication_, a licensed grammatical value is successfully expressed but is false, misleading, or inappropriate for the described situation. A yesterday-past form used for an event that actually happened today needn’t be ungrammatical. The grammatical tense choice may be false of the situation without conflicting with any other linguistic material.

3. In a _message-selection error_, a speaker chooses a grammatical competitor that doesn’t express the intended message. The production is mistaken, but the resulting expression can remain grammatical: saying _She left_ when one intended _He left_ doesn’t make the produced sentence {++inherently++}{#s65} grammatically defective.

Of these three value cases, only internal incompatibility establishes a grammatical fault by itself. The distinction follows from the constitutive account: grammaticality concerns conformity to the relations that assemble the linguistic token, not direct correspondence between that token and the world or the speaker’s private intention. Semantic and pragmatic theories are needed to explain those further relations. This conclusion concerns token status. What a respondent notices and why they react remain the separate questions of target attribution and response causation.

Selecting a {~~poor~>inapposite~~}{#s66} but licensed word supplies the lexical analogue. The sign keeps its conventional identity and meaning, and the surrounding construction can be well formed; the selected content is simply inapt. A register mismatch can likewise preserve the proposition and the form’s conventional social-indexical value while deploying that value in an unsuitable situation. These choices may be serious, accountable, or sharply sanctioned. Their social importance doesn’t move the failed relation into grammatical assembly.
# What semantic and pragmatic explanations explain
The third question concerns semantic and pragmatic consequence: what happens to meaning and interaction when a performance conforms to or fails a linguistic relation? Semantic and pragmatic theory enter here in two ways. First, they help describe the content organized by grammar. {==Tense, definiteness, evidential source, clause type, reference continuity, and commitment are examples rather than an exhaustive list.==}{>>and why are we listing them here?<<}{#c27} Second, they explain the consequences of combining those values: an attempted interpretation may conflict, remain underspecified, invite coercion, or alter the course of a conversation. The first contribution helps specify what a norm organizes; the second explains what conformity or failure does.
## Semantic repairability
Abrusán ([2019, 329–31, 344–48](#ref-abrusan2019)) asks a close relative of the present question: why can a failed interpretation feel ungrammatical in one case, semantically anomalous in another, and pragmatically infelicitous in a third? A _negative-polarity item_ such as _anything_, which ordinarily requires negation or another licensing environment, is often classified as ungrammatical in *_John saw anything_ even though the proposed licensing condition is semantic. By contrast, _My toothbrush is pregnant_ is readily understood as a selectional absurdity rather than as a syntactic failure. The contrast shows why locating a source in semantics doesn’t yet predict how speakers will classify the fault.

Abrusán et al. ([2021](#ref-abrusanetal2021shift)) explain an important part of that contrast through type level, locality, and semantic shift. A local conflict involving a relatively specific type can sometimes be repaired by adjusting a word’s interpretation in context. The adjective in _heavy traffic_, for example, doesn’t retain exactly its ordinary physical-weight interpretation. But conflicts involving high-level types such as question formation or determiner logic may leave no nearby meaning-preserving shift. Because the type system and permitted shifts are motivated independently of the eventual anomaly label, Abrusán and colleagues explain rather than merely rename a classification distinction.

That semantic explanation has a defined explanatory domain. It can explain responses where composition or interpretation supplies the trouble. But it doesn’t, by itself, decide whether French article non-elision, a suppletive past form, a pure phonotactic violation, or a spelling error belongs to grammar. Those examples remain easy to understand because semantic composition has largely succeeded. The assembly-norm proposal and the repairability account overlap when grammar organizes semantic composition, but they answer different questions.

This difference also yields distinct failure conditions. If type level and shiftability fully explain why respondents classify a semantic conflict as ungrammatical rather than anomalous, an additional assembly-based explanation of that response is unnecessary. But Abrusán’s resources challenge the present answer to norm constitution only if they also explain what makes the failed linguistic relation grammatical. Explaining a response and constituting the norm remain separate tasks.

{++This means that++}{#s68} {--S--}{#s67}emantics is {--therefore--}{#s69} neither outside grammar nor identical to it. A tense construction can be grammatical partly because it conventionally assembles a temporal value, and semantic theory explains what that value is. If two linguistically encoded temporal instructions conflict, semantic theory may also explain the resulting anomaly. But a further premise is still needed to show whether the convention that assembled those instructions belongs to grammar, lexical semantics, or a pragmatic practice.
## Discourse consequences without a unitary public state
Some grammatical choices do more than contribute to propositional content (the situation an utterance depicts). They also help determine what conversational move the speaker has made and what an interlocutor is now entitled, expected, or able to do. An interrogative construction {++typically++}{#s70} presents an issue for response, whereas a declarative construction ordinarily presents a claim. An evidential marker can indicate whether the speaker presents {==themself==}{>>avoid<<}{#c28} or someone else as the source of the information. A switch-reference marker can indicate whether the next clause continues with the same participant or shifts to another. I call these _discourse consequences_: effects on the public organization of interaction, not merely on the situation described.

Several traditions converge on this terrain from different directions. Heltoft ([1981](#ref-heltoft1981intersubjectivity)) separates grammaticality from comprehensibility in his treatment of epistemic verbs: a form can remain understandable even when it fails a grammatical relation, and the grammatical distinction can concern how the speaker positions a claim. Heltoft ([2005](#ref-heltoft2005modality)) distinguishes the illocutionary role of mood (the kind of communicative act performed) from the interlocutionary role of dialogic particles, which helps organize the relation between one turn and the next. Hansen and Heltoft ([2011](#ref-hansenheltoft2011gds)) and Jakobsen and Heltoft ([2012](#ref-jakobsenheltoft2012paradigm)) develop the connection between paradigmatic organization and discourse function. Interactional research starts from the organization of conversation and treats grammar as a resource for constructing sequences of action and locating and repairing trouble ([Fox et al. 1996](#ref-foxetal1996repair); [Muntigl and Ventola 2010](#ref-muntiglventola2010); [Dingemanse et al. 2014](#ref-dingemanseblythedirksmeyer2014)).

These traditions support the claim that grammatical organization can have public consequences. They don’t support the converse claim that anything with a public consequence is grammatical. An interjection such as _oh_ can redirect the next turn without thereby belonging to a grammatical paradigm {==([Ameka 1992](#ref-ameka1992))==}{>>does Ameka make claims about grammatical paradigms?<<}{#c29}. Conversely, a grammatical distinction can make little difference in a particular performance. In _These books are useful_, plurality is already expressed by _these_ and _books_, so the agreement realized by _are_ adds little locally. The agreement paradigm may nevertheless matter for participant or referent tracking in less redundant environments. {++And so++}{#s53} {--D--}{#s54}{++d++}{#s55}iscourse consequence and grammatical status {~~must~>have to~~}{#s56} {--therefore--}{#s57} be identified independently.

It would also be misleading to represent all discourse consequences as movement along one scale, as though every grammatical choice simply changed more or less of a single public information store. Bary ([2025](#ref-bary2025speechacts)) argues that at least three things {~~must~>need to~~}{#s58} be separated: what interlocutors jointly treat as settled, who is publicly committed to a proposition, and what institutional or performative change an utterance itself brings about. Simons ([2025](#ref-simons2025availability)) presses the separation further. Information can be available and appropriate for interpretation without being mutually accepted as common ground, and the information used to interpret a speech act needn’t be identical to the information that the act changes.

A grammatical choice can therefore have several potentially independent discourse effects. A _discourse-consequence profile_ is the analyst’s estimate of those effects, not an additional property that constitutes grammatical status. Rather than assigning a paradigm one number, the profile records evidence bearing on:

- _Move type_: whether the utterance functions as an assertion, question, request, or some other move.

- _Commitment holder or source_: whether the speaker, another person, or no fully committed source is presented as responsible for the information.

- _Participant or referent continuity_: whether subsequent material is interpreted as continuing with the same person or shifting to another.

- _Response relevance_: which kinds of reply the utterance makes conditionally appropriate.


A paradigm may have a strong effect on one dimension and almost none on another. {==The proposal==}{>>which one?<<}{#c30} assumes neither a unitary public state nor universal weights among these dimensions.

The discourse-consequence profile applies only to a grammatical choice already identified on independent grounds. It isn’t part of the definition of grammar. Clause type, evidential anchoring, and switch-reference are plausible high-consequence examples because changing their values can alter the move, source, or participant carried forward. Ordinary allomorphy is usually a low-consequence example. Replacing _went_ with *_goed_ violates a realization relation but ordinarily leaves the intended past value and conversational move intact. Interjections make the converse point: a resource can reshape uptake without thereby forming part of grammatical assembly.

This separation permits a more specific repair prediction. Consider an independently identified switch-reference paradigm whose values distinguish continuation by the same subject from continuation by a different subject. Now compare two failures. In the first, a same-subject value occurs in a clause chain whose clauses specify different subjects. The performance supplies incompatible grammatical instructions: one part indicates continuity, while another indicates a change of participant. A hearer who detects the conflict may need to clarify who performs the second action. In the second failure, the intended different-subject value remains recoverable but its exponent has an excluded phonological realization. The participant shift is already clear, so correcting the form can leave the interpretation and discourse organization unchanged.

The prediction isn’t that every value conflict provokes clarification or that every realization failure receives an overt correction. Speakers can accommodate, ignore, mishear, or respond to some other aspect of the utterance. The prediction concerns the _locus of repair_: what a response attempts to settle. Internal value incompatibilities should more often direct repair toward force, commitment, participant role, or reference. Value-preserving realization failures should more often direct repair toward form while preserving the intended discourse effect. Across independently identified paradigms, this difference should grow with the independently estimated discourse effects. The hypothesis concerns a consequence of grammatical status: it predicts what different grammatical failures may do rather than deciding what counts as grammatical.

{==The information-theoretic intuition==}{>>what information-theoretic intuition?<<}{#c31} belongs at this same consequence level. A constrained set of alternatives creates redundancy. Suppose a paradigm contains only same-subject and different-subject values. Even when the exponent is malformed, the surrounding clause chain and the limited competitor set may make the intended value recoverable. Entropy or divergence can represent how much uncertainty about interpretation or response changes when one value is selected rather than another ([Shannon 1948](#ref-shannon1948); [Cover and Thomas 2006](#ref-coverthomas2006)).

But these measures apply only after linguistic analysis has identified the alternatives and their conventional functions. An information-theoretic calculation can quantify how strongly same-subject and different-subject values constrain a response space. It can’t discover that these are the relevant alternatives, determine whether they form a grammatical paradigm, establish that a performance fails a form–value relation, or explain why that failure is accountable within a community. The linguistic codebook is an input to the calculation, not its output.

## Inflection, complementation, and lexical knowledge
*_Goed_ conforms to the productive past-tense schema with respect to one relation: past is realized by _-ed_. A more specific, lexeme-indexed relation realizes go.past as _went_ and pre-empts the general realization for this lexeme in the scoped repertoire. The form preserves past and satisfies the productive schema while failing the more specific realization relation. The two-relation analysis is supported independently by the productive schema and the stable lexeme-specific opposition. The exponent isn’t intrinsically excluded; it’s excluded for this lexeme.

*_Depend of_ falls under the dependencies developed in Section [3.2](#sec:individuation). A complement construction introduces an argument relation; that relation constrains a phrasal category; and the particular head restricts the preposition. The more schematic dependencies independently contribute to grammatical assembly. The present evidence doesn’t settle whether the final _depend_–_on_ restriction is lexically indexed licensing or collocation. Mixed attribution is informative only when compared with those independently specified dependencies and their stated indeterminacy.

The lexicon more generally contains conventional form–meaning pairings. Choosing an ill-fitting but licensed word normally preserves both the word’s identity and the larger construction. Calling a downpour _strong rain_, for example, doesn’t by itself show that English adjective–noun modification has failed. The combination is unusual because English convention favours a different lexical collocation.

Selectional restrictions and valence frames are harder because lexical content can constrain how a construction is assembled. Abrusán’s type and shift diagnostics help determine whether a semantic conflict is doing the work; constructional recurrence and substitution show whether a conventional assembly relation is also present. This evidence bears directly on grammaticality. If a head-specific restriction helps realize an independently supported complement or valence construction, its failure is grammatical because it concerns that assembly relation. If the restriction amounts only to an association between stored lexical items, the failure is lexical. For _depend on_, the argument-role and PP-category dependencies have clearer constructional support than the final choice of _on_. On the evidence considered here, that item-specific relation remains indeterminate between lexically indexed grammatical licensing and collocation.
## Deployment, representation, and grammaticalization
Register, accent, and politeness marking, as in Japanese polite/plain-style verb suffixation, can be highly systematic and socially consequential ([Agha 2007](#ref-agha2007); [Eckert 2012](#ref-eckert2012); [Cook 2008](#ref-cook2008speechstyle); [Ide 2005](#ref-ide2005)). A mismatch may preserve the form’s conventional social value perfectly and fail only because that value is unsuitable for the occasion. In that case, assembly has succeeded and deployment is the fault. An honorific or style contrast participates in grammatical assembly when it functions within a repertoire as a recurrent constructional choice with a domain, alternatives, and conditioned selection. The account doesn’t exclude pragmatic or social content from grammar in advance.

Orthography normally maps graphic forms to linguistic expressions. Consider a writer who intends _They’re leaving now_ but writes *_Their leaving now_. The spellings _their_ and _they’re_ distinguish a possessive form from the contraction of _they are_, even though they are homophonous for many speakers. A reader may recover the intended clause immediately. On a purely representational analysis, the writer has selected the wrong graphic form for an otherwise recoverable linguistic expression; the fault is orthographic. But the spelling also makes a different syntactic analysis available: _their leaving_ can {==begin a possessive gerund phrase==}{>>make this CGEL compliant<<}{#c32}, so the written token may contain a secondary constructional conflict. {++As a result,++}{#s71} {--T--}{#s72}{++t++}{#s73}he analysis should {--therefore--}{#s74} identify the graphic mapping and the clause structure separately rather than infer one fault type from the fact that the sentence is written. More generally, a graphic contrast is grammatical when it realizes a constructional value, but merely orthographic when it represents an expression already identified. The medium doesn’t determine the norm type; the relation’s contribution to expression formation does.

These possibilities are also diachronic. Grammaticalization can recruit lexical or pragmatic material into constructional and paradigmatic organization ([Hopper and Traugott 2003](#ref-hoppertraugott2003); [Lehmann 2015](#ref-lehmann2015grammaticalization); [Waltereit 2011](#ref-waltereit2011discourse); [Degand and Evers-Vermeul 2015](#ref-degandeversvermeul2015); [Jakobsen and Heltoft 2012](#ref-jakobsenheltoft2012paradigm)). Historical origin doesn’t fix present status. The relevant question is how the relation functions in the scoped repertoire now.
## A discourse-consequence application
Amele switch-reference illustrates the narrower distinction between an internal value conflict and a value-preserving realization failure. Roberts ([1987, 239–40, 298–300, 371](#ref-roberts1987)) describes a sequential paradigm contrasting same-subject (SS) and different-subject (DS) linkage on medial verbs, with person–number marking and vowel harmony in the DS form. Example [[ex:amele-ds]](#ex:amele-ds) is Roberts’s attested different-subject clause chain.

_Hina_ _ho-co-m_ _sab_ _je-i-a._
2sg come-ds-2sg food eat-3sg-tod.pst
‘You came and he ate the food.’

The medial verb _ho-co-m_ marks the subject of “come” as second-person singular and signals that the next clause has a different subject. The final verb _je-i-a_ supplies the third-person subject and today’s-past tense. Thus the DS form and the person marking converge on a change of participant; the translation alone isn’t being used to infer the paradigm. Table [7](#tab:amele-sr) starts from this attested conforming form and gives two deliberately constructed candidates that would have to be validated before use in a study.

| **Condition** | **Amele form** | **Analysis** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Attested conforming form | _Hina ho-co-m sab je-i-a_ | ‘You came and he ate the food’ (DS) |
| Constructed internal-conflict candidate | _Hina h-u-me-g sab je-i-a_ | licensed 2sg SS form conflicts with the distinct subject supplied by the following clause |
| Constructed realization-failure candidate | _Hina ho-ce-m sab je-i-a_ | DS and 2sg material are retained, but the DS vowel is disharmonic after _ho-_ |

A schematic Amele switch-reference comparison. The two candidate sequences are constructed from Roberts’s paradigm and haven’t been validated as judgments in the intended clause-chain context. {#tab:amele-sr}

The internal-conflict candidate constitutes a grammatical failure only if the subject information is supplied by the clause chain and the SS value conflicts with it. A speaker who merely intends a different continuation after producing a well-formed SS chain has made a message-selection error instead. The realization condition preserves DS but violates the harmony relation described for its form.

Because SS and DS choice is sensitive to agentivity, clause level, time, place, world, and pragmatic deixis, those factors can’t be inferred from subject identity alone. The published grammar also doesn’t print the exact disharmonic stimulus. The comparison is a source-grounded template that requires validation, not an empirical result.

If the internal conflict is established, the two failures yield different discourse predictions. An SS/DS conflict can unsettle reference continuity; a disharmonic realization need not. Whether participants direct repair to reference or form remains a separate causal and empirical question. The ontology permits this cross-level inference; it isn’t evidence used to define the switch-reference relation as grammatical.
# Methodological consequences and failure conditions
Many studies begin with a judgment (a low rating, an asterisk, or a correction) and then ask what caused it. That evidence is useful, but by itself it bundles three of the questions distinguished above. The response has a target, such as article formation or pronunciation; it has causes, such as linguistic knowledge, processing, schooling, or stigma; and it gives the analyst fallible evidence about the norm and the token. If an analyst uses the same response both to infer the norm and to confirm that speakers identify it, the argument is circular. The account requires a different evidential order.

A worked investigation would proceed in six steps. First, declare the relevant community, activity, medium, and period. That scope can be refined from outcome-blind evidence about interaction, exposure, production, and interpretation.

Second, identify the proposed relation. State the signs, exponents, values, and constructional site, using substitution, omission, productivity, contrast organization, and distribution rather than grammar labels.

Third, state the closest alternative analysis. For French elision, the relevant rivals are a general phonotactic relation and a graphic convention. The analysis has to say what outcome-blind evidence would favour each account.

Fourth, freeze the analysis before examining the target judgments or repairs. This includes the repertoire, the candidate relation, the uncertainty in its assembly profile, and the conditions under which it would fail.

Fifth, collect response evidence from new tokens or a disjoint corpus partition. Open descriptions and spontaneous corrections should precede forced choices among “grammar”, “wording”, “style”, and related labels.

Finally, explain the responses. Code what each response targets separately from what probably caused it. Schoolroom terminology, processing difficulty, stigma, and task demands can shape a response without determining the token’s grammatical status.

The order doesn’t make judgments irrelevant. It makes clear what they can show. A spontaneous replacement of _le_ with _l’_ may indicate that a respondent has located the trouble in article realization. An explanation such as “two vowels can’t occur together” may instead target phonology. Neither response, by itself, decides which worldly relation is present. That question was addressed in the outcome-blind stages; the response provides evidence about whether the respondent identifies the proposed relation and how they describe it. A construction-directed response isn’t automatically grammar-directed because lexical constructions are part of the distinction at issue. Mixed and unresolved responses therefore remain visible.

The comparison set has to challenge the constitutive distinction rather than merely contrast easy grammatical examples with unrelated controls. It should include strict paradigms, productive constructions, value-preserving realization failures, item-specific licensing dependencies, pure phonotactic and orthographic departures, poor but licensed lexical choices, social-indexical mismatches, and violations of social acts. The items should remain interpretable, since the question is why recoverable performances receive different fault classifications. If the assembly analysis is adjusted after the responses are known, the test has collapsed back into an account of labeling.

Rival explanations operate at different levels. Semantic repairability may explain classifications where composition fails. Boye and Harder’s discursive secondary status may better identify grammatical norms. Frequency, surprisal, perceptual salience, and processing can cause low ratings or rapid correction. Schooling, stigma, participant role, and institutional authority can cause grammar-directed labels or person-directed sanction. Activity, medium, register, community, and period define the population of the claim rather than serving as a bag of statistical controls. A good design restricts or matches these causes where possible and states which remaining comparison is identified.

Three empirical consequences then become available. First, relations independently identified as grammatical assembly norms should more consistently prompt speakers to identify expression formation as the problem than matched lexical, phonotactic, representational, deployment, and social-act faults. Explicit grammar labels should correlate with that more specific fault attribution, but the correlation is a further response-level prediction and may be weakened by schooling or local metalinguistic practice.

Second, performances with independently supported relations of different types, and relations changing over time, should produce different or changing classifications among grammar, lexicon, phonology, and style. Third, among paradigms that independently support both error types, a stronger discourse-consequence profile should increase the difference in discourse-directed repair between internal value conflicts and value-preserving realization failures.

These predictions are evidential consequences, not definitions. The constitutive account fails if its functional criteria can’t distinguish phonotactics, lexical identity, representation, deployment, and mixed constructional cases without relying on the responses to be explained. Its empirical reach fails if independently estimated assembly organization adds no stable held-out expectation about fault attribution or classification change beyond semantic repairability, discursive secondary status, processing, schooling, and sanction. The discourse application fails if the independently estimated consequence profile doesn’t predict the repair-locus difference in an identifying comparison.

Some outcomes are inconclusive rather than favourable or fatal. A corpus of corrections contains only occasions on which someone corrected, so it can’t by itself estimate how a community classifies all comparable tokens. Naturally occurring internal value conflicts and realization failures may also be too sparse or uneven for the repair comparison. Such data can test search procedures, coding, and candidate analyses without being promoted into a confirmatory result. The account earns support only from comparisons whose norm evidence and outcome evidence are separable in practice.
# Conclusion
Why does one interpretable deviation count as ungrammatical while another does not? {~~The paper’s~>My~~}{#s75} answer can now be stated in the order of its six questions.

First, coordinated linguistic practice supplies a thin standard of correctness within a scoped repertoire. This answers the question of _norm constitution_. The standard is grammatical when it relates independently supported signs, exponents, positions, or values in the recurrent assembly of constructions. Lexical conventions, phonotactics, orthography, and usage norms are equally conventional, but they ordinarily establish sign identity, constrain form shape, represent an expression, or govern its deployment.

Second, _token application_ asks whether a performance conforms to the assembly standard. A performance can be defective with respect to one relation while remaining recognizable as an attempted instance because other relations are preserved. *_Le hiver_, for example, preserves definiteness, number, gender, and reference while failing the proposed article-realization relation. Recoverability doesn’t erase the fault, and failure of one relation doesn’t prevent the performance from being identified through the relations it preserves.

Third, the _semantic or pragmatic consequence_ of a grammatical fault requires its own explanation. A failure may block composition, invite a semantic repair, leave the proposition intact, or unsettle move type, commitment, participant continuity, or response relevance. Abrusán’s account explains an important subset of anomaly classifications, and discourse theory explains why some grammatical values reorganize interaction. Neither type of explanation alone determines whether the failed relation belongs to grammar.

Fourth, _target attribution_ concerns whether a respondent treats the trouble as article formation, pronunciation, spelling, wording, style, or conduct. That attribution may be mistaken.

Fifth, _response causation_ asks why the respondent reacted. Processing difficulty, frequency, perceptual salience, schooling, stigma, participant role, and institutional authority can produce rejection or correction without determining grammatical status. They can also prevent a speaker from noticing a genuine grammatical fault.

Finally, _epistemic justification_ requires the proposed relation, its repertoire, and its closest rival to be individuated from outcome-blind production, interpretation, substitution, omission, and distribution before held-out judgments and repairs test whether speakers track it.

Norm types aren’t assigned by expressive substance: phonological material can realize grammar, pragmatic values can enter paradigms, and item-specific knowledge can participate in constructional dependencies. Multiple-relation analysis is justified only when each proposed relation recurs or contrasts independently and predicts further cases; otherwise, the unit of analysis remains indeterminate.

The motivating French case has a deliberately conditional purpose. *_Le hiver_ counts as a grammatical-form error only if the conditioned realization of the article, rather than pure phonotactics or graphic convention, is what fails in the scoped repertoire. The theory can be wrong about that case without redefining grammar to save it. More generally, if the assembly criterion can’t distinguish the difficult cases without recycling their labels, the constitutive answer fails and the narrower theory of metalinguistic attribution is all that remains.
