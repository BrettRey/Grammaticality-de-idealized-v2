#### 1. INTRODUCTION

Linguists have learned a great deal about language by studying when things go wrong, for example, when an utterance is “weird.” A major goal of modern syntax has been to find principles that rule out sentences that seem ungrammatical. To achieve this goal, it has been proposed that there are different types of weirdness; in particular, syntactically odd (or ungrammatical) sentences can be distinguished empirically and theoretically from semantically odd (or semantically anomalous) sentences:1 (1a) ∗Read you a book about modern syntax?

(Chomsky 1957) Initial psycholinguistic evidence seemed to confirm this sharp division. Kutas & Hilyard (1980, 1984) discovered that reading a semantically anomalous word in a sentence (e.g., #John buttered his bread with socks) produces what came to be known as the N400 effect: a strong negative eventrelated potential (ERP) 400 ms after the presentation of the anomalous word.2 Simple grammatical errors—for example, agreement violations—did not produce the N400 effect (see Kutas & Hillyard 1983). In contrast, syntactic or morphological anomalies (e.g., *The cat will eating the food) were found to elicit a large positive wave peaking 600 ms after the presentation of the anomalous word (the P600 effect) as well as a left anterior negativity (LAN) (see Osterhout & Holcomb 1992, 1993; Osterhout & Mobley 1995; Osterhout et al. 1996).

However, sometimes it is not clear why a sentence is weird, and this has repercussions for syntactic and semantic theories. Various authors have proposed that semantic and pragmatic processes can lead to weirdness that empirically feels more like ungrammaticality than semantic oddness. For example, the unacceptability of negative polarity items in unsuitable contexts, as in example 2, has been argued to follow from semantic or pragmatic processes. But empirically the example feels ungrammatical rather than semantically anomalous: (2) ∗Anyone stole John’s sandwich yesterday.

As more and more cases of seemingly ungrammatical sentences started to receive semantic explanations, the dichotomy of syntactic versus semantic oddness became blurred. Closer scrutiny revealed that processing data do not support a one-to-one mapping between N400 and semantic processing, or between P600 and syntactic processing (see, e.g., Coulson et al. 1998, Kutas & Federmeier 2011, Osterhout et al. 2012). Rather, it is currently thought that N400 is involved in lexical access (lookup) and can be influenced by both semantic and syntactic factors. P600 seems to be present when there is a deviation from some expected pattern or sequence. Neither effect is language specific: Stimuli in the visual or musical domain can elicit N400 or P600 as well.

If interpretive processes can lead to what feels like ungrammaticality, then a question arises: What explains the intuitive difference between sentences that feel ungrammatical and those that merely feel semantically anomalous or pragmatically infelicitous in a given context? This question is rarely raised in the literature3 and even more rarely answered.

In this article, I describe and compare various semantic and pragmatic proposals that were put forth in order to explain the reasons for the unacceptability of ungrammatical sentences. The first type of proposal assumes that ungrammaticality can follow from a failure to satisfy semantic conditions. The second type of proposal derives ungrammaticality from systematic pragmatic failure. The third type of proposal takes logical triviality to be responsible for various cases of ungrammaticality. I also discuss, where relevant, how these explanations differ (or not) from explanations for semantic anomaly and different types of pragmatic infelicity. I present a number of different linguistic examples, but the running theme of this article involves polarity items: Several competing semantic and pragmatic analyses have been put forward to explain their distribution, so they can serve as a test case for the comparison of different types of analyses. In the last section of the review, I outline a recent proposal that aims to explain what leads to the difference of intuitions between cases of semantic anomaly and ungrammaticality.

#### 2. SEMANTIC CONDITIONS FOR GRAMMATICALITY

#### 2.1. Lexical Semantic Conditions

Perhaps the best-known examples that argue that semantic conditions can underlie ungrammaticality judgments are polarity items. Consider so-called negative polarity items (NPIs), underlined in the sentences below: (3a) John didn’t see anything.

(3b) ∗John saw anything.

(4a) Few people lifted a finger to help.

(4b) ∗Someone lifted a finger to help.

(5b) ∗Three students who budged an inch were rewarded.

NPIs such as anything, lift a finger, or budge an inch have a restricted distribution: They are acceptable in certain types of sentences but not in others. In example 3a, the element that licenses the NPI is negation, in example 4a it is the quantifier few, and in example 5a it is the restriction of the quantifier every. The most influential analysis of NPIs was presented by Ladusaw (1979), who (building on Fauconnier 1975) argued that the environments that allow NPIs to occur should be characterized in semantic terms: He proposed that NPIs are licensed in downward-entailing (DE) contexts. The property of downward entailingness does not delineate a particular syntactic environment (contra Klima 1964, Linebarger 1980, Progovac 2005); it is a potential semantic characteristic of the sentences in which NPIs occur, which is also illustrated by the fact that NPIs are acceptable in the complement of negative-implying verbs such as lack, as in He lacks any sense of humor. On Ladusaw’s view, the requirement that NPIs should occur in DE environments is formulated as a special semantic licensing condition that must be met in order to use these expressions in a grammatical way. This means that syntax and semantics must conspire in order to produce a grammatical output; grammaticality results from syntactic and semantic well-formedness (see also Ladusaw 1983). Thus, if a correct syntactic structure is not semantically well formed, then such a structure can be filtered out by the semantics. The result of this semantic filtering, in the case of NPIs, is a sentence that feels ungrammatical, not only semantically anomalous in the sense of example 1b.

Much has been written on polarity items since the seminal work of Ladusaw; both the empirical generalizations and the accuracy of downward entailingness as a licensing condition have been challenged (see Chierchia 2013, Giannakidou 1998, Kadmon & Landman 1993, Krifka 1995, Linebarger 1980, Progovac 2005, von Fintel 1999, Zwarts 1998 for some influential proposals). But much research on polarity items has preserved the idea of explaining their distribution in terms of a semantic licensing condition. Examples other than downward entailingness include Strawson DE (von Fintel 1999), intensionality, nonveridicality (Giannakidou 1998, Zwarts 1998), and most recently scope licensing (Barker 2018). The licensing condition might be expressed as a filtering condition on the whole structure in which polarity items appear (e.g., Barker 2018, Ladusaw 1979) or as a rule of lexical semantics (e.g., Giannakidou 1998, von Fintel 1999, Zwarts 1998). Either way of thinking entails that the unacceptability of examples 3b, 4b, and 5b is the result of a lexical semantic condition gone wrong.

#### 2.2. Failures of Semantic Composition

In the case of NPI licensing, one can identify a single lexical item (e.g., anyone) that requires its environment to meet special semantic conditions. When these conditions are not met, composition fails. In other cases there is no single culprit; a general failure of semantic composition can lead to ungrammaticality. An example is the analysis of so-called weak islands by Szabolcsi & Zwarts (1993). Wh-movement was considered a classical example of a syntactic process, but it shows certain restrictions that appear to be semantic in nature. For example, negation and some other negative elements block the formation of degree questions: (7) ∗How fast didn’t John swim?

But negation is not an absolute block for wh-movement. For example, questions about individuals are not sensitive to negation: (8) Which painting did John not see?

#### 2.3. Comparison with Semantic Anomaly

Szabolcsi & Zwarts (1993) explicitly liken the deviance of weak islands to cases such as example 10: (10) #six mists They argue that in this example, as in the case of example 7, we observe failure of semantic composition. In example 10, we cannot apply a numeral to a mass noun; counting is not defined in the mass domain. However, there is something to explain about the difference in intuition with respect to these two examples.4 More generally, there is an open question concerning why semantic failure such as that observed with polarity items or intervention gives rise to a different intuition than semantic failure that results from composing a predicate with an unsuitable argument: (11) #My toothbrush is pregnant.

Intuitively, the reason why example 11 is unacceptable is because a toothbrush is not the sort of object to which the predicate pregnant can apply. In the linguistic and philosophical literature, many competing ways to capture this intuition formally have been proposed (see Magidor 2013 for a recent overview). The various types of proposals make different predictions about the unacceptability of ungrammatical versus semantically anomalous sentences. Let us briefly review three major types of proposals for semantic anomaly.5 According to one popular view, in sentences such as example 11 semantic composition fails because the argument is not in the domain of the function denoted by the predicate, and thus the result is uninterpretable (or “meaningless”) (see, e.g., Beall & Fraassen 2003, Chomsky 1957, Drange 1966, Russell 1908, Strawson 1952). This view does not predict a difference between cases of semantic anomaly and the ungrammatical examples discussed above.

According to another popular approach to semantic anomaly, examples of semantic anomaly have meanings that speakers can intuitively grasp (and hence are not meaningless), but they cannot be used in a truthful way in any situation. Semantic composition can succeed, but the selectional restrictions of predicates (modeled as preconditions for having a truth value) are not met (see, e.g., Asher 2011, Goddard 1966, Halld´en 1949, Lappin 1981, Shaw 2015, Thomason 1972). Such selectional restrictions might be expressed as type presuppositions that guide semantic composition (see Asher 2011). Technically, this approach does predict a difference between the ungrammatical sentences discussed above and semantic anomaly because in the latter case the semantic failure is at the level of presuppositions. However, precisely because the difference between semantic conditions and semantic presuppositions is merely technical (and semantic conditions are often formulated technically as semantic presuppositions), we still need an explanation of the difference.

#### 3. SYSTEMATIC PRAGMATIC INFELICITY

The pragmatic context, the norms of assertion, and the assumptions of the conversational partners all influence and modulate what sentences are taken to mean in context. Traditionally, pragmatics was assigned the role of a conversational “postprocessor,” but increasingly, pragmatic processes are thought to interact with other components of the language system (syntax, semantics, prosody, and phonology) in a fundamental way. Most interesting for the purposes of this review is the proposal that systematic failure to observe general conversational principles can also lead to ungrammaticality.

#### 3.2. Systematic Versus Occasional Pragmatic Infelicity

A question that is often raised about the idea that systematic pragmatic infelicity can lead to ungrammaticality is the following: What distinguishes ungrammatical cases from better-known and more innocent pragmatic violations such as implicature cancellation or presupposition failure? For example, uttering the sentence John stopped paying taxes in a context in which it is known that John has never paid taxes forces the hearer to accommodate a presupposition that clashes with what is known. Similarly, the ignorance implicature of the sentence John is at home or at the bar will clash with the context if it is known that John is at the bar. The result in such cases might be a communicative breakdown, a cancellation of the relevant presupposition/implicature, or an adjustment of the context, but it does not seem to involve ungrammaticality.7 It is for this reason that I have emphasized the word systematic above. In simple cases of presupposition failure or violations of Gricean maxims it is not possible to accomplish a successful speech act, because what is presupposed or implicated clashes with the particular context in which the utterances are made. Under different circumstances, the relevant sentences could have been felicitously uttered. These cases are similar to Grice’s “particularized conversational implicatures.” In contrast, the pragmatic infelicity of the type proposed by Krifka and others does not depend on the particular context chosen; any context will lead to an infelicitous assertion. Thus, these cases represent a special type of pragmatic infelicity, similar to Grice’s “generalized conversational implicatures” (Oshima 2006 refers to such cases as “pragmatic anomaly”).8 The same point holds for the analyses cited above, where unacceptability is based on systematic presupposition failure that does not depend on the discourse context.

One might wonder what explains the difference in intuition between the ungrammatical examples referred to in this section and semantic anomaly understood as a failure of pragmatic presuppositions. A possible answer is that examples of semantic anomaly, as discussed in the last section of this review, show a high degree of context sensitivity, unlike the cases presented above.

#### 4. UNGRAMMATICALITY FROM TRIVIALITY: A CASE FOR NATURAL LOGIC?

Another line of research has proposed that triviality of meaning can also lead to ungrammaticality. A well-known case concerns the acceptability of quantifiers in there-existential sentences. Milsark (1977) observes that certain quantifiers, which he calls “strong quantifiers,” such as every, both, and neither, are unacceptable in this construction, as shown in example 16a. In contrast, weak quantifiers such as some, few, and three, are acceptable:9 (16a) ∗There is every fly in my soup.

(16b) There are some flies in my soup.

There is no obvious syntactic difference between the two examples above, and indeed most explanations of this contrast reduce it to some difference in the semantic and pragmatic meaning of the quantifiers involved (see, e.g., Barwise & Cooper 1981, Comorovski 1995, Keenan 2003, McNally 1998, Milsark 1977, Zucchi 1995).

According to a prominent early explanation of the contrast (Barwise & Cooper 1981), a strong quantifier in a there-existential construction is unacceptable because the meaning of such sentences is trivial: They are true (or false) independently of the properties of the context. In contrast, a there-existential sentence with a weak quantifier has a meaning that is contingent on the properties of the context. This conclusion follows from the theory of generalized quantifiers and the crucial assumption that the expletive there denotes the domain of individuals De. If we grant this assumption, then example 16a can be paraphrased as ‘The set of flies in my soup is a subset of De,’ which cannot be false, since the empty set is also a subset of De. In contrast, the meaning of sentence 16b can be paraphrased as ‘The intersection of the set of flies in my soup and the domain De is not empty,’ which can be false if there are no flies in my soup.

This proposal, although controversial, has been quite influential in the literature. Similar explanations have been given for a number of phenomena, such as the partitive constraint (Barwise & Cooper 1981, Ladusaw 1982), the but-exceptive construction (von Fintel 1993), restrictions on comparatives (Gajewski 2008), polarity items (Chierchia 2013), free-choice items (Men´endezBenito 2005), and various island constraints and intervention effects (Abrus´an 2007a, 2014; Fox & Hackl 2007; Mayr 2013). Most recently, the idea has been used to explain restrictions on complement selection (Theiler et al. 2017, Uegaki & Sudo 2017).

#### 4.1. L-Triviality

In an unpublished but widely cited paper, Gajewski (2002) proposed that there is a formally specifiable subset of trivial sentences, called “L-trivial,” whose members are systematically unacceptable.11 On the standard, Tarskian conception, logical truth and consequence are defined in terms of variation of truth across all interpretations of the logical form (LF). The LF of sentences, as usually understood, is a level of semantic representation that preserves all semantically significant features of the sentence (e.g., its lexical content) but is free of imperfections such as ambiguity. L-triviality, in contrast, is calculated on modified LFs in which all lexical material has been “bleached,” that is, replaced nonuniformly by a fresh variable of the appropriate type. Gajewski (2002) calls these impoverished logical forms “logical skeletons” (LS):12 Definition. LS are obtained from the LF α as follows: (a) Identify the maximal constituents of α containing no logical items, and (b) replace each such constituent with a distinct variable of the same type.

In the resulting picture, grammar itself is endowed with the capacity to calculate L-triviality. This means that the grammar of natural languages has to include (or at least interact with) a system of “natural logic,” or a “natural deductive system” (see Chierchia 2013, Del Pinal 2017, Fox & Hackl 2007). By assumption, this deductive system is blind to conceptual information and cannot “see” nonlogical terms; it operates only on the basis of functional terms. This in turn presupposes that terms can be sorted into two nonoverlapping classes, lexical terms and functional (or logical) terms.

#### 4.2. Application to Chierchia’s Analysis of Polarity Items

Chierchia’s (2005, 2013) analysis of polarity items is based on Krifka’s (1990, 1995) approach, but with some important differences. Chierchia adopts the idea that NPIs contribute a property whose alternatives are subproperties. He also adopts the idea that there is a communicative pressure to make the strongest claim possible with scalar items. However, instead of the SCALAR.ASSERT operator, this is achieved by a silent exhaustivity operator (O) whose meaning is roughly equivalent to only. Similarly to SCALAR.ASSERT, the presence of an exhaustivity operator is triggered by the presence of grammatically encoded alternatives. In contrast to Krifka’s approach, in Chierchia’s analysis exhaustification occurs at the level of semantics, and is not tied to making a speech act.

Thus, Chierchia assumes that any associates with focus, causing it to trigger alternatives. Any also has a lexical requirement that its active alternatives must range over subdomains D′ of the domain D that any associates with. Any furthermore requires that these alternatives be exhaustified by an exhaustive operator O: The operator O negates every alternative in the domain that is not entailed by the original sentence. In the case of a sentence such as Anything is blue, its LF, truthconditional import, and focus alternatives are as follows: (21a) LF of Anything is blue: O[anyF,D thing is blue] (21b) the truth-conditional import of 21a: ∃x∈D[thing(x)∧blue(x)] (21c) the alternatives of 21a: {∃x∈D′ [thing(x)∧blue(x)] | D′⊆D} Just as in Krifka’s analysis, each alternative in example 21c entails assertion 21b. As the alternatives in example 21c are not entailed by assertion 21b, exhaustifying the assertion via the O operator will negate them. This leads to a contradiction because the resulting statement implies that there there is some thing that is blue (i.e., the meaning of example 21b) but there is no particular thing such that is blue (i.e., the negation of each alternative in 21c).

The interpretation of the LF in example 21a is thus a contradiction, but is it also L-trivial in Gajewski’s sense? Only with a number of auxiliary assumptions. First, any and the silent operator O need to be part of the logical vocabulary. This is a stipulation, but not problematic in itself. However, Chierchia’s derivation is encoded by a set of lexical restrictions introduced by any. These restrictions, namely that any associates with focus, the requirement that its active alternatives must range over subdomains of the domain D that any associates with, and the requirement that the alternatives have to be exhaustified by the operator O, are presumably encoded as presuppositions of any. #### 4.3. Some Problems

Gajewski’s (2002) proposal relies on two fundamental assumptions that are often viewed as problematic. The first is that lexical items can be sorted into logical and nonlogical vocabulary. The second is that grammar is insulated from conceptual knowledge in a very radical manner. I briefly discuss these two issues below.

4.3.1. Defining logical words. Gajewski’s (2002) proposal hinges on distinguishing two types of vocabulary, logical and nonlogical. Despite intuitions about the existence of the two classes of words, finding a precise semantic difference has proven difficult. The most frequently cited idea, borrowed from a tradition in logic aimed at defining logical constants, is that function words have meanings that are invariant across certain algebraic transformations of their domains.13 Examples of transformations that have been proposed to diagnose logical constants include invariance under permutations (Sher 1991, Tarski & Givant 1987, van Benthem 1989), invariance under surjective functions (Feferman 1999), invariance under potential isomorphisms (Bonnay 2008), and so forth. The underlying idea is that logical meanings are topic independent: The validity of a logical inference should not depend on the particular properties of what we are talking about. The appropriateness of the above ideas for diagnosing logical constants is a subject of lively debate, but they are clearly unsuitable for diagnosing function (logical) words of natural language (see Gajewski 2002, van Benthem 2002). This is because they predict certain lexical items to be logical (e.g., the predicates self-identical, exist), and they also predict that certain intuitively logical elements of natural language; for instance, the quantifiers every and each are not logical, because they have a lexical restriction that they need to quantify over countable objects (hence *Every/*Each milk is in the fridge).

It seems that there is no foolproof method that can distinguish logical words from nonlogical ones that also makes the cut in a linguistically intuitive way. The difficulties mentioned above suggest that the logical/grammatical aspects and the conceptual aspects of meaning do not map neatly onto two different classes of words. Instead, both functional and lexical words have logical and conceptual aspects of meaning, packaged together (see also Abrus´an et al. 2018).

An alternative possibility, one that Gajewski (2002) also considers, is to replace the logical/ nonlogical distinction with the functional/lexical distinction familiar from the linguistic literature (see, e.g., Abney 1987, von Fintel 1995). This, too, suffers from difficulties, as some words, such as prepositions and the word there, cannot be clearly assigned to either category.

4.3.2. An exotic deductive system. Gajewski’s (2002) proposal has profound implications for how we should think about the language system and its interaction with other cognitive systems in general. If L-triviality can have implications for grammaticality, grammar needs to interact with a natural deductive system (see also Chierchia 2019, Fox & Hackl 2007). The idea that grammar might be related to some system of natural logic is not in itself new; it has been discussed a great deal in the context of the semantics of quantifiers, monotonicity reasoning, polarity items, and so forth (see, e.g., Dowty 1994; Moss 2015; van Benthem 1986, 2008). What is new is that L-triviality suggests a radical form of modularity of language: Grammar is insulated not only from conceptual systems, general world knowledge, but also from most of the information encoded in lexical items. At the same time, grammar needs to have privileged access to some aspects of the meaning of lexical items, namely their types and (other) presuppositions that they induce.

#### 5. FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Where do we stand now? This review has presented several different types of semantic and pragmatic analyses of ungrammatical sentences and has shown how systematic pragmatic infelicity (or pragmatic anomaly) differs from occasional pragmatic infelicity. However, no clear answer has emerged as to what distinguishes sentences that are felt merely to be semantically anomalous from other cases of semantically or pragmatically deviant sentences that are felt to be ungrammatical.

Here, I outline a recent proposal by Abrus´an et al. (2019) that argues that the underlying cause of both semantic anomaly (e.g., #My toothbrush is pregnant) and ungrammaticality is failure to observe semantic compositional rules (including semantic presuppositions).14 According to this proposal, the difference arises because cases of semantic anomaly, in contrast to ungrammatical examples, could be repaired by meaning shift, however far-fetched a scenario that would require.15 This approach, in a sense, asks the reverse of the question this review has asked so far: Why don’t the examples of semantic anomaly feel ungrammatical? In the case of example 1b, semantic composition fails because the meaning and the selectional restrictions of the predicates give us a meaning that is nonsensical. But in contrast to many ungrammatical examples, we know exactly where the locus of inconsistency lies, and we also have the ability to shift the meaning of the predicates in an interpretive context in order to try to resolve this inconsistency (see https://linguistlist.org/issues/2/2-457.html#2 for the results of a 1985 Stanford competition to find contexts in which example 1b could be meaningfully uttered16). Contextual shifting of the meaning of words has been a major topic in the philosophy of language.17 Some of the most extreme cases of meaning shift are demonstrated by metaphors (example 25a) and metonymy (example 25b) (e.g., coercion, or a reinterpretation process triggered by a mismatch between the selectional restrictions of a function and its argument): (25a) Language is the breath of God, the dew on a fresh apple, it’s the soft rain of dust that falls into a shaft of morning sun when you pull from an old bookshelf a half-forgotten volume of erotic memoirs. It has been observed that semantic anomaly is a graded phenomenon (see Drange 1966, Lappin 1981, Magidor 2013). For example, it becomes increasingly difficult to interpret the following examples: (27a) #Tigers are human.

(27c) #Tigers are Zermelo-Fraenkel sets.

One way to think about this gradience is in terms of distance within a semantic space. Intuitively, tigers and humans are fairly close in meaning in that they share a number of features (e.g., both are animate). In contrast, tigers and Zermelo–Fraenkel sets do not have much in common. Let us assume that selectional restrictions of predicates and other lexical conditions are modeled formally as type presuppositions in a richly typed system, as in type composition logic (TCL) (see Asher 2011). Types are organized into a type hierarchy that represents the conceptual organization of semantic categories and allows for a calculation of semantic distances. Applying meaning shift to examples 27a–c means that we can try to relax the type presuppositions in question. Depending on the semantic distance between the actual and the target types, type shift allows us to make sense of examples of semantic anomaly to a greater or lesser degree. The higher the type that is involved in the type clash, the more difficult it is to shift the meaning of predicates to resolve type conflict. Nevertheless, even in sentences such as example 27c we can glean what could have been said. The contradiction between type presuppositions in these examples is somehow localized. And by relaxing those type presuppositions (e.g., by supposing that the predicate in example 27c simply is seeking an entity of general type E), the composition could actually have succeeded.

The problems of composition in ungrammatical sentences are worse in that no shifting of the type presuppositions seems possible. These types are somehow constitutive of the construction in a way that simpler type presuppositions of open-class expressions, nouns, verbs, and adjectives are not. The hypothesis put forward by Abrus´an et al. (2019) is that the type presuppositions that are violated in ungrammatical sentences are constitutive of the type of denotation at the highest level of the type hierarchy. For instance, it is something about the nature of existential predication that leads to the uninterpretability of there is every girl; it is something about the semantic type of question that leads to the uninterpretability of weak island sentences; and so on (I return to these ideas below).

#### 5.1. Meaning Shifts in Distributional Semantics

Abrus´an et al. (2019) assume that meaning shift can be diagnosed and calculated precisely with the help of a computational method called distributional semantics (DS). A simple way to think about word meaning in this framework is to assume that it is a vector in space V whose dimensions are contextual features.18 This computational method can find low-level types and calculate distances in semantic space as well as meaning shift involved in semantic composition (see Asher et al. 2016). It predicts, for instance, shifts that occur with the various occurrences of heavy in examples 26a–d or meaning shifts enforced by the context, as in the shift from Julie enjoyed the monologue to Julie enjoyed listening to the monologue. It does not, however, predict shifts with high-level types (e.g., the types for questions or quantifiers) for at least two reasons. First, these types denote contextinvariant logical meaning that is simply invisible to distributional methods. Second, the conflicting type clash is attached to a type so high in the type hierarchy that it has no neighbors that share the same syntactic/semantic dependencies, so there is nowhere in the space for its meaning to shift. Thus, types that are at a maximally general level, such as questions, whose type is a family or set of propositions, propositions themselves, the general type E of entities, the general type of determiner phrases DP, quantifiers or second-order properties, and so forth, do not have any neighbors in a vector space in the way that, say, a common noun such as tiger or idea does. This is because other expressions that are not of, say, DP type belong to a different syntactic category with different syntactic/semantic dependencies and occupy a different space (although they may share the same latent dimensions as particular DPs). As a result, there is nowhere in the space for such a type to shift and preserve its corresponding syntactic category. DS methods cannot shift these types in the way that they can shift the meaning of lexical words in context. Furthermore, because these types have no neighbors in the vector space, if indeed they inhabit a vector space at all, these semantic principles will not show up in particular latent dimensions of the vector space. DS methods, at least those of the sort employed by Asher et al. (2016) and Abrus´an et al. (2019), cannot see these principles. For expressions that have type presuppositions at this level, it follows that they cannot be shiftable.19 In contrast, one can imagine another kind of shift—one that rescues some sort of content from a predication in which there is an irresolvable type clash between a predicate and its argument, as in examples 1b and 27a–c. If we simply move to the supertype of the type presuppositions, we see that the author of example 1b or of examples 27a–c was predicating a property of some object; she was simply confused about or willfully misusing the meaning of the property or the object expression. The distance between the type presuppositions of the predicate and arguments may be significant, but it is still defined, as both tiger and, say, ordinal are in the same vector space DS defines for common nouns. #### 5.3. Meaning Shift Versus Triviality

Triviality of meaning, by contrast, does not play any role in this explanation: Trivial sentences do not show a compositionality problem and thus are predicted to be acceptable. (Meaning shift might still apply to them in order to increase their informativity, as in the case of example 17a.) Nor is there any reason to assume a sharp conceptual division between lexical and logical vocabulary. The conceptual content of lexical as well as logical words might have both logical aspects (their model-theoretic meaning) and lexical (“worldly”) aspects. For example, the conceptual content of a universal quantifier such as every might be composed from its logical content (i.e., universal quantification) and more lexical content (e.g., its lexical restriction to countable predicates). We cannot neatly separate grammar and conceptual knowledge, because they are packaged together within lexical entries. However, we can distinguish conceptual content that is contextually invariant from shiftable conceptual content (see Abrus´an et al. 2018). Conceptual content that supports logically valid inferences for first-order definable quantifiers (whose conceptual content in the form of proof rules can determine all logical consequences of such quantifiers) should always be contextually invariant, since a particular context should not render logically valid inferences incorrect. We would never expect meaning components that lead to logically valid inferences to shift in context. Other types of conceptual content of logical words, however, can be shiftable; that is, context might affect the conceptual content and the extension of a predicate or its argument as well.

#### 6. CONCLUSION

We find out how things work when they are broken. In linguistics, it has often been assumed to be intuitively clear to which domain the source of the problem of an unacceptable sentence can be attributed: phonology, syntax, semantics, processing, and so forth. However, the history of the field shows that problems that at first appear to belong to one domain (e.g., syntax) may later be shown to have a semantic or pragmatic (or processing) motivation. One example, discussed in this review, is the case of NPIs.

Once we accept that different types of “weirdness” can have similar interpretive sources, a new question arises: What leads to a difference in intuition if the source of the problem is the same? In particular, what distinguishes ungrammatical examples from cases of semantic anomaly or pragmatic infelicity? This article has reviewed various proposals that address this question and has also outlined a possible new direction.
