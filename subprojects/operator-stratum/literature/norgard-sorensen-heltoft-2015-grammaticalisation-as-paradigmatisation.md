### Grammaticalisation as paradigmatisation

### Jens Nørgård-Sørensen & Lars Heltof University of Copenhagen / Roskilde University

By arguing that grammaticalisation inevitably involves paradigmatisation, i.e. formation of new paradigms or reformation of existing paradigms, we challenge leading approaches to grammaticalisation that consider the stages on the so-called cline of grammaticality the prototypical path of grammaticalisation. Afer having questioned the notions of scalarity and gradualness, we present our concept of the grammatical paradigm and demonstrate the value of this concept in a discussion of examples from Polish, Russian, Danish, Italian and Finnish. Te examples show that paradigmatic organisation is not restricted to morphology, but is also characteristic of word order and constructional syntax. Articles by prominent contributors to Te Oxford Handbook of Grammaticalization (2011) are discussed during the presentation.

### . Introducing the paradigm

In Nørgård-Sørensen, Heltof and Schøsler (2011: xi) we argue that grammaticalisation always involves paradigmatisation; moreover, that paradigmatic organisation or reorganisation is an essential part of grammaticalisation. We further emphasise that this is true not only for grammatical items that are traditionally associated with paradigms: morphological markers (inflections) and grammatical words (auxiliaries, particles, prepositions, etc), but also for all other layers of grammatical organisation, including constructional syntax, word order (topology) and prosody.

In the linguistic tradition, a paradigm is identified as a set of grammatical elements alternating in one and the same syntagmatic context (Lyons 1968: 73). Not rejecting this fundamental understanding we want to stress that it ignores some essential features of the paradigm and, thus, is in need of specification. First and foremost, we point to paradigmatic organisation as the defining feature delimiting the grammatical system from non-grammatical patterns of linguistic organisation (lexical, phrasal, and others). One cannot speak of grammaticalisation without a clear concept of grammar and grammaticality.

Lehmann emphasises that grammaticalisation is scalar (Lehmann 1995: 121) and gradual (Lehmann 1995: 12); in other words, that diﬀerent grammatical markers exhibit diﬀerent degrees of grammaticality and that grammatical items tend to develop from a less to a more grammatical status. Te ideas of scalarity and gradualness of grammaticalisation are not in keeping with our theory; a point to which we shall return in Section 3. In harmony with his view on grammaticalisation as characterised by gradualness and scalarity Lehmann (1995: 123) sets up a system of parameters which in his view reflect the degree to which a linguistic item has been grammaticalised. Paradigmatic aspects play a prominent role in the definition of these parameters, but still ‘paradigmaticity’ is treated as just one out of altogether six parameters. Te parameters are set up to specify the degree of autonomy of the linguistic item under consideration, because, as Lehmann (1995: 122) claims, “autonomy of a sign is converse to its grammaticality”. Providing a measure for autonomy the parameters, in Lehmann’s argument, function as a measure of the degree of grammaticalisation. As will soon be clear, we do not see autonomy – and its opposite, bondedness – as a defining feature of grammaticality and grammaticalisation.

Our approach calls for a specified concept of the paradigm like the one we have presented and applied to a number of languages and diﬀerent grammatical subsystems in Nørgård-Sørensen, Heltof and Schøsler (2011).

In the following we shall briefly present our concept of a paradigm, emphasising what we see as its defining features. Ten we will illustrate not only how this concept can be applied to diﬀerent grammatical subsystems, but also that it lends new insight to grammatical description. We shall start with a relatively simple, but usually ignored morphological change in Polish and then proceed with a handful of examples representing diﬀerent grammatical subsystems, including some that are not traditionally described as paradigmatically organised.

Te grammatical paradigm can be identified through a set of defining features (Nørgård-Sørensen, Heltof and Schøsler (2011: 5–6).

First, the grammatical paradigm is a closed set of items, the number of members being fixed at a given language stage.

Second, for every paradigm one must specify its domain, i.e. the syntagmatic context where it applies.

Tird, a paradigm has a semantic frame, i.e. a common semantic denominator within which the content of the individual members is defined in opposition to each other. Semantic frames are language specific and cannot be taken Grammaticalisation as paradigmatisation  to be equivalent to the use of cognitive networks in Crof (2001) and Crof and Cruse (2004).

Fourth, the choice between the members is obligatory. In the given syntagmatic context defining the domain of the paradigm speakers cannot avoid picking one or the other member. Te choice can be free or bound, but must be made.

Fifh, a paradigm is asymmetric, distinguishing marked and unmarked members, possibly in a hierarchical structure (especially in the case of multi-membered paradigms). Te markedness organisation of a paradigm is an important part of its semantic language specificity.

While the domain is a name for the syntagmatic delimitation of the paradigm on the expression plane, the ‘frame’ is its counterpart on the content plane. A paradigm is not a pure expression system, but a sign system with the domain/frame pair constituting a unity of expression and content.

With grammar and grammaticality defined with reference to the paradigm it follows that grammaticalisation, as already mentioned, is largely a matter of paradigmatisation. Considering that our model diﬀers from others in emphasising the role of the domain and especially the frame as necessary components of the paradigm we shall focus on these concepts in the analyses. We shall open the discussion by considering a fairly transparent example from Polish historical morphology.

### . Speaker and community, abruptness and gradualness

As already mentioned, the traditional notion of the paradigm is primarily associated with two layers of grammatical organisation: (i) morphological markers (inflections) and (ii) grammatical words (auxiliaries, particles, prepositions, etc.). Tese two layers are combined in the branch of grammaticalisation research that focuses on the transition from lexical unit to grammatical word and further from grammatical word to clitic and ultimately to aﬃx. In this direction of grammaticalisation studies such transitions, which can be observed in a number of grammatical  Jens Nørgård-Sørensen & Lars Heltof change scenarios, are identified as stages on the so-called cline of grammaticality, for example from Hopper & Traugott (2003: 7; see also Heine & Kuteva 2002: 2–4). content item > grammatical word > clitic > inflectional aﬃx Te very idea of a ‘cline of grammaticality’ may leave the impression as if adherents of this approach perceive grammatical changes as taking the shape of abrupt leaps. Tis is, however, not necessarily so. Recent years have seen an increasing emphasis on usage phenomena in linguistic research, including grammaticalisation studies, and linguists focusing on usage tend to emphasise the gradualness of grammatical change. Tis is, for instance, reflected in several contributions to the recently published Oxford Handbook of Grammaticalization (Narrog & Heine (eds.) 2011). In her discussion of usage-based theory and grammaticalisation, Bybee (2011: 70) states that “[b]ecause language change and, in particular, grammaticalization, is traced back to small changes that take place in actual usage-events, the theory provides a natural account of the gradualness of change.” Further, she mentions that “a number of researchers have defined grammaticalization as ‘reanalysis’ – a change in constituent organization or category membership”, and argues – correctly, as we see it – that such a view on grammaticalisation presupposes an understanding of linguistic categories and constituents as discrete. According to Bybee (2011: 72), this view is not in harmony with the “robust facts that languages are always changing and that grammaticalization is gradual […]”.

In the same handbook, Poplack (2011: 210) states that the assumption of gradualness of change is shared by grammaticalisation theory (apparently perceived as a cline phenomenon, though this is not made clear) and variation theory. In the same vein, Lightfoot (2011: 445–446) points to gradualness as one of the parallels between lexicalisation and grammaticalisation. Further, in his study of complex words arising from earlier syntactic phrases Haspelmath (2011: 345–46) goes somewhat deeper into the question and argues against what he calls “the widespread simplistic view according to which the coalescence of phrases into complex words consists of an abrupt reanalysis from the syntactic level to the morphological level […]”.

Considering the diversity and variability of documented language usage in all historical periods these statements appear intuitively convincing. However, as we see it, the linguists cited above fail to draw an important distinction, namely that between describing changes in the language of an individual speaker and in the language of an entire speech community. Tis distinction is crucial, and a number of problems of grammaticalisation, among others the problem of gradualness vs. abruptness, cannot be approached without specifying the level at which the change considered takes place: in the language of an individual speaker or in the language of the community as a whole.

Grammaticalisation as paradigmatisation  Let us consider the question of gradualness from each of these two points of view. For the linguist focusing on the development of the language of a speech community changes will appear to spread gradually – at times very quickly, at other times extremely slowly – but they are never introduced overnight, abruptly. We believe that the statements above are made under the condition – unfortunately not specified – that changes are considered as instantiated by a whole community rather than by an individual speaker.

Somewhat surprisingly, for the linguist focusing on the language of an individual speaker the picture turns more complicated. It is diﬃcult, if not impossible, to observe a change in the language of an individual speaker as it occurs, but this empirical problem should not prevent us from attempting to answer the question if changes in the language of an individual speaker are gradual or abrupt.

Referring to Andersen (2008: 32), Nørgård-Sørensen, Heltof and Schøsler (2011: 10) emphasise that, in principle, any change originates in the language of an individual speaker. Tere is simply no other possibility. A change cannot possibly occur simultaneously in the language of several speakers. If attractive (for whatever reason), the change will be accepted by other speakers and activated in their language – and so on.

Te changes that are documented by linguists are only the successful ones that spread to the whole community or a substantial part of it. Tus, what is observed and described is the spreading of the change, rather than the change itself – or what, following Andersen (2001), we will call actualisation. An actualisation process is by definition gradual. Te change itself, on the other hand, perceived as originating in the language of an individual speaker, is a matter of structural change; a change in the language system stored in the mind of the speaker and enabling him or her to produce utterances that can be understood and accepted as usage manifestations in the given language.

For this reason, we are critical of the grounds on which linguists argue in favour of gradualness in language change. We believe that they focus on the consequences rather than the origin of the changes. Te extended idea of gradualness presupposes a general disorder in language and a lack of the fairly complicated structure of logically interconnected lexical and grammatical categories without which language would not function as an eﬃcient means of communication. It follows that we consider the alleged oppositions between usage and structure on the one hand and between abruptness and gradualness on the other as unfounded. All of these concepts are essential to the description of language and language change, but while structure and abruptness are connected to reanalysis as a mental event in the mind of an individual speaker, usage and gradualness are relevant to the description of actualisation as a process instantiated by and aﬀecting the speech community as a whole.

### . Te marginal status of word order in grammaticalisation

Another consequence of the prevailing perspective on grammaticalisation: structure involves syntagmatic dependency − is the status ascribed to word order, or topology. In their contribution to the Oxford Handbook of Grammaticalization, Sun and Traugott (2011: 378 ﬀ., referring to Lehmann 1995) count the development of obligatory subjects in Germanic and Romance languages among the hallmarks of grammaticalisation since this process is an instance of increased dependency. Syntagmatic bondedness is an established parameter of standard grammaticalisation theory, and an extension of the concept of grammaticalisation to include developments of obligatory syntax is therefore an expected move. ### . Topological positions expressing grammatical categories

In Section 1 we characterised the cline theory as viewing grammaticalisation processes as changing free morphemes to bonded, dependent morphemes, a process leading to obligatory selection of the relevant morpheme in a given syntagmatic context (‘domain’, in the sense of Nørgård-Sørensen, Heltof & Schøsler 2011). Lehmann stresses − and we, too − that this must always mean a choice of this element in its category, that is: as an alternative to something else. Tis means that syntagmatic bonding presupposes paradigmatic alternation, and more than that: it means paradigmatic alternation in a closed, limited set.

Tis restriction on grammaticalisation proper is a useful tool for controlling the role of the semantic parameters characteristic of grammaticalisation, namely bleaching and subjectification. Especially bleaching processes are tricky since they  Jens Nørgård-Sørensen & Lars Heltof are ofen read as suﬃcient indicators of grammaticalisation. In Sun & Traugott’s analysis rendering of Breban’s analysis, cf. Section 4, appeal is made only to the bleaching process descriptive > distributional > quantificational, but bleaching processes are not suﬃcient as a criterion for identifying grammaticalisation. Once the issue of paradigmatic organisation is recognised, the question of grammaticalisation amounts to deciding whether the post-determiner quantifier class now including bleached several is a closed paradigm. From a topological point of view, this class includes numerals, a definitely open-ended class, but it is sensible to identify a small subset of pronominal quantifiers: some, same, many, few, a few, numerous, several etc. that quantify over numbers. ### . Te concept of a frame

A paradigm has a semantic frame, i.e. a common semantic denominator within which the content of the individual members is defined in opposition to each other. Behind the concept lies the idea that semantic organisation is at one level language specific and that this level cannot be described by a mapping of universal conceptual distinctions onto syntactic form. Hjelmslev (1943) called this kind of organisation content form, ‘form’ meaning ‘systematic organisation’. Tis structuring principle – not the word ‘form’ – has been maintained as a part of Danish Functional Linguistics (Engberg-Pedersen et al. (1996); Harder 1996a, b; Heltof 1996).

No other language known to us organises the speaker-hearer dimension in the way described initially for Polish verbal morphology; no other language organises the categorical-thetic contrast in the way described for Danish, and once the paradigmatic dimension is applied, it is hard to see how such organisation of meaning can be dealt with in taxonomic networks (as suggested by construction grammars). Te paradigmatic distinctions applying in Figure 1a, b are not parallel subspecifications of a taxonomic network in the style used by Crof and Cruse (2004: 321) or by their semantic networks (2004: 322), but our distinctions cut across the hierarchy. Te importance of semantic networks for providing models of conceptual space – or semantic substance, in classical terms – is beyond doubt, Grammaticalisation as paradigmatisation  but their use as models of mapping language specific syntax onto conceptual universals is not clear.

### . Conclusion

We have discussed three detailed examples of paradigmatic organisation, and semantic specificity is captured at two levels: (a) in the delineation of the semantic area covered by the paradigm (the ‘frame’), and (b) in its markedness organisation. We have given examples and analyses in support of the stance that paradigmatic organisation is not restricted to morphology, but is found in word order and in constructional syntax as well. As striking examples of the language specificity of the content organisation of paradigms, we have analysed from Polish verbal morphology a subparadigm organising the dimension Speaker-Hearer, and from Danish constructional syntax the paradigm of categorical and thetic constructions organising the area of descriptivity in a distinction between Descriptive and Nondescriptive12 sentences.13 To clarify our view of the relation between structure and usage, we have discussed the concepts of abruptness and gradualness and, contrary to the tradition perceiving these concepts as mutually contradictory exponents of traditional structuralism (abruptness) and modern functional-cognitive approaches (gradualness), argued that they are actually both relevant to grammatical change, although viewed from diﬀerent angles. While a reanalysis of a grammatical paradigm in the language of the individual speaker – though not observable – must be considered an abrupt turn, the spreading of a change in the language community is – as can be observed – gradual.

We believe that the concept of a paradigm as introduced here and exposed in detail in Nørgård-Sørensen, Heltof and Schøsler (2011) is a necessary contribution to the theory of grammatical change and grammaticalisation.
