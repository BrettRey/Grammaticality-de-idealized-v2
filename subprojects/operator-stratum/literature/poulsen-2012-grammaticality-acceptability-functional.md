#### The usefulness of the grammaticality–acceptability distinction

#### in functional approaches to language

Mads Poulsen* Department of Scandinavian Studies and Linguistics, University of Copenhagen, Copenhagen, Denmark The distinction between grammaticality and acceptability has been regarded with strong scepticism in functional linguistics because of its origin in Chomskyan linguistics. In this article, I will argue that the distinction is useful in functional linguistics, provided that it is based on a distinction between competence and performance, rather than on a distinction between syntax and meaning. The basic rationale for having such a distinction is that much of linguistics is concerned with describing relatively stable grammatical knowledge, rather than the psycholinguistic dynamics of language use. This article will briefly summarize the early history and rationale of the notion of grammaticality within Chomskyan and functional linguistics, before defining a functional, usage-based definition of grammaticality. Finally, the article will illustrate how this usage-based notion of grammaticality can be used as a framework for interpreting corpus and experimental data on language use.

Keywords: grammaticality; acceptability; competence; functional linguistics; usage-based linguistics 1. Introduction One of the main aims of linguistics is to describe the knowledge that users of a language possess, which allows them to speak and comprehend a language. A fundamental challenge to this endeavour is that we do not have direct access to knowledge. Instead, linguists have to make inferences about knowledge from observations of language use and intuitions about language. There are different approaches to how this is done. The tradition of Chomskyan linguistics maintains that the possibilities for combining words in sentences should at some level be described by rules that do not refer to meaning (e.g. Chomsky 1957; Newmeyer 1998), while various functionalist traditions maintain that the rules for combining words in sentences at the same time specify the meaning relations of the words (e.g. Croft 2001; Engberg-Pedersen et al. 1994; Harder 1996; Langacker 2000, 2008). But the different approaches seem to share the common goal of accounting for the fact that certain word combinations are possible while others are not within a given language. For example, Danish word order in subordinate clauses is usually described as following a different pattern than main clauses. However, in *Email: m.poulsen@hum.ku.dk q 2013 The Linguistic Circle of Copenhagen spoken Danish about half of the subordinate clauses appear to have main clause word order (Jensen 2011). The tendency to use main clause word order in subordinate clauses is much stronger if the subordinate clause carries the main point of the utterance, rather than backgrounded information. Christensen and Heltoft (2010, 93–4) reinforces this generalization with the judgment that main clause word order in subordinate clauses is possible if the subordinate clause carries the main point, as in (1) but not if they do not carry the main point as in (2): (1) Problemet var at hun fik aldrig betalt raterne Problem.DET was that she get.PST never pay-PTCP installments.DET ‘The problem was that she never got around to paying the installments’ (2) *Det er underligt at jeg har nu taget skridtet fuldt ud It is strange that I have-PRS now take.PTCP step.DET fully out ‘It is strange that I have now taken the step completely’ The position of the adverb aldrig ‘never’ and nu ‘now’ after the finite verb constitutes main clause word order. Christensen argues that the impossibility of (2) is explained by the emotive predicate ‘strange’ that attracts the main point of the utterance. These observations have been the basis of the generalization that subordinate word order is (to some degree) governed by the information status or mood of the subordinate clause, rather than its syntactic dependency. The very simple and uncontroversial point in the present context is that functional linguistics, as well as the tradition of Chomskyan linguistics, use distributional patterns and the researchers judgments of what sentences are possible (and which ones are not) as the basis for making linguistic generalizations (cf. Croft 2001).

If we thus accept that the issue of possible and impossible sentences is the basic data for grammatical description, two questions become relevant: (1) What does it mean theoretically that a sentence is (im)possible, and (2) how is this determined? Chomsky provided fairly specific answers to the first question from the outset of Chomskyan linguistics, but corresponding proposals within functional approaches have been limited, in part because of general scepticism of concepts originating from Chomskyan linguistics. The main purpose of the present article is to specify such a proposal within a functionalist linguistics. The specifics will be framed in terms of Langacker’s usage-based model, but I believe they can be generalized to most functional frameworks.

The answer to the second question, how does one determine whether a sentence is (im)possible, used to be that linguists consulted their own intuition, or were informed by more or less randomly observed real-world language usage. This is still a valuable information source. Over the last 20 years, however, there has been a growing interest in systematizing methodology. The availability of large corpora has made systematic analyses of what word patterns actually occur possible, and in recent years linguists have begun to use experimental methods to collect data from non-linguists on the interpretation and acceptability of specific word patterns (Bard et al. 1996; Hofmeister and Sag 2010; Poulsen 2008; Schu¨tze 1996). Both of these approaches can be seen as attempts to base linguistic generalizations on language use by non-linguists, rather than the intuitions of the linguist.

It is, however, not absolutely clear that language use can always be trusted to reflect the potential of the language system of the user. With regard to corpus data on language production, for example, people occasionally produce utterances in a way that they did not intend. For example, if we find language-use evidence that people sometimes say yes, when they mean “no”, it still does not seem reasonable to conclude that yes is ambiguous between a “yes”-meaning and a “no”-meaning. Likewise, with regard to comprehension, results from experimental studies could lead to odd conclusions if interpreted at face value. For example, Ferreira (2003) found that college students routinely interpret the subject of passives as the agent (cf. Kristensen et al. [2013] for similar results with unambiguous Danish objectverb-subject (OVS) structures). Despite such results from language use, it seems unreasonable to conclude that these structures are ambiguous between opposite meanings, or that they do not carry any coded meaning with regard to distribution of semantic roles. The main utility of a definition of grammaticality is to provide a framework for understanding how to deal with uncertainties about the interpretation of such data.

But there has been much reluctance to embrace this distinction in the usage-based tradition. In section 2, I describe the rationale for the Chomskyan distinction. In section 3, I briefly describe the reluctance to embrace this distinction in the functional tradition, but I argue that some of this scepticism is partly unjustified. In section 4, I describe a definition of grammaticality based on Langacker’s usagebased model (2000, 2008), but I modify it to allow a distinction between grammaticality and acceptability. But I do believe these reinterpretations are useful in linguistics in general.

### 2. The early history of grammaticality

In Syntactic Structures (Chomsky 1957, henceforth SS), Chomsky described the goal of linguistic analysis of a language as (1) the proposal of a generative device that will generate the grammatical sentences of the language and not the ungrammatical ones and (2) the description of the structure of the grammatical sentences (SS, 13). Grammatical sentences were described formally (but not quite explicitly) as the infinite set of sentences that constitute a language, informally as those that are acceptable to native speakers. Separating grammatical from ungrammatical strings was presented as a major concern in SS. But the focus was shifted in Aspects of a Theory of Syntax (Chomsky 1965, henceforth ATS) to the theory’s ability to assign a structural description to grammatical sentences. The distinction between grammatical and ungrammatical sentences was still maintained, though, as evidenced by the introduction of the distinction between the grammaticality and acceptability. In SS, these terms were used synonymously, or rather a grammatical sentence was loosely characterized as one that is acceptable to native speakers. In ATS, the two terms were separated and defined relative to the distinction between competence and performance. Competence reflected the abstract knowledge a speaker has of her language, while performance reflected how this knowledge is put to use in actual language use. Thus, while memory limitations, attention and other on-line pressures influenced performance, these factors were irrelevant to competence. Competence was characterized as a generative system that derives structural descriptions for grammatical sentences, and grammatical sentences were now more or less explicitly defined as sentences for which structural descriptions can be derived by the generative system, irrespective of performance factors (ATS, 11). Acceptability, on the other hand, was a matter of whether a sentence seems natural or is easily understood: “[L]et us use the term ‘acceptable’ to refer to utterances that are perfectly natural and immediately comprehensible without pen-and-paper analyses, and in no way bizarre or outlandish” (ATS, 10).

The acceptability status of a sentence reflects both the grammaticality of the sentence – whether the generative system is capable of deriving a structural description for it – and various performance factors having to do with how the cognitive system derives that description. As a consequence, a sentence can for example be unacceptable but grammatical. A famous example of this is the phenomenon of centre or self-embedding like in (3): (3) John saw the man who the boy who the girl kissed kicked leave Because this sentence simply contains a relative clause nested within another relative clause, a recursive generative grammar can easily derive a structural description for it. The sentence is thus grammatical. But native speakers find it unnatural and hard to understand, so it is unacceptable at the same time.

Grammaticality was also thought of as independent of semantic factors. This was quite clearly stated in SS where the famous sentence: (4) Colourless green ideas sleep furiously was given as an example of how a sentence can be nonsense, yet still utterly grammatical in the sense that it has the same structural description as: (5) Revolutionary new ideas appear infrequently Conversely, some generalizations seem to lack any semantic motivation, as exemplified by the difference between: (6) *The child seems sleeping (7) The book seems interesting The ungrammaticality of (6) as opposed to the grammaticality of (7) was explained by the analysis that interesting is a true adjective, while sleeping is derived by transformation from the verb sleep (SS, 73–5).

The exclusion of semantic and pragmatic factors from the notion of grammaticality got less clear-cut in ATS, because it was considered possible that selectional restrictions are part of the grammar. The question was whether examples like (4) should be marked as deviant by a syntactic or a semantic component because the problem with the sentence is that it violates selectional restrictions. Although the question was left open in ATS, the idea that separate syntactic and semantic components exist was still maintained. Only the placement of various phenomena within the separate components was up for discussion.

Newmeyer boils down the hypothesis of autonomous syntax in mainstream generative grammar to the following question:... whether the relationship between purely formally defined elements is so systematic that a grammar should accord a central place to formalizing the relationship among these elements without reference to their meanings or functions. (1998, 31) Newmeyer takes a slight departure from the description of generative grammar presented by Croft (1995) – an analysis that he otherwise follows – because Croft adds the additional qualification that autonomous syntax implies that the systematic relationship must also be self-contained in the sense that the system only refers to formally defined elements. Newmeyer’s motivation for not including self-containedness in his characterization of generative practice is that in some cases meaning has been allowed to leak into syntax. He gives as an example the inclusion of thematic roles into later developments of generative theory of syntax, and the discussion of selectional restrictions in ATS mentioned above at least opens the possibility of semantic features playing a role in the syntactic system. Even so, if semantic elements are allowed into the system, it is only in restricted and highly formalized ways.

How, then, does the autonomy of syntax fit into the grammaticality– acceptability distinction? On the one hand, grammaticality is, at least in SS, defined as a purely syntactic concept. On the other hand, grammaticality was defined in ATS as reflecting competence, as opposed to performance. And a full generative grammar – the model of competence – was proposed to contain syntactic, semantic and phonological components. One way of summing up the purpose of the grammaticality–acceptability distinction is that the grammaticality status of a sentence is data for linguistic theory, while the acceptability status is only data for linguistic theory to the extent that the status reflects grammaticality: a sentence may be unacceptable because it is ungrammatical. Seen in this light, a purely syntactic concept of grammaticality would make sense in Chomskyan theories of syntax, but a less restrictive notion of grammaticality (and its distinction from acceptability) might still be useful in functional theories because it provides a terminology for distinguishing between linguistic knowledge and the cognitive processes employed in using that knowledge.

### 3. Grammaticality in functional linguistics 3.1 Functional criticism of the notion of grammaticality

The distinction between grammaticality and acceptability has, however, been viewed with considerable scepticism in functional linguistics. The primary reason is that grammar in functionalist approaches is seen as intimately connected with meaning (cf. Lakoff and Johnson 1999, 485ff.). And because the traditional grammaticality–acceptability distinction is (among other things) based on a distinction between syntax and meaning, the basis of this distinction is considered false. The animosity towards the Chomskyan distinction between grammaticality and acceptability has been handled in different ways. Lakoff and Johnson (1999) appear to do away with the term acceptability. Others explicitly prefer to abolish the term grammaticality in favour of the term acceptability because the latter is not perceived as tainted by the idea of autonomous grammar (e.g. Jakobsen 2000; Jensen 2000). Others again seem to abandon the two terms completely in favour of other neutral terms like felicity (Goldberg 1995).

But the distinction between grammaticality and acceptability was also motivated by a distinction between competence and performance, rather than syntax and semantics. In Chomskyan linguistics, the distinction between competence and performance is a reflection of the belief that knowledge of language should be studied independently of language use because knowledge of language is thought to exist independently of the processes that put it to use. The status of the distinction is not entirely clear in functional linguistics. Haspelmath (2000) has argued that the distinction is a pseudo-issue in the debate between functionalists and generativists because although a number of prominent functionalists have expressed themselves in ways that could be interpreted as being against the distinction, almost everybody accepts that people have domainspecific knowledge of language. Haspelmath (2000) agrees with Newmeyer (1998) that most functionalists seem to accept the distinction between knowledge of language and use of language, and that even Hopper (1987), who seems to have expressed himself most extremely against such a distinction, can be interpreted as assuming it in the following quotation: [S]peaking is more similar to remembering procedures and things than it is to following rules. It is a question of possessing a repertoire of strategies for building discourses and reaching into memory in order to improvise and assemble them. (Hopper 1987, 145) Haspelmath analyses these statements as follows: This passage can be interpreted as distinguishing clearly between knowledge of language (‘procedures’, ‘things’, ‘possessing a repertoire of strategies’, ‘memory’) and use of language (‘remembering’, ‘building discourses’, ‘reaching into (memory)’, ‘improvise’, ‘assemble’). (Haspelmath 2000, 237) Haspelmath’s conception of the competence-performance distinction seems to share its intuitive motivation with Chomsky’s, that is knowing something and using that knowledge are two different things. The primary difference probably lies in what is concluded from the recognition of the distinction. Haspelmath summarizes the difference in conception by suggesting that functionalists should be able to accept the following statement: “Knowledge of language (‘competence’) can and should be characterized independently of language use (‘performance’) and the social, cognitive, and communicative factors contributing to use” (Newmeyer 1998, 24, cited by Haspelmath 2000, 237, emphasis by MP). But functionalists should not accept the follow-up conclusion: “Competence should be studied independently of performance” (Haspelmath 2000, 238, original emphasis).

### 3.2 Performance as acts-of-use or mechanisms-of-use

Schu¨tze (1996, 20) has pointed out that the term performance has been used ambiguously to designate any kind of knowledge not specifically classified as grammatical knowledge, but also to designate the aspects of specific instances of use of knowledge of language as opposed to knowledge of language. I will refer to the latter as the act-of-use sense of performance; the act of applying the same rules recursively is what makes multiple self-embedding incomprehensible, not problems with the rules, according to Chomsky (cf. above). When Haspelmath argues that functionalists accept a competence-performance distinction, he uses the term performance in the act-of-use sense (behaviour as opposed to stable knowledge), but not in the second sense (anything but grammatical knowledge).

Some connectionist approaches to the study of language (Allen and Seidenberg 1999; Bates and MacWhinney 1989; Seidenberg and MacDonald 1999) have argued against a distinction between competence and performance on the grounds that connectionist models capture both language knowledge and use of the knowledge with no clear distinction between competence and the mechanisms that put this knowledge to use, i.e. performance. This is, however, a third sense of the term performance, which I will refer to as the mechanisms-of-use sense. A distinction between competence and mechanisms-of-use is unnecessary, connectionists argue, because connectionist networks can store grammatical knowledge and process language with one computational mechanism.

Even if this is true, one can, from the point of view that Haspelmath represents, say that it is actually possible to distinguish between competence and performance in the act-of-use sense, even in connectionist networks; the competence aspect is the network architecture (i.e. the nodes, the connections between them and the connection weight values), while the (act-of-use) performance aspect is the specific instantiations of processes of the spreading of activation in networks (computing an output from an input). The point of such a perspective would be to emphasize that even connectionist models possess an element of somewhat stable grammatical knowledge. It may be true that most connectionist models of language implement learning algorithms to the effect that the knowledge may change slightly from performance event to performance event, but the outcome of each performance event is (among other things) the product of the accumulated knowledge.

Thus, the connectionist views on the competence-performance distinction need not be in contradiction with Haspelmath’s view because by performance the connectionists mean mechanisms-of-use, while Haspelmath means acts-of-use. A clear distinction between competence and acts-of-use should be acceptable to all parties. I will assume that the question of the distinction between competence and mechanisms-of-use is a psycholinguistic or neurolinguistic issue, which linguists are not obliged to solve. In the following section I will assume an act-of-use sense of performance.

### 4. Usage-based grammaticality

### 4.1 A usage-based model of language

Even though many functional linguists seem to reject the notion of grammaticality, considerations of which sentences are possible are often the foundation of description and generalizations in functional linguistics. This raises the question: What does it mean for a sentence to be possible within a functional linguistic framework? Langacker (1987, 2000, 2008) deviates from the rule by providing an explicit definition within his usage-based cognitive grammar framework. The followingis based on Langacker (2000),but I consider this frameworkto be largely compatible with most construction grammar approaches (e.g. Croft 2001; Goldberg 1995; Lakoff 1987), and I believe that what follows in general applies to these approaches.

The main feature of the framework is that grammatical and lexical knowledge is perceived as consisting of an inventory of symbolic units. Units are in other versions of construction grammar referred to as constructions. I will use the term construction in the following text because it is more widely used. A construction is an association of a mental representation of an observable entity – known as the phonological pole – with a conceptualization, i.e. some sort of meaning representation, known as the semantic pole. The job of the linguist is to describe the set of constructions that allow the language users to comprehend and produce sentences. For example, the fact that people ascribe a certain meaning to a sentence like: (8) The man was bit by the dog, is explained by the existence of a construction that roughly can be described as follows:

be VERB:PASS by OBLIQUE]
[PATIENT
ACTION
AGENT]

In the usage-based model, the task for the comprehender of an utterance is to categorize the utterance as an instance of a construction (or complex of constructions) in her linguistic inventory. The terms elaborate and instantiate are further used to designate categorizing relationships where the categorization proceeds smoothly because the utterance meets the specifications of the categorizing unit.

This leaves the following problem: how does the comprehender choose by which construction a particular utterance should be categorized? Langacker (2000) suggests that in the categorization process, a number of constructions, an activation set, that share some similarity with the utterance, are activated to some degree. The construction that attains the highest level of activation after a process of competition wins. Several factors can influence this competition:

(1) The entrenchment of the construction: Highly entrenched units are easier

to activate than less entrenched units. Entrenchment is a process whereby a unit gets established and strengthened in memory through the repeated experience of the association of a certain expression structure with a certain conceptualization. (2) Contextual priming: Constructions can be activated by features in the context. (3) Degree of overlap: Constructions that have a higher number of overlapping features with the target utterance will be more highly activated.

Entrenchment can be exemplified with numerous examples from the psycholinguistic literature. Frequent structures have been shown to be processed more easily than less frequent structures (e.g. Ferreira 2003; Garnsey et al. 1997; Kaiser and Trueswell 2004; MacDonald et al. 1994). For example, subject-relative sentences are read faster than object-relative sentences, but the processing cost can be ameliorated through experience (Wells et al. 2009).

The best-researched example of contextual priming is semantic priming where recognition of a word is facilitated if the word is preceded by a semantically related word (cf. Balota 1994 for a review). It has also been found that the difficulty of certain infrequent structures is ameliorated if the structures are read in suitable contexts that prepare or prime the comprehender for this structure. For example, Kaiser and Trueswell (2004) found that the extra reading time associated with Finnish OVS structures (compared to canonical subject-verb-object structures) was greatly reduced when presented in a suitable context. In the usage-based model this could be explained as contextual priming pre-activating the OVS construction, which will then get a leg up, as it were, in the ensuing competition process, allowing faster resolution of a winner (cf. MacDonald et al. 1994 for a similar account couched in Government & Binding terms).

The third factor, degree of overlap, has a certain intuitive motivation. According to Langacker, it also has the effect that more specific constructions will have an advantage over more general constructions in competition, because the higher degree of detail in the specific units automatically allows for better overlap, if overlap is measured in the number of matching features.

4.2 A usage-based definition of grammaticality Within this model, Langacker defines grammaticality (the terms conventional and well-formed are also used) as a matter of the degree to which an utterance can be categorized as a unit in the inventory of units that comprises the knowledge of grammar a person has: Let us consider the minimal case, in which a single linguistic unit (i.e. construction, MP), [A], is used to categorize a particular facet, (B), of a usage event. There are two basic possibilities.... On the one hand, [A] can be recognized in (B), which thus instantiates [A].... This amounts to the judgment that (B) is well-formed with respect to [A]. We can also describe it as being conventional, in the sense of conforming to the linguistic convention embodied in [A]. On the other hand there may be some discrepancy between the two structures. In this case (B) is not perceived as an elaboration of [A], but rather as an extension from it... This amounts to the judgment that (B) is ill-formed, or non-conventional with respect to [A]. (Langacker 2000, 10, original emphasis) Thus, if there is a straightforward match between an utterance (B) and a construction [A] in the inventory of constructions, (B) can be said to be grammatical with respect to [A]. If (B), however, does not completely meet the specifications of [A], (B) can be said to be ungrammatical with respect to [A]. Langacker’s definition of grammaticality is thus relative in the sense that a particular acceptability judgment will depend on which unit a particular utterance is matched against.

It is notable that Langacker’s definition of grammaticality is described in terms of “a usage event” and the “perception” of such events, i.e. what might be considered performance terms. This does, however, entail some difficulty with regard to the grammaticality status of certain sentences. If we consider actual categorization situations where the performance process of finding a specific construction of comparison is relevant, it is possible that a sentence can sometimes be grammatical according to the definition, at other times ungrammatical. For example, Ferreira (2003) found that college students interpreted reversible passive structures as actives in 19% of the cases (as opposed to 1% of the cases for active structures). Within Langacker’s framework, this amounts to saying that the participants in these cases attempted to categorize the structures as instantiations of an active construction. However, the passive morphology of the verb in the perceived utterance would presumably mismatch the morphological specifications of the active construction, on the assumption that passive morphology is not part of the active construction. This assumption seems reasonable given that both active and passive sentences were interpreted in the standard ways in the vast majority of cases (81% in the case of passive sentences). Thus in these cases, the students sometimes seem to interpret passive sentences as active sentences even though the sentences therefore would be ungrammatical (according to their own criteria) relative to the chosen construction of comparison.

To avoid the confusion of having the same sentence be grammatical and ungrammatical at the same time, I suggest that it is useful to have a different term than grammaticality reserved for the outcome of specific usage-events, whether they are judgments or simple comprehension events. The term acceptability offers itself nicely here, because it was basically because of situations tempting one to designate the same sentence as both grammatical and ungrammatical that Chomsky introduced the distinction between acceptability and grammaticality. To put the distinction differently: When we ask if a sentence is grammatical, we ask if under the “right” circumstances it can be categorized and thus processed effortlessly. When we ask if a sentence is acceptable, we ask if a sentence is categorized and thus processed effortlessly under the “particular” circumstances. Under this definition, all intuition-based judgments are acceptability judgments, while the assertions of grammaticality are theoretical abstractions about the language system.

Langacker’s definition of grammaticality can be modified to fit this distinction if we abstract away from particular usage events and specific reference constructions: (10) Generalized usage-based definition of grammaticality Grammatical: A sentence (B) is grammatical to the extent that there exists a construction in the inventory of constructions that it instantiates. Ungrammatical: A sentence (B) is ungrammatical to the extent that there exists no construction that it instantiates.

Another way of putting this is that a sentence is grammatical if the language system provides straightforward, conventionalized means for deriving the meaning it is ascribed. The main differences between this definition and Langacker’s are that it is not relative in the sense that it does not refer to a particular construction of comparison, and it does not refer to the process of choosing a reference construction. The main benefit of the generalized definition is that it defines the grounds for distributional analysis, which should be based on grammaticality, not acceptability. If a sentence is grammatical, a description of the language should include a construction that it instantiates. Conversely, if a sentence is ungrammatical, a description of the language should not contain any constructions that the sentence instantiates. It is still not clear exactly how we empirically determine whether a sentence is grammatical because we only have access to acceptability data, but we now have a framework for discussing and exploring methodology in a principled way, rather than dismissing data on ad hoc grounds.

The above definition of grammaticality is ambiguous with regard to where to find the inventory of constructions: in the head of the individual language user or in an abstraction over the language use of a number of individual language-users (cf. Harder’s, 2012, distinction between competency and langue)? Both interpretations are possible. The individual language-user has an inventory, and thus grammaticality can be seen as relative to the individual user’s system (Harder’s competency). Linguistics, however, is usually concerned with describing language as practiced by communities of people, and often it seems reasonable and practical to assume that the inventories of language-users in a language community are convergent. On this interpretation, grammaticality is relative to a shared norm. In the following section I will assume convergence, but the definition of grammaticality can be specified to allow a distinction between individual and shared grammaticality.

### 4.3 Differences between usage-based and Chomskyan grammaticality

There are still differences between the usage-based and the Chomskyan distinction between grammaticality and acceptability. First of all, the usagebased conception of grammaticality and acceptability specifies the performance process associated with making judgments to some degree. Even though the definition of grammaticality abstracts away from particular usage events, the usage-based model still explains both competence and performance patterns. We can thus, based on the model, make predictions about when grammatical sentences will be judged unacceptable, e.g. when the relevant unit of comparison is not very entrenched. The example of misinterpreted passive sentences (cf. section 4.2) can be explained within the model: the active construction is much more frequent and thus more entrenched than the passive construction, with the result that the active construction might win out in the activation competition in some usage events concerning passive structures because there is some feature overlap between active and passive sentences.

Another difference between the Chomskyan and the usage-based concept of grammaticality is that there is no reason why meaning apriori should be considered irrelevant to grammaticality in the usage-based conception. Usagebased grammaticality is a matter of meeting the specifications of a construction, and because constructions are defined both in terms of expression and meaning, meaning may be relevant for grammaticality to the extent meaning is specified in constructions.

Yet another difference between the usage-based and Chomskyan notions of the grammaticality–acceptability distinction stems from differences in the ontological status the theories ascribe to their descriptions. For Chomsky, the distinction between competence and performance was presumably thought to correspond to distinctions between neuropsychological components. Langacker (2000, 6), on the other hand, warned against taking his model too literally. He argued that in actual neurological implementation, we should probably not think of the constructions as discrete entities that get accessed and directly compared to utterances. Instead, he suggests that the neurological implementation is more like a dynamical system, such as a connectionist network. Constructions are implemented as attractors so that given some input utterance, the output of the system will converge on the closest attractor. The precise details of how the usagebased model should be implemented are very sketchy indeed. The basic point is that the warning against taking the model too literally is a way of sidestepping the issue of the distinction between competence and performance in the mechanismsof-use sense (cf. section 3.2).

In summary, I have argued that it is possible and useful in functional, usagebased linguistics to have a distinction between grammaticality and acceptability, which is based on a distinction between competence and performance (in the actof-use sense). The usefulness of the distinction is that it provides a framework for principled arguments about which data to base linguistic generalizations on. Linguistic description should mainly be based on grammaticality, rather than acceptability. One may wonder why we need the concept of grammaticality when it abstracts from what are important language processes in the model. The answer is that a lot of linguistic work is more concerned with describing the potentials of a language than the mechanisms and processing underlying the use of these potentials. A description of the inventory of constructions of a language is a description of the potential means of expression for that language. Such a description can be useful even if it does not specify all the processing mechanisms that implement these potentials. This does not rule out that linguistic description can also be concerned with performance issues, or that performance facts can inform linguistic description. For example, if we find that infrequent structures that are associated with severe processing costs, e.g. OVS structures, are more easily processed in specific contexts, then that provides evidence about contextual expectations associated with the structure (cf. Altmann and Steedman 1988; Kaiser and Trueswell 2004).

### 5. Empirical implications

The usage-based distinction between grammaticality and acceptability was introduced to capture the possibility that actual language use does not always reflect the stable knowledge of linguistic constructions. The problem is that if language use only ambiguously reflects competence, how then do we know the grammaticality status of a sentence? The generalized definition of grammaticality does not solve this issue, but the usage-based model provides a framework for principled discussion of how and to what extent different methodologies provide evidence for grammaticality in particular cases. It is very important that such discussions have a principled foundation, because otherwise the distinction between grammaticality and acceptability devolves into a pretext for dismissing annoying data.

If a certain structure is widely attested, e.g. in corpus studies, it is very difficult to argue that it does not reflect grammaticality. As mentioned earlier, Danish subordinate clauses have traditionally been described as having a word order pattern that is distinct from main clauses. But corpus studies of spoken Danish have shown that a very large proportion of subordinate clauses (around 50%) have so-called main clause word order (Jensen 2011). It is difficult to dismiss what language users do half of the time as a performance phenomenon.

Corpus methods have enjoyed much attention in recent years, especially in usage-based approaches because the data are usage data by definition. But corpus studies have their limitations. First, we cannot conclude from non-occurrence that a structure is ungrammatical; it could just be sufficiently infrequent to not show up in a corpus of a certain size. Secondly, it is not clear what to conclude about structures that are infrequently represented in a corpus because they may simply represent slips of the tongue rather than the status of the inventory of constructions. To compensate for these problems, researchers across theoretical approaches have begun to use experimental methods to explore the language sensibilities of language users. These methods generally involve having more or less linguistically naive language users respond to carefully manipulated sentence materials. A popular response method has been acceptability judgments where experiment participants rate sentences on an acceptability scale without knowing the theoretical question under investigation (e.g. Bard et al. 1996; Hofmeister and Sag 2010; Poulsen 2008; Sprouse et al. 2012). Others have used self-paced reading experiments that reveal whether a certain structure is associated with a processing cost compared to minimally different structures (e.g. Hofmeister and Sag 2010; Poulsen 2008). The benefit of these methods over corpus studies is that they allow the researcher to carefully control which structures to investigate in a way that corpus methodologies do not. They do, however, share with corpus data the problem that the data only indirectly reflect language competence and grammaticality.

In the following section, I will present two examples of investigations that used experimental methods to complement corpus observations. The point is to illustrate how the generalized usage-based definition of grammaticality can be used as a framework for understanding what can and what cannot be concluded from the data.

### 5.1 Grammatical agreement in Danish

It is a common observation that grammatical agreement in language use sometimes deviates from standard linguistic description. For example, Danish has number (and gender) agreement between subjects and subject predicates, as in: (11) Jeg er vild med dem I am crazy-ø about them ‘I am crazy about them’ (12) Vi er vild-e med dem. we are crazy-PL about them ‘We are crazy about them’ But Engberg-Pedersen and Poulsen (2010) found the following attested example of complete lack of agreement: (13) Det er jeg glad-e for at høre at du kunne lide det... 1 that am I happy-PL PREP to hear that you could like it... ‘I am pleased to hear that you liked it... ’ In (13), the plural marking on the adjective does not agree with any of the nouns in the utterance. The question then is whether and how the description of Danish should be amended to include these examples. Engberg-Pedersen and Poulsen explored the phenomenon further in a self-paced reading experiment. In the framework of the present article, the rationale was that grammaticality is a matter of straightforward categorization. Reading-time is generally in psycholinguistic work assumed to reflect cognitive effort, and so reading-time can be used as an index of how straightforwardly a particular structure is categorized. EngbergPedersen and Poulsen foundthatnaivelanguage comprehendersslowed downtheir reading in sentences with complete agreement mismatch (similar to [13]) compared to sentences with traditional agreement. This suggests that complete lack of agreement violated their grammatical expectations, an indication that such sentences are ungrammatical despite the fact that they are sometimes produced.

However, the case was less clear when the adjective mismatched the subject, but matched another noun in the utterance, as in the following example: (14) Altsa˚ normalt er jeg vild-e med gadgets in fact normally am I crazy-PL about gadgets ‘In fact, generally I am crazy about gadgets’ In a corpus study, they found such alternative agreement in somewhere between 0 and 11% of the relevant cases. Also, in the self-paced reading experiment, such sentences with possible alternative agreement were read significantly faster than no-agreement sentences. This suggests that alternative agreement is categorized more straightforwardly than no agreement. The alternative agreement sentences were read slower than the traditional agreement sentences, but the difference was not statistically reliable. It is thus difficult to conclude that traditional agreement is categorized with less effort than alternative agreement. Engberg-Pedersen and Poulsen concluded that it was not possible to rule out that such examples are indications of a budding alternative agreement convention in Danish, but that language users sometimes produce agreement errors that probably do not reflect their grammatical knowledge (competence). In other words, examples of complete agreement mismatch should not inform the description of the Danish agreement system, while the status of alternative agreement is uncertain.

5.2 Constraints on extraction in Danish Extraction out of complement clauses is well attested, and is generally considered possible in Danish. However, in a corpus study of spoken Danish Jensen (2001) found some, but very few, examples of extraction out of adverbial clauses, which is otherwise considered ungrammatical (e.g. Togeby 2003): (15) Deti blev hun smaddersur fordi jeg sagde _i thati became she very.angry because I said _i ‘She became very angry because I said that’ On the basis of a few examples and her own acceptability judgment, Jensen suggested that extraction out of adverbial clauses is possible if there is a high degree of semantic cohesion between the main and the adverbial clause. But in a series of self-paced reading experiments, Poulsen (2008) found that extraction out of cohesive adverbial clauses was associated with a reading-time cost similar to extraction out of non-cohesive adverbial clauses. Extraction out of complement clauses was not associated with a cost (compared to non-extraction sentences). Similar to the agreement example above, this suggests that language users are not able to straightforwardly categorize structures such as (15) as instantiations of an extraction construction. Thus, the evidence suggested that the description of extraction in Danish should not refer to semantic cohesion because semantic cohesion had no measurable effect. The evidence also suggested that a description of the extraction construction probably should not allow instantiations with adverbial clauses because they are very infrequent and associated with processing costs not found in complement clause extraction. Furthermore, they were judged very low on acceptability in separate acceptability judgment experiments.

However, even though the data showed that categorization of adverbial clause extraction is not straightforward, we do not know whether this is because there are no constructions that such structures instantiate, or whether a suitable construction exists, but that the categorization is made difficult because of performance factors in the same way that passive sentences are difficult to categorize (cf. section 4.2). The difference is important for whether the constraints should be implemented in the description of the construction or in the details of the processing system. Some researchers have found evidence that constraints on extractions can be explained by general cognitive constraints (Hofmeister and Sag 2010; Kluender 1998; Kluender and Kutas 1993), but others have failed to find correlations between individual differences in cognitive capacity and acceptance of extraction phenomena (Sprouse et al. 2012). So, at present it is unclear whether the constraints should be captured by the details of the involved constructions, or in the details of the processing system that put the constructions to use. Still, there is no empirical evidence supporting the inclusion of semantic cohesion in the description of extraction, so that possibility can for the time being be ruled out.

### 6. Conclusion

In this article, I have argued that it is theoretically and empirically useful to have a distinction between grammaticality and acceptability in functional linguistics, as long as the distinction is based on a competence-performance distinction, rather than a syntax/everything-else distinction. The distinction is useful in dealing with the possibility that not every act of language use is a good reflection of the linguistic conventions the language user has at her disposal. It is, however, important that acceptance of this possibility does not become a pretext for ad hoc dismissal of annoying data. A usage-based model provides a framework for understanding both competence and performance aspects of the data, and thus for pursuing principled empirical investigations of what should be ascribed to competence and what should be ascribed to the performance. But an important point is that no one methodology provides direct and unambiguous data on competence if we understand competence to mean the largely stable knowledge of language that the language users possess.
