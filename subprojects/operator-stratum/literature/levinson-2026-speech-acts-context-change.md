## Stephen C. Levinson* Why speech acts can’t be reduced to matters of context change

https://doi.org/10.1515/tl-2026-2001 Abstract: The concept of common ground has proved to be invaluable for many linguistic purposes, for the explication of, among other things, presupposition, implicature cancellation, and discourse entailments. It has been hoped that speech acts could be characterized purely in terms of context change, with assertions adding beliefs, promises, commitments, and so forth, to the common ground. But consideration shows that our current concepts of common ground are very underdeveloped. Moreover, the diﬀerent types of speech act are very diverse and culturally variable. Most important, speech acts are properly construed as a species of action, and the common ground as a set of propositions merely records the current score or state of play. The idea then arises of supplementing the context change theory of speech acts with an Austinian overlay. But the Austinian concept of speech act is too atomistic, too ungrounded in the empirical study of conversation, and doesn’t accord with the larger plan-motivated unfolding of discourse, due to its action characteristics. The paper concludes that while a better theory of common ground is highly desirable, it will not alone oﬀer a theory of speech acts.

Keywords: speech acts; common ground; context change

### 1 The idea: speech acts as operations on common ground

In the 1970s an intriguing idea was broached by a number of authors, David Lewis (1979), Robert Stalnaker (1978) and Charles Hamblin (1971) among them, namely the idea that speech acts could be characterized in terms of how they change the context, where context can be construed as common ground, that is, a body of information This paper owes its inception to a panel on the topic of speech acts and common ground, convened by Paul Portner and Manfred Krifka at the International Pragmatics Association meeting in Brisbane, June 2025. Common ground is, in Stalnaker’s words, “the mutually recognized shared information in a situation in which an act of trying to communicate takes place” (Stalnaker 2002: 704). 1 It had already come to the fore as a notion required by the phenomena of presupposition: 2 One can presuppose whatever has been mutually taken for granted, encoding the presuppositions in forms specialized for the job, so licensing such expressions as The Empress of India, John too did it again, She regrets taking LSD, and so forth (where the specialized linguistic forms are underlined). Therefore there has to be some such bank of mutual assumptions, incrementally augmented as we chat along. So, according to the original attractive idea about how to characterize speech acts, what does an assertion do? It augments common ground with the proposition expressed, and by and large that is the function of assertion. Of course, there are other speech acts, but they too can be thought of as operations on common ground. Promises add the speaker’s commitment to a future act, orders add an obligation for the addressee to do such and such, permissions remove prohibitions, and so forth. Viewed this way, we can think more generally of speech acts as specialized functions mapping contexts into other contexts.

Turning the idea into a full-fledged doctrine would require a couple of prerequisites. First, we should have an inventory of speech acts, complete with how they change the common ground. This turns out to be non-trivial, and, as Austin (1975) already implied, a culture-specific matter – for example, while saying “I do” in the right place in an English marriage ceremony can change one’s marital status, a Muslim man saying “I divorce you” three times also changes his marital status by eﬀectively divorcing his wife. So, in practice we will probably have to be content with some range of core examples from familiar cultures. A further issue is that the kind of speech act categories we’ve inherited from Austin (1975) and Searle (1969) are not in accord with those found useful by empirical analysts of conversation – these latter are categories like “pre-closings”, “self-identifications”, “assessments”, “continuers” and the like (Schegloﬀ 2007). These describe those action types left out of the philosophical treatments and they also more accurately subcategorize others. Here we leave the issue of speech act types to one side (but see Levinson 2017), as the second prerequisite is more pressing. For, we do definitely need a coherent notion of common ground, and that turns out not to be so easy, a matter we now turn to.

### 2 The concept of common ground

Like most good ideas about language, common ground has a philosophical background. It has proved essential to the analysis of many linguistic concepts: anaphora, deixis, ellipsis and of course presupposition. Gerald Gazdar (1979) showed that it could play a crucial role in explaining the cancellation of presuppositions and implicatures if they are contradicted by what is already in the common ground – think of it like this: we first chuck the entailments of an utterance into the common bucket, then the presuppositions, then the implicatures, in each case only if they’re compatible with what is already in the bucket (Levinson 2000: 49f.). Further, if we take the union of the content of an utterance together with the common ground we can get a bonanza of entailments that seem to go some way to explaining the eﬃciency of human communication, a feature exploited in DRT (Geurts et al. 2024) and Relevance Theory (Sperber and Wilson 1995). In addition to oﬀering many piecemeal solutions to semantic problems, a notion of systematically augmented common ground is foundational to the ‘dynamic turn’, the many varieties of formal semantics and syntax that now attempt to capture the mutual dependency between utterance and discourse context (see e.g. Asher and Lascarides 2003; Peregrin 2003; Ginzburg 2012). So, the need for an adequate concept of common ground cannot be in doubt.

Early versions were couched in terms of mutual knowledge, and introduced a number of problems. First, knowledge thought of in the philosophical way as justified true belief seemed much too strong – what we take for granted during a conversation we may hardly stop to query, let alone verify. Secondly, what does ‘mutual’ in ‘mutual knowledge’ imply: I know that you know that I know…. ad infinitum, which looks infinitely recursive? Are there ways to short-circuit the loop, stopping perhaps at the third re-iteration? The issue of iteration does not disappear if we prefer denominating common ground in terms of mutual belief, but then additional issues arise, like being committed to the non-obvious implications of a set of beliefs. Reviewing these issues, Bart Geurts (2024) argues we need a less psychological and more normative core concept or denomination, and he proposes commitment. A mutual commitment is a public undertaking that such and such is a state of aﬀairs, but we will see that this is also not devoid of problems.

Let us suppose that Bart asserts that Sydney is the capital of Australia; he thereby publicly commits to the truth of the proposition p. I don’t believe him, because I know better. I tell him Canberra is the capital of Australia. Clearly, Bart and I have diﬀerent sets of commitments. So already the idea of a single bucket of mutual commitments is inadequate. So, let’s have two buckets, one for me and one for Bart, each collecting our own assertions (a possibility considered by e.g. Asher and Lascarides 2003: 298ﬀ.). But in that case we are losing one of the key virtues of the context-change theory of assertions, namely that it gives an account of information exchange. So, we need a third bucket, into which we chuck all the asserted propositions that Bart and I agree with, and of course the fact that we each have stated such-and-such. Could we fill up the third bucket automatically, by mutually accepting anything that doesn’t contradict our own commitment buckets – that is, which is consistent with our own commitments? That would be too generous if I knew you were a famously unreliable source (a certain Donald comes to mind). Perhaps for the purposes of the conversation I act as if I am committed to your commitments, privately filtering the joint bucket later. Then conversation would be like a theatrical play: we entertain the fictions for the pleasure of the moment. 3 Clearly, we already have problems with assertion, but what about other speech acts like promising, threatening, apologizing, christening and all the sorts of distinctive action that Austin drew attention to? Here Geurts (2024) is I think trading on an equivocation on the term commitment. Commitment to do something is not the same beast as commitment that such and such. Portner (2007) recognizing this, introduced a “to do” list: my promise publicly adds an item to my list – but you, my interlocutor, will have your own list, and hey, we’ll need a third list, the shared commitment to, for example, meet tomorrow for breakfast. This makes clear that common ground is actually denominated in a number of diﬀerent currencies. Permissions for example change common ground by removing prohibitions, so we’ll have to keep a record of the current state of prohibitions. Declaring a man guilty makes it public that the defendant once presumed innocent is now guilty. When the Pope gives me an apostolic blessing my state of sin is, through the power of plenary indulgence, converted to sinlessness (at least if certain preconditions are met)! So, the currencies that common ground needs to be denominated in seem multiple and diverse, ranging from commitments that p to commitments to do p, from prohibitions against p, to wishes that p come true, from deciding attributes like innocence versus guilt, to (perhaps absurdly) conversions from sinfulness to sinlessness. For these are all the kinds of things that speech acts can change in the public record, as Austin (1975) was fond of pointing out.

### 3 Interlude: the problem of disputations

Much of conversational activity is cooperative, but we don’t always see eye-to-eye. Consider the following disputation between a teenage daughter Virginia and her mother:

But what gets added to the common ground as the outcome of the dispute over a raise in allowance or pocket money? As the action glosses (in conversation analytic style) make clear (partly motivated by mother’s shrill tones), the proposal to increase the allowance does not go down well. Notice how Mom’s outraged request for clarification ($10 a week?) is used to adumbrate a challenge, and how that is used to adumbrate forthcoming rejection: questioning is not really what these queryformatted turns are about. But the issue here is how do we update the common ground: there are individual commitments (e.g. to opinions about a proper amount of allowance) but little in the way of commitments to which both parties subscribe, other of course than the fact that these words have been used, and something like the glossed actions publicly exchanged.

This familiar family diﬀerence of opinion pales into insignificance against the disputatious discourse that fills the media both public and private. The point of this digression is to make the point that conversational activity does not always, or perhaps even by default, involve subscription to other peoples’ beliefs and opinions. We need a way to record disagreements in the common ground: it won’t be suﬃcient to simply abstain from adding p to the common ground (as in Ginzburg 2012: 18), we need to record that so-and-so holds such and such a proposition. Ginzburg (2012: Chapter 2) at least acknowledges the need to keep individual ledgers of propositions, although he ends up with what he calls FACTS, which seem to constitute more or less the traditional notion of common ground. Asher and Lascarides (2003: 298ﬀ.) suggest separating mutual belief about the discourse that has transpired (shared), from mutual belief about its content (which may or may not be shared), but then common ground is shorn of its epistemic content, undermining the function of conversing in the first place. This is the argument for the three (or more) buckets, one for each participant’s commitments or opinions, and one for joint commitments or beliefs, which will include naturally a record of what was actually said, with or without endorsement. (One notes incidentally that the philosophy of aesthetics has already wrestled a bit with this theme of diversity of opinion – see e.g. Baker and Robson 2017).

### 4 Another look at common ground in the context of conversational record-keeping

First, let us just review what most authors have in mind when they talk about common ground (for useful reviews of more recent work see Dänzer et al. 2025; Rubio-Fern´ andez and Harris 2026). Clearly, there must be some incremental representation of what has been said – a discourse representation of some kind (call this CG Basic). We know from the psycholinguists that it is the gist that is preserved in memory, not for the most part the actual words, so something probably more schematic than Kamp’s DRT for example (perhaps the gloss of the first line in (1) would be: ‘proposal from V: $10 a week from M to V’). 4 The essential contribution of speech act theory was of course that in addition to the saying, the locutions, there are illocutions, the acts performed by the words: these also need to be part of a basic incremental record, and, as mentioned already, these may need a variety of currencies, from individual but public commitments to joint undertakings, and more. Even a conservative reckoning, along the lines of Geurt’s (2024) recommendations, will need individual commitment slates and joint commitments – issues we’ll return to in Section 5.

Also, of course, we trade on what any reasonable person would take for granted – facts about the world, physical, social and political, the kind of thing we designate as ‘background knowledge’ (call this together with record of the discourse CG+; see Clark 1996). An unexplored issue here is how we actually assess what the other parties take to be common background assumptions, an issue that will come to the fore when talking to children, the demented and the like. An additional tranche of presupposable elements is provided by the mutually manifest speech event setting, the parties, the surroundings, the time and place that may be invoked in deictic reference (call this, in union with discourse representation and background CG++). The nested nature of CG++ is I think (perhaps fondly) uncontroversial: these are all things in the public domain.

Common ground might terminate there, but there are many other things that we need to mutually keep track of as a conversation proceeds. These incremental registers can be thought of as ledger books in which we record where we now are as we go along – the conversation analyst John Heritage (2012) suggested these could be called ‘tickers’ and suggested a number of them. For a start we need an anaphora window on the discourse representation: this is partly determined by the matter under discussion (about which more in a moment) and partly by language-specific constraints on person-number-gender agreement which make pronominal referents recoverable, but clearly this is a moving searchlight, as it were, over the discourse representation (Fox 1987). The formal semanticists have thought about the introduction of referents (e.g. with indefinite reference, allowing definite reference later; Kamp and Reyle 1993), but less about the way in which anaphora windows fade in discourse. We also need to recollect exactly how a referent was introduced (e.g. Manfred vs. Professor Krifka) so that back reference can be recognized. 5 Then we also need to keep track of the topic under discussion, else our contributions will be non-sequiturs. Unfortunately, we don’t know a lot about the empirical handling of topic in conversation (although see Yang 2019), but these are the spoken ‘paragraphs’ that aﬀect lexis (e.g. pronoun vs. full reference, Fox 1987) and prosody, and which warrant markers like ‘Oh by the way’ which announce change of topic. This conversation-analytic notion of topic is not quite the same as the ‘question under discussion’ (QUD), a notion used in formal pragmatics to pick out the focus and information structure of (mostly) individual conversational contributions and the relevance of answers (Roberts 1996, 2004); the conversational notion of topic is much more global in character. 6 We can with suﬃcient warning flip back to prior topics, so we need to track them.

Another ‘ticker’, another thing that conversationalists need to keep track of, is sequence structure, that is the sequence of speech acts, which can be centreembedded (Schegloﬀ 2007; and see Section 7 below). For example, in the exchange between Virginia and her mum, Virginia’s proposal for a raise is subject to a set of maternal queries and challenges, so the proposal still stands as not dealt with (in the part of the conversation illustrated). I will show below that it is absolutely essential to keep track of sequence structure because this determines what an individual turn at talk is addressed to, and thus the role it plays.

One initial thing we must track is when we last saw our interlocutors, because this governs the eﬀusiveness of greetings and more important, what counts as news. Implied by this, as Sacks (1992) pointed out, is the astounding thing that we have to try and keep a record of everything we have already told to each of our interlocutors – failure to observe this will make us thought of as at best the party bore or worse, a geriatric case. Only children, the elderly and the demented will be exempt from this constraint. Without this, we won’t even get our definite reference right (Clark 1996: 114).

Another running issue is activity structure (Levinson 1992): utterances are understood in relation to an ongoing activity, be it basket-ball, seminar or law court. Consider talk over dinner: at one level, we exchange views on politics, switching back and forth to another level, the passing of plates, the oﬀering of second helpings and the like, where the latter level takes priority. Such multi-level topical streams are commonplace. A related but additional ‘ticker’ is the need to keep track of our interlocutors’ special knowledge and expertise. I can’t without hazard tell the doctor what to prescribe, or criticize the handling of your children, or bargain about the price with the supermarket teller. In an elaborate division of epistemic labour, we must tread gingerly (Heritage 2013). The epistemic imbalance at the start of a conversation is, arguably, what motivates engaging in conversation in the first place, as Heritage has argued. This goes along with a general careful handling of relationships which is expressed linguistically, either in formal address terms and titles, honorifics in languages which have them (probably the majority), or more parenthetically by modulating utterance form (as in the elaborate indirections of English indirect speech acts). Although relationships may remain relatively static, the modulation of respect is often systematically upgraded when performing speech acts that are impositions of some kind (Brown and Levinson 1987), and all this requires tracking our slowly evolving relationship. A further ‘ticker’ that has been noted to mould conversation is the need to keep track of debts, obligations and favours done (Drew and Couper-Kuhlen 2014), because having done you a favour I can ask one in return. Every speech act has a beneficiary and a donor – my gift of salacious gossip, your oﬀer to pick up my kids, my oﬀer to pay for the lunch, my undertaking to find out the answer – they all create an uneven deontic terrain.

Although I don’t know of any serious review of all the ‘tickers’ or ledger books in which we record our incremental commitments in conversation, we can already see from this brief overview that there are many such ledgers beyond the relatively uncontroversial CG++ we outlined above. They are in diﬀerent currencies, epistemic, deontic, social commitments and undertakings, live anaphoric referents, current and last topics traversed, degree of sequence embedding, politeness levels, expertises to be respected, favours owed and even such special speech act denominations as curses or Apostolic blessings. Are these all potentially part of common ground? Yes, in the sense they form part of the cumulative mutual tracking that underlies the dynamic unfolding of interactive discourse – the essence of the thing that has recently attracted the attention of scholars in formal semantics and pragmatics. 7 In summary, a full and adequate theory of common ground continues to elude us. The original Stalnakerian vision of a single bucket of propositions or beliefs is hopelessly inadequate. Rather, it is clear that common ground will have to be thought of in terms of multiple specialized registers or log-books, some specialized by participant and eﬀect (e.g. Portner’s ‘to do’ list), some requiring acknowledgement of designated agents each with epistemic and deontic powers (doctors, priests, judges and so forth) – and each of these have their own intrinsic time-spans or discourse windows (e.g. a list of everything I have ever told Bart, vs. the current anaphora window on our chat, vs. the topic we were both addressing before the last one) and so on.

How all this works cognitively is a diﬀerent matter and indeed a puzzle: there seem to be a myriad of things to keep track of (Rubio-Fern´ andez and Harris 2026 usefully review the psychological literature). However, some of the cognitive load is lightened by the reminders before us – the presence of the participants jogs our memories of when we last saw them and our roles and relationships to them, while their responses clarify the conversational topic in hand, their echo of referring expressions indicate the anaphora window has not closed, and the very setting reminds us of the activity we’re in and the requisite formality, language or style of interchange. Our current place in a push-down stack of conversational turns is something shared between interlocutors and subject to repair if we get it wrong. So, some of the load is handled automatically, as it were, by processes of memory and

attention and joint action. 8 But still, how we handle all this in the heat of the moment under the extreme pressure of rapid turn-taking (Levinson 2016) remains a puzzle.

### 5 Can speech acts be reduced to changes in the common ground?

Let us revert for a moment to the original and attractive simple vision in which the function of an assertion is to add a proposition (e.g. as a belief or commitment) to the common ground. Updating the common ground in this way might beheld to capture the basic function of an assertion, its informativeness. This view is often attributed to Stalnaker, although he apparently held that an account of assertion would be independently needed (as Deniz Rudin has pointed out) 9 – with good reason, because one can assert things already taken for granted, or things everyone knows to be false, as populist politicians are wont to do. (The question of whether speech acts can be reduced to matters of context change is however live – see below, and van Elswyk 2021). So, informing would have to be taken as its basic default function rather than its essence, even though it is the reductive programme that is seductive. But that might be enough: one can claim that in the normal case we assume others’ assertions are grounded, and we add the content to our accumulating common ground. Abnormal cases may be viewed as abuses of a normative procedure, allowing us to hang on to the idea that the basic function of assertion is to inform. Many formal theories treat that increment of information as a reduction in possible worlds, an idea that goes back to Bar-Hillel’s sketch of semantic information. Geurts (2024) opines that this is psychologically implausible, and I agree: If I say “Thank God, my headache’s gone” you don’t suddenly scrub all the (contemporaneous) worlds in which Steve has a headache, because surely you never even entertained them. To use a sculpture analogy, the acquisition of new information feels more like adding clay to my model of the world than it feels like the subtractive process of cutting away stone.

This elegant picture of the function of speech acts seems to dissolve though once one considers speech acts other than assertion. Although there are correlations between sentence form and function, specifically for the three major types (declaratives, interrogatives, imperatives), there is no one-to-one mapping with asserting, questioning and ordering, as long noted (Levinson 2013a). A more reasonable suggestion is that distinctive semantic types associated with the sentence types can each lend themselves to diﬀerent pragmatic uses (as in e.g. Roberts 2018).

But once we leave this relatively tidy corner, things get involved. Now we have all the clutter of the specialized currencies that other speech acts require – permissions requiring the removal of prohibitions, with promises requiring ‘to do’ lists, declarations requiring institutional additions (e.g. assigning guilt, adjusting marital status, etc.), blessings requiring changes in the disposition of the supernatural, while exclamations signal the addition of surprise or horror or annoyance, and so forth.

But there is one idea that promises to remove this messy clutter brought by all the various speech acts. In the 1970s, Haj Ross, George Lakoﬀ and others adopted the so-called ‘performative analysis’ which held that an assertion like “Those cakes have cream inside” has an underlying form ‘I hereby assert that those cakes have cream inside’ (see e.g. Ross 1970). This has the merit of explaining the attachment of adverbials as in “Those cakes have cream inside, because I had one yesterday” which appears to modify the asserting. The attraction for us though is that we can now generalize the analysis across all speech acts, with underlying structures like “I hereby request/promise/declare…” etc. All these speech acts would have the property of being true just by being spoken: if you utter “I hereby promise to come”, you have indeed promised. Now, the original performative hypothesis ran into a number of problems which we will not rehearse here (see e.g. Levinson 1983: 247–263), but given that there has been a recent revival of the idea, with new versions which we may call Neo-Performative Hypotheses (see e.g. Krifka 2024; van Elswyk 2021), one signal problem is worth pointing out. The problem is that it is hard to get the truthconditions right. Consider if I say “The world is flat”, which I think you will agree is false under a normal construal. But the corresponding “I hereby state that the world is flat” is, ex hypothesi, true just by virtue of being said. To this George Lakoﬀ responded that the explicit performative should be true only in case both the asserting clause and the embedded proposition are true. But then there is an asymmetry between “I hereby state the world is flat” and “Steve stated the world is flat” since the latter will be true just in case Steve stated it, regardless of the truth of the embedded proposition. It is this kind of internal problem that would seem to doom performative analyses, old or new.

Even if the neo-performative analysis can somehow circumvent these kinds of internal problem, there is another objection. Because all speech acts are true just by virtue of being uttered, they all come in just one flavour. The unique character of diﬀerent types of speech act is erased by this analysis: we will add to the common ground, say, that the speaker is committed to the fact that p where p is, variously, e.g., the speaker will repay you the borrowed $6, God will bless you, you have been found guilty, etc.

But this is not my main objection. My main objection to the attempt to reduce speech acts to matters of context change or common ground update is that it is what Gilbert Ryle would have called a category mistake (Ryle 1949).

### 6 The ontological divide between actions and beliefs, knowledge or commitments

A precise record of an event is not the same thing as the event itself. A history of the Gallipoli campaign is not equivalent to the real thing, with bullets whistling by. Or, to echo the famous paper by David Lewis called ‘Score keeping in a language game’ (1979), consider a cricket game. At any particular point in the game, the score is shown on a big board (Figure 1). It reflects the play, ball by ball (an Over is a play of 6 balls; Note that D. Lewis is doing well!). But no-one would mistake this score-board for the actual sweaty game. What the common ground is, of course, is just a kind of score-board, not the actual doing of the speech acts. This score-board plays an important role as we have already detailed, licensing presuppositions, cancelling potential implicatures, guiding anaphor resolution, and so forth. But it is not where the action is. Common-ground is post-hoc, it doesn’t make the score, it just records the action.

But are speech acts REALLY actions? Some may think they are immaterial, with more akin to thoughts than to, say, kicking a stone – which was Samuel Johnson’s refutation of Bishop Berkeley’s immaterialism. For the doubting Thomases, consider:

### 1. Speech acts have material preconditions – for “Pass the salt” to work, there has to

be some salt in the vicinity. 2. Speech acts have material consequences – I may get the salt. 3. Speech acts are often hierarchically stacked (see (3) below), just like other action sequences. This helps to explain the coherence of discourse – conversations are not composed of isolated individual shots, but of systematic steps towards a goal. 4. The fact that speech acts are real actions explains the interdigitation of verbal and non-verbal actions. Consider for example the exchange at the supermarket checkout: (2) A: Can I have a packet of Marlboros B: Here you are dear ((passes packet)) A: ((produces money)) B: ((produces change)) It is because of this that there is a real ontological divide between speech acts and a set of beliefs or joint commitments.

### 7 Austin to the rescue?

A question that then arises is whether we can remedy the context-change theory of speech acts by a modest dose of Austin, which will get the action back in. Austin held that assertions too need to be thought of as actions, and cannot be reduced simply to matters of, e.g., belief increment. So, the question is: Does a model combining a theory of common ground with an Austinian account of speech acts, each with their felicity conditions, amount to a satisfactory account of conversational contributions? Although such an account is on the right lines, we now know, thanks to the conversational analysts, a great deal more about the empirical conduct of conversation (see e.g. Sidnell and Stivers 2013). The philosophical version needs a radical empirical update. Here are some things that need adjusting: 1. A single utterance can perform more than one speech act (see Levinson 2013a, 2017). Consider the utterance “Is anybody sitting there?” said pointing to an empty chair; a question it seems (although the empty chair itself already provides an answer), but primarily a conditional request for permission to use the chair. An Austinian account would make the questioning the illocution and the request a perlocution, although that request is obviously the main point or function of the utterance. Looking back at the quarrel between the mother and her teenage daughter above, we can see that nearly every utterance is double-barrelled (e.g. a challenge masquerading as a repair initiator). It is these alleged perlocutions that are actually governing the conduct of the conversation. There is of course a large literature on so-called indirect speech acts, with some discerning rules (e.g. question a felicity condition) for forming indirect English requests, but such rules won’t capture the double-barrelled utterances in our little quarrel in (1). 2. The kinds of actions that the conversational analysts have uncovered have in many cases no recognition in the natural language metalanguage (see Schegloﬀ 2007; Clift 2016). So, Austin’s discovery procedure for speech acts, namely potential occurrence in a “I hereby’’ phrase, is only a coarse sieve. Take for example “pre-closings”: these are the exchange of little wells that presage the closing of a telephone call: I say “Well” to indicate that I have no more to say, you say “Well” back, and then we know we can exchange Goodbye tokens. 3. Austin’s notion of uptake implies that utterances might often come in pairs like: (3) (Clift 2016: 13 simplified) L: Granny’s coming Monday K: Oh!

I hope the point is clear: conversational discourse is not flat, but highly structured. The idea that common ground would simply be an unordered set of propositions, a ‘bucket’ in the analogy we used above, is obviously not going to do the job. Nor will having a designated last move with some notion of ‘follow ups’ as in Ginzburg (2012: 17) be suﬃcient. But the actual depth and complexity of conversational structure may surprise our philosophical brethren. 4. Another, perhaps more important, problem for the Austinian analysis is that conversational participants often don’t address the action actually performed, but rather the plan or goal that lies behind the performance. That is why when the mother in extract (1) says shrilly “$10 a week?” you realize that Virginia’s proposal is not falling on fertile ground – a challenge or rebuttal is in the oﬃng. Austin’s emphasis on conventional procedures doesn’t capture the essence of the phenomena. This brings us to another topic.

### 8 The logic of action

When we make a cup of tea, we might first put water in the kettle, then boil it, and pour water in a mug prepared with a tea bag. Every stage of the process is governed by means-end reasoning – each stage should satisfy steps towards the goal. Now, as Aristotle (1969) made clear in the Nicomachean Ethics two and half millennia ago, the logic of action diﬀers significantly from assertoric reasoning or the logic of belief. For example, while aﬃrming the consequent is invalid in assertoric reasoning, it is essential in practical reasoning (Kenny 1966). Thus, “if I take the Qantas flight, I will get to Brisbane in time for the conference; I want to get to Brisbane in time for the conference, ergo, let me take the Qantas flight”.

Now, as Grice noted, conversation is a collaborative form of inter-action, and it follows that we try to discern each other’s goals and, other things being equal, expedite them. Hence in a service encounter you find short-circuited interactions like the following: (7) (cf. Merritt 1976) A: Have you got Marlboros? B: Here you are, $20.50 In that light, review again example (6) above. Here the whatcha doin’? near the beginning of a telephone call foreshadows a proposal, it is a pre-invitation or preproposal in the parlance of conversation analysis. The response Not much (in contrast say to Doing my homework) encourages delivery of the proposal by making it clear there is no impediment to the forthcoming proposal. So both utterances are oriented to the unvoiced but discernible plan of the caller. In many ways, then conversation skirts around unvoiced goals and plans. 10 Consider the following phone call, where a distraught bank manager rings to see if he can get an emergency ride to work: (8) (Schegloﬀ 2007: 64) D: ‘hh My ca:r is sta::lled ← Announcement of problem ((5 lines ommitted…)) I don’ know if it’s po:ssible, but (0.2 hhh) ← Unvoiced Request see I haveta open up the ba:nk.hh (0.3) a:t uh: Brentwood? hh= M: =Yeah:- en I know you want- (.) ← Unvoiced Rejection en I whoa- (.) en I would, but I’ve gotta leave in about five min(h)utes. (hheh) Here there is an unvoiced but manifest request, and an unvoiced but manifest rejection, and the bank manager rings oﬀ to call another acquaintance.

In short, actual verbal interaction does not look like the stately exchange of well-formulated speech acts, a kind of Austinian ping-pong. Instead it rather consists in adumbrating one’s desires hoping that the other party can short-circuit the whole exchange. This ‘oﬀ-record’ character of much conversational activity is much exploited in politeness strategies and strategic negotiations.

### 9 Conclusions

From all this we can reach some significant conclusions. Turning first to common ground, some model of this is motivated independently by a large range of linguistic phenomena, from presupposition to derivative inferences, and, yes, indeed it needs incrementing with the ongoing conversational discourse. Much of the recent upgrading of notions of common ground in formal semantics and pragmatics can still go through (e.g. extensions to imperatives, expressives, and social meaning), but as a record of what has been done, not the actual doing. A good start has been made for models of this kind of record in such works as Asher and Lascarides (2003), Ginzburg (2012) (with the proviso that they do not capture all of our ‘tickers’).

Yet common ground is a much more complex entity than normally construed. It has a nested character, with a basic discourse representation encased within further background assumptions. Second, the structure of the discourse representation is fundamentally hierarchical, with nested and embedded contributions which need to be kept track of. How best to formulate the details remains unresolved: It seems essential to have both public but participant-specific data repositories and a shared bank of information. But how should the records be denominated – how many currencies as it were do we need? Belief, commitment, agreement-for-currentpurposes, undertakings, obligations, changes in institutional status, and so forth? Thirdly, it would behove us to think about common ground in tandem with all the conversational properties that need to be kept track of, including anaphor windows, topic tracking, what is newsworthy for particular participants, favours done and asked, and so forth. It would be very useful to have a fuller review of all this, well beyond the brief remarks here.

But the idea that speech acts could be fully reduced to matters of context change or common ground update is, in my view, a mirage. First there are problems with the performative hypothesis which would seem to hold for its modern reincarnations. But more decisively, speech acts are really actions, many having material preconditions and material consequences. Here Austin was right, but the actual conduct of conversation doesn’t look Austinian: it is highly structured, yet is full of abbreviations and the unsaid. For the logic that drives speech act use is means-ends practical reasoning. Hence understanding conversation involves the pre-emptive guessing of intentions, plans and motives. So, common ground is a post-hoc record of what is done, like a cricket score, and the updating alone does not constitute the action.
