\begin{abstract}
I develop an account of why some readily interpretable deviations count as
ungrammatical while others count as lexical, phonological, orthographic,
stylistic, pragmatic, or social. The account treats a grammatical norm as a
socially sustained relation that governs how recurrent linguistic expressions
are formed, and a grammatical fault as a performance's failure to conform to such
a relation. It distinguishes that norm and failure from their consequences for
meaning and interaction, from what speakers treat as wrong, from what causes
those responses, and from the evidence available to analysts. I apply the account
to French article elision, English inflection and complementation, register and
honorific choice, and switch-reference. The analysis separates failures of
expression formation from recoverable meanings, unintended messages,
pronunciation, spelling, and downstream judgments or sanctions. It predicts that
relations identified independently as part of grammatical expression formation
will attract more consistent formation-directed attribution than matched
neighbouring faults. It also predicts that, among grammatical paradigms,
contrasts with stronger consequences for discourse organization will produce
larger differences between discourse-directed and form-directed repair. These
predictions test whether the analysis extends beyond the cases used to construct
it; they don't define grammatical status.
\end{abstract}

\noindent \textbf{Keywords:} grammaticality; linguistic norm; linguistic assembly; target attribution; semantic repair; discourse consequence

\section{The selective-classification problem}
\label{sec:problem}

In ordinary connected Reference French, \olang{hiver} `winter' begins with
\term{h muet}, and the singular definite article undergoes elision:
\olang{l'hiver}, not *\olang{le hiver}
\citep{boersma2007}, with the asterisk marking the latter as ungrammatical. The
deviant form remains easy to understand.
\textcite{simonenkocarlier2020} treat the modern French \olang{l}-forms as
definite determiners; \olang{le} still presents the noun phrase as masculine,
singular, and definite. The problem lies in how that article is realized before
this class of noun. The deviation is neither a wrong referent nor a failure to
communicate definiteness. It also needn't be treated as a general phonological
defect of French. The disputed relation belongs to the realization of the
article construction.

The transparency of this deviant form raises a general question. Why do some
interpretable departures count as ungrammatical while others count as semantic anomaly, poor wording,
pronunciation, spelling, style, or social conduct? Meaninglessness can't supply the
distinction: *\olang{le hiver}, *\mention{goed}, and many agreement errors are
transparent. Expressive substance can't mark it either. Segments, words, word
order, prosody, gesture, and writing can realize grammatical contrasts, while the
same resources can distinguish words, accents, and styles. An asterisk records a
classification; it doesn't yet explain that classification.

The relevant literature establishes several parts of the puzzle without
supplying one general answer. Acceptability work shows that a negative judgment
doesn't reveal whether grammar, processing, interpretation, or contextualization
caused it \citep{hofmeisteretal2013source,leivadawestergaard2020}. Semantic work
explains why some anomalous combinations remain interpretable
\citep{abrusan2019,abrusanetal2021shift}. Work on grammatical paradigms and
interaction shows how conventional choices can organize both expression and
uptake \citep{norgardsorensenheltoft2015,foxetal1996repair}. The remaining question
is classificatory and constitutive: what makes the failed relation grammatical in
the first place, and how can that status be distinguished from people's fallible
reactions to it?

My central claim is:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Constitutive claim.} A grammatical fault is a failure in a socially
sustained relation used to assemble recurrent linguistic expressions.
\end{quote}
In *\olang{le hiver}*,
the intended noun phrase and its definiteness remain identifiable, but the article
doesn't have the realization required in that environment. A poor lexical choice,
a misspelling, and an inappropriate register can also violate conventions, but
they disrupt different relations. The distinction is contribution-based: it
concerns what the convention governs, not whether the departure is noticeable or
important, or whether the relevant contrast is realized in segments, words, word
order, prosody, gesture, or writing.

The constitutive claim becomes clearer when six questions are kept apart. The
French example illustrates all six:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
  \item \term{Norm constitution}: what makes the article-realization relation a
  grammatical norm rather than a general phonological constraint of French?
  \item \term{Token application}: does *\olang{le hiver} fail that relation while
  preserving the other relations needed to identify the noun phrase?
  \item \term{Semantic or pragmatic consequence}: what remains interpretable, and
  what effect does the failure have on reference, force, or uptake?
  \item \term{Target attribution}: does a respondent identify the trouble as
  article formation, pronunciation, spelling, foreignness, or something else?
  \item \term{Response causation}: did that response arise from linguistic
  knowledge, processing difficulty, schooling, stigma, or the task itself?
  \item \term{Epistemic justification}: what evidence warrants the analyst's
  answers to the preceding questions?
\end{enumerate}
The last two questions concern the same judgment or repair, but they ask different
things about it: what the response points to and why the response occurred. And so
the six questions concern five kinds of object rather than six.

Norm constitution comes first. Within a specified linguistic repertoire, a
grammatical norm is a socially sustained relation among constructional units,
exponents, positions, or values in a recurrent construction. In a broad extension
of Saussure's relational
notion of \term{value}, I use the term for a conventional semantic, structural,
relational, or discourse-organizing alternative (for example, past, plural,
interrogative, or same-subject) selected or realized within a constructional
domain \citep{saussure1916}. A grammatical norm supplies a standard
for whether a performance conforms to the relevant constructional relation.

The adjective \term{grammatical} is easily equivocal here. A relation can be
\emph{grammatical} in the classificatory sense that it belongs to grammar, whereas
a performance can be \emph{grammatically conforming} or \emph{grammatically
defective} relative to that relation. I use the bare adjective for the first sense
only where the object is plainly a relation or norm; for performances, conformity
language keeps the two senses apart.

Other linguistic norms govern other jobs. A lexical convention establishes the
identity and content of a sign. A phonotactic convention constrains how
phonological elements may be combined. Orthography represents linguistic
expressions in writing. A usage norm
governs whether an otherwise licensed expression or persona fits an occasion.
These can overlap in one performance, but they shouldn't be collapsed
merely because all are conventional or socially consequential.

The constitutive claim is relative to a \term{repertoire}: the conventional resources sustained
by a particular community in an activity, medium, and period. Reference French
conversation, formal written French, and an English-speaking learner classroom
needn't sustain exactly the same relations. These dimensions define the scope of a
claim about grammatical status. Researchers may refine that scope using evidence
about who interacts with whom, what forms are produced and understood,
and where and when the forms occur. But the scope has to be fixed before examining
the judgments or repairs that the analysis aims to predict. If two independently
reasonable scopes yield different results, the broader repertoire may genuinely
contain competing norms; selecting whichever population restores the preferred
verdict would hide that fact.

An \term{assembly profile} organizes the analyst's evidence about a proposed
norm. It records whether a form and value are coupled at a constructional site,
how the alternatives are organized, and where the relation is licensed. Evidence
can come from production, interpretation, substitution, omission, productivity,
and distribution. The category descriptions needed to state the relation (such
as \emph{definite}, \emph{singular}, \emph{determiner}, and \emph{vowel-initial
noun}) aren't excluded. What's withheld is the prior verdict that the relation or
performance is grammatical, together with corrections and repairs that express
such a verdict, because those responses are later used to test whether speakers
identify the same relation. The profile can be uncertain or wrong; it represents
the proposed norm rather than constituting it.

With category descriptions allowed but status verdicts and later responses
withheld, the primary prediction is that relations identified as grammatical
assembly norms should more consistently
prompt speakers to say that expression formation went wrong than matched lexical,
phonotactic, graphic, or deployment faults. Explicit use of the word
\enquote{grammar} is a further outcome and may depend heavily on schooling. A
separate application concerns discourse: among independently identified
grammatical paradigms, stronger effects on move type, commitment, reference
continuity, or response possibilities should produce a larger difference between
discourse-directed and form-directed repair.

The six questions organize the paper. Section~\ref{sec:levels} explains why they
require different kinds of answer. Section~\ref{sec:norms} addresses norm
constitution, and Section~\ref{sec:failure} addresses token application.
Section~\ref{sec:semantics} concerns semantic and pragmatic consequences.
Section~\ref{sec:boundaries} uses difficult examples to test the proposed norm and
fault classifications. Section~\ref{sec:method} returns to target attribution,
response causation, and epistemic justification by asking how the account could be
tested or shown to fail.


\section{Five levels, six explanatory questions}
\label{sec:levels}

The word \enquote{grammaticality} is used for objects at several explanatory
levels. Here \enquote{level} marks a difference in what's being explained, not
a hierarchy in which one object is more fundamental than another. A formal
theory licenses a representation; a community sustains a linguistic norm; a token
conforms or fails to conform; a speaker issues a judgment; and an experimenter
records a rating. These claims aren't interchangeable. A formal grammar may fail
to generate a community-licensed form because the model undergenerates. A speaker
may reject a licensed form because it belongs to an unfamiliar dialect. And an
experimenter may record a low rating caused by processing difficulty rather than
by any grammatical fault. Treating all three outcomes as measurements of one
latent yes--no grammaticality property hides the dependencies that need
explanation.

For *\olang{le hiver}*, the norm organization is the proposed relation between the
singular definite article and its realization before this class of noun. Token
status concerns whether this particular performance conforms to that relation.
The semantic consequence is limited because definiteness and reference remain
recoverable. A correction such as \enquote{Say \olang{l'hiver}} is a public
response, and its wording may target article formation, pronunciation, or learner
identity. Corpus distribution, substitution contrasts, elicitation, and that
response all provide epistemic access, but none is identical to the relation
itself.

These levels are connected, but they don't form a single causal sequence. A norm
supplies the standard relative to which token status is assessed. The form and
value relations in a performance can affect interpretation and response; whether
the performance is called grammatical or ungrammatical doesn't itself produce
those effects. Processing difficulties, mishearing, task
demands, schooling, stigma, and institutional authority can also affect response
without altering the performance's grammatical status. A teacher may correctly
identify the article error because their training makes them attend to it; the
target of the correction and its cause are still different questions. Over longer
periods, production, accommodation, correction, and sanction can help maintain or
change the repertoire. That historical feedback doesn't make a particular
response the truth-maker of the norm to which it reacts.

Each question also calls for a different kind of explanation. A \term{constitutive
explanation} says what makes a relation a grammatical norm and what makes a token
defective relative to it. A \term{functional explanation} says what work the
relation does in assembling expressions or organizing discourse. A semantic or
pragmatic explanation says why an attempted combination is coherent, anomalous,
repairable, or consequential.

An attributional explanation identifies which relation a public response treats
as failed. A causal explanation says why a particular person reacted. An epistemic
justification says why an analyst should believe any of these claims. These are
the six questions in explanatory form. More specifically, successful prediction
of held-out fault attributions from an independently specified norm analysis can
strengthen the epistemic justification for that analysis. But a black-box
classifier of the word
\enquote{grammar} wouldn't thereby explain what makes the classified relation
grammatical.

The source-ambiguity literature establishes the need for this separation at the
response level. \textcite{hofmeisteretal2013source} show that a low acceptability
rating doesn't identify whether grammar, processing, interpretation, or
contextualization caused it. \textcite{leivadawestergaard2020} likewise document
dissociations between acceptability and grammaticality. These are constraints on
the use of judgments as evidence. They don't by themselves determine which
worldly relation a respondent is trying to classify.

The distinction between object and evidence also applies to formal and descriptive
grammars. A formal theory can undergenerate a
community-licensed pattern or generate one that speakers in the specified
repertoire don't sustain. Conversely, a descriptive grammar may record a robust relation without
explaining why it's grammatical rather than governed by a different norm. This
is why descriptive evidence has to enter as evidence for a proposed relation,
rather than as an inherited status verdict. In a compatible methodological
argument,
\textcite[12--20]{poulsen2012grammaticality} treats corpus distributions,
processing measures, and judgments as indirect evidence for relatively stable
conventional knowledge. The linguistic object, its representation, and the
evidence for it remain separate even when a good theory brings them into alignment.
Section~\ref{sec:norms} now turns from this map of explanatory questions to the
first and most basic one: what constitutes a grammatical norm.


\section{What makes a norm grammatical?}
\label{sec:norms}

As a representational starting point, I use a broad semiotic inventory but
individuate norms by what they govern. \textcite{goldberg1995constructions} and
\textcite{sag2012sign} treat
words, idioms, partly filled patterns, and abstract constructions as conventional
form--meaning pairings. I adopt that representational choice, but it doesn't by
itself classify the relations inside the inventory. Because words and schematic
patterns are constructions on the same continuum, Construction Grammar makes the
present question sharper rather than making it disappear: which relations within
that inventory belong to grammar, and why? The assembly-norm account adds a functional
criterion for that purpose; it doesn't depend on Construction Grammar having
overlooked or denied the grammar--lexicon question.

\subsection{Practice-dependent, response-independent standards}
\label{sec:practice}

The answer has two parts: coordinated linguistic practice explains how a relation
supplies a standard of correctness, while participation in recurrent
constructional assembly explains why that standard is grammatical.

A linguistic norm is more than a frequent event type. It supplies a standard
against which novel or disputed tokens can conform or fail to conform. Low-token-
frequency constructions can be securely licensed, while frequent innovations can
remain restricted or unstable. A norm needn't be explicitly represented as a
rule, prescribed by an institution, morally obligatory, or identically stored by
every speaker. It's socially sustained when production and interpretation
converge on a relation, participants rely on it in forming and understanding new
tokens, and counterfactual substitutions or omissions make systematic differences
within a scoped repertoire.

\textcite{coseriu1952} supplies a precedent. His distinction among
system, norm, and speech separates functional possibilities, customary social
realization, and individual production. Later presentations also distinguish
congruence, idiomatic correctness, and situational appropriateness
\citep{pardollibrer2021,sotovergara2022}. This account inherits the idea
that correctness relative to a historical language and appropriateness in a
situation are different evaluative relations. It adds a question that Coseriu's
distinctions don't settle on their own: which contribution to expression formation
makes the relevant norm grammatical?

The paper explicates public repertoire status for conventional linguistic
classification and descriptive grammar. Other legitimate targets for \enquote{grammaticality}
remain. Psycholinguistic accounts can model individual
competence and innovations not yet sustained more widely. A formal grammar can
model an idiolect or a public variety. In each case, derivability establishes
status relative to that model and target; it doesn't by itself establish public
licensing. Conversely, speakers can
reliably follow a public relation without consciously representing, correcting,
or institutionally enforcing it. Individual, model-relative, and public claims
can inform one another, but none is a proxy for the others
\citep{poulsen2012grammaticality,leivadawestergaard2020}.

The first question, norm constitution, receives a functional answer. A grammatical
norm relates independently supported constructional units, exponents, positions,
or values within a recurrent construction. The constructions involved needn't be
numbered or placed at one size. In subject--verb agreement, the subject noun phrase
supplies a number value and the finite-clause construction provides the verb
position whose form covaries with it. In French article elision, the definite noun
phrase construction relates a selected determiner value to its realization before
a particular class of nominal. In complementation, a head--complement construction
relates a head, a complement function, and the phrase that realizes that function.
These relations differ in generality, but each helps determine how linguistic
material is assembled as an instance of a recurrent construction.

\enquote{Assembly} here names a functional relation, not a claim that speakers
mechanically join a sequence of pre-existing pieces.\footnote{This use of
\emph{assembly} isn't Minimalist \emph{Merge}. Merge is a formal
structure-building operation on syntactic objects \citep{chomsky1995}; assembly
here is a theory-neutral relation among independently supported constructional
units, positions, values, and realizations. It assumes neither binary set
formation nor a derivational procedure.} An assembly relation can hold
simultaneously across a construction, be expressed prosodically or gesturally, or
have no overt exponent. In Construction Grammar terms, it's a relation within or
among constructions; the added claim is that some such relations help determine
grammatical conformity while others establish lexical identity or govern use. The
term says only that the relation helps determine whether a performance conforms
to a recurrent expression type.

Other conventions govern different aspects of the same performance. A lexical
convention establishes that \mention{winter} is a word with a particular content.
English phonotactics constrains the possible phonological form of that word.
Orthography
specifies its written representation. A register convention can make the word or
construction more appropriate in one activity than another. Words, phonological
units in spoken or signed languages, and social meanings can participate in
grammatical assembly, but merely
being conventional or meaningful doesn't make a relation grammatical. The
question is whether the relation organizes how independently identifiable
linguistic resources combine or realize a constructional value.

The English double-copula pattern in \mention{The thing is, is that we left early}
illustrates the difference between recognizability and grammatical analysis. The
sequence is readily understood, and it recurs in conditioned spontaneous-speech
environments \citep{coppockStaum2004double}. Those facts make it a candidate
construction rather than random repetition. Its grammatical status depends on
whether the two copulas occupy independently supported positions in a recurrent
construction, not simply on the fact that speakers understand it or that editors
dislike it. The same reasoning applies to the disputed examples throughout this
paper.

Defective performances require three objects to remain distinct. First comes the
utterance or inscription itself. Second comes its recoverable affiliation
with a construction: listeners can recognize *\olang{le hiver} as an attempted
definite noun phrase. Third comes its conformity or defect relative to each
relation in that construction. The noun, definiteness, gender, and number can be
preserved even when article realization fails. Calling the performance defective
doesn't require denying that it's recognizably an instance of the broader
construction.

This functional distinction has to be identified without using the response it's
later meant to explain. One useful counterfactual holds the intended words,
constructional values, and situation constant while changing the disputed
relation. If the change determines whether the material conforms to the recurrent
construction, the relation contributes to grammatical assembly. If it changes
only phonological form, spelling, lexical fit, or suitability for the occasion,
a different norm is at issue. Recurrent social reliance supplies the proposed
difference between a norm and an accidental one-off pattern.

\textcite{miller2021words} provides a near precedent, though not a complete
argument. He treats word instances as governed by community-relative local
performance standards, sustained by external social mechanisms while internal
mechanisms enable the production of new instances. On Miller's account, the
standards are conditions on conforming word instances, not summaries of observed
frequencies.
Miller leaves their nature open. I draw the further conclusion that stable
clustering alone can't supply normativity: a regular distribution becomes a
standard only when the linguistic practice relies on it in producing and
interpreting further instances.

I call that dependence \term{constitutive reliance}. Appropriately situated
participants are disposed to produce and interpret novel performances through the
relation. Their dispositions are sufficiently coordinated to stabilize new
instances of the same recurrent constructional organization, and counterfactual
substitutions or omissions systematically preserve or disrupt that organization.
Corpus distributions, elicitation, and responses provide evidence about this
pattern of reliance without constituting it.

The reliance matters normatively because participants use the relation not merely
to predict which forms will occur, but to produce and interpret performances as
instances of the same constructional type. A departure can convey its
intended message while failing a practice-internal condition for a conforming
instance. This supplies the thin sense in which the performance is defective
relative to the repertoire.

Correctness is thin and practice-internal. A performance is correct or defective
relative to a repertoire insofar as it conforms or fails to conform to a standard
for constructing or realizing expressions. This needn't amount to moral obligation,
institutional approval, actual correction, majority preference, or statistical
expectedness. Transmission, teaching, correction, and repair can maintain the norm
through time without being part of what sustains it at a given time. A token-level
judgment may misidentify the defect, and the absence of correction doesn't remove
it. An innovation can be surprising while conforming to an existing construction,
whereas a familiar shibboleth can attract condemnation without any assembly
failure. Participation in constructional assembly supplies the assembly-norm
account's
criterion of grammar-specificity: the standard governs relations among signs,
exponents, or values in
recurrent expression formation. It's a functional explication of grammatical
status, not a conclusion forced by recurrence or social coordination alone.

Not every performance has to fit one box. Lexical valence can help determine
whether a complement construction is licensed, and item-specific allomorphy can
realize a grammatical value. Such performances contain more than one relevant
relation. But the analysis can't posit a grammatical relation merely because some
respondents call the problem \enquote{grammar}, or a lexical relation merely because
others call it \enquote{wording}. Sign identity, conventional value, contribution
to construction formation, and repertoire scope have to be identified independently of
those labels.

Table~\ref{tab:norm-types} excludes pure phonotactics from grammar in the target
sense. A pure phonotactic norm constrains the well-formedness of a phonological
form in the relevant modality
independently of any contrast among constructional values. A phonological relation
enters grammatical assembly only when conditioned by, or when realizing, a
grammatical choice. English, for example, doesn't ordinarily permit /ŋ/ at the
beginning of a word; that restriction concerns possible word form regardless of
which grammatical value the word expresses. By contrast, an English pitch contour
can distinguish a statement from a questioning use of the same words
\citep{ladd2008}. In Amele, vowel harmony forms part of the realization of a
different-subject switch-reference value \citep{roberts1987}; in French, elision
forms part of the conditioned realization of the singular article. The sounds are
grammatically relevant in the latter examples because they realize a
constructional choice, not simply because their realizations are phonological.

One performance can contain multiple independently supported relations of
different types. The preposition selected by \mention{depend}, for example, can
participate both in a complement construction and in lexical
knowledge about that head. Japanese polite verb suffixation can organize a
recurrent constructional contrast while also indexing persona and relationship
\citep{cook2008speechstyle,ide2005}. A graphic contrast can enter grammar if
writers conventionally use it to realize a constructional value, even though most
spelling conventions merely represent an expression already identified. These
intersections don't make every linguistic norm grammatical. They show why status
belongs to a particular relation as it operates in a repertoire, not to a sound, word,
or social meaning considered in isolation.

\subsection{Individuating the assembly relation}
\label{sec:individuation}

The preceding subsection said that grammar relates independently supported
constructional units, exponents, positions, or values. \enquote{Independently
supported} doesn't mean already
classified as lexical or grammatical. The point is to avoid inventing a relation
solely because a disputed performance has received a grammar label. The analysis
proceeds in three stages.

First, the proposed parts need independent support. A sign is supported when its
form and contribution recur across combinations. A bound exponent can be
identified by its contrast with other realizations in the same conditioned
position. A construction is supported when a distribution and interpretation
recur under substitution, omission, or embedding. These patterns identify the
possible signs, values, and constructional sites without yet deciding which norm
type governs them.

Second, a relational analysis has to explain more than the stored identity of one
large expression. Its proposed parts should recur or contrast independently, and
varying one should reveal a dependency over an independently motivated position,
value, or class. The analysis should also predict behaviour in substitutions,
derived forms, or constructional environments not used to posit it. Productivity
and structural separability can provide that evidence, but neither is required:
an item-restricted construction can still contain a determinate assembly relation.

A whole-sign analysis is favoured when the supposed parts don't preserve their
contributions outside the expression and the proposed internal relation predicts
nothing beyond an item list. The appropriate unit of analysis is determined by
comparison. Competing
whole-sign and relational analyses are evaluated by how much independently observed
distribution and interpretation they capture without adding a dependency solely
for the disputed case. If the evidence supports both analyses equally, the unit
of analysis is epistemically indeterminate. If different groups or activities
sustain different organizations, the repertoire itself is unsettled at the
declared scope. Neither
result licenses an automatic verdict of \enquote{partly grammatical}.

Third, an analysis containing more than one relation has an additional burden.
Each proposed relation has to be attested outside the response being explained and
make a distinct counterfactual prediction. Inflection inside an idiom, for
example, can be separated from the idiom's lexical identity only where the
inflectional relation recurs elsewhere and predicts changes within the idiom.
Respondents dividing between \enquote{grammar} and \enquote{wording} isn't enough
to establish two relations.

Simple contrasts show what the three stages are meant to distinguish. In
*\mention{These books is useful}*, the words and proposition are recoverable, but
the number relation between subject and finite verb fails. In \mention{strong
rain}, ordinary adjective--noun modification succeeds, but the lexical
combination is unusual compared with \mention{heavy rain}. A novel word beginning
with /ŋ/ can conflict with ordinary English word shape without disrupting a
constructional value. And a Japanese plain-style verb addressed by a young
student to a professor
can be linguistically well formed while being unsuitable for the relationship.
The four performances may all attract negative reactions, but the independently
identified relation at issue differs.

English complementation and collocation provide a harder contrast. In
\mention{depend on an answer}, the meaning of \mention{depend} introduces a
semantic dependency relation. In the clause, a PP realizes the complement function
within the VP, and \mention{on} heads that PP. The verb, the complement function,
the PP realization, and possible complements of the preposition recur
independently. Related forms such as \mention{dependence on} and
\mention{dependent on} preserve the head-specific preposition. This supports a
set of dependencies connecting semantic relation, syntactic complement function,
phrasal category, and an item-restricted choice of preposition.

Not every recurring part of that dependency set is thereby grammatical. Collocations and
morphological families also recur across forms. The relevant evidence includes
preservation of the semantic relation across different complements,
participation in complement alternations, recurrence across derived forms,
contrasts with non-complement uses, and whether the preposition realizes a
constructional relation rather than merely accompanying a lexical family. The
semantic-to-syntactic and complement-to-PP dependencies clearly contribute to
grammatical assembly. On the
evidence considered here, the final choice of \mention{on} remains indeterminate
between lexically indexed grammatical licensing and lexical collocation~-- in more
traditional terms, between grammar and lexis.

The comparison with \mention{heavy rain} clarifies the distinction. Replacing
\mention{heavy} with \mention{strong} can leave the adjective--noun construction
grammatical while producing a lexically odd combination. If \mention{depend on}
turned out to be stored only as a larger lexical construction whose proposed
parts made no independent predictions, the item-specific grammatical analysis
would likewise lose support; the final preposition restriction would then be
lexical rather than grammatical. The opposite result is also possible. If
\mention{heavy} developed into a conventional intensity exponent
over an independently supported class of nouns, its selection would become part
of grammatical assembly. These conclusions are fixed from distribution and interpretation
before corrections or prior grammaticality verdicts are examined.

\subsection{Paradigms, constructional dependencies, and discursive status}

Strict paradigms provide a strong case of grammatical assembly.
\textcite{norgardsorensenheltoft2015} characterize a grammatical paradigm through
a recurring domain, a language-specific semantic frame, a bounded set of
alternatives, obligatory choice in the domain, and marked--unmarked organization.
Their account extends beyond inflection to word order and constructional
syntax. \textcite{hansenheltoft2011gds} likewise allow prototype and radial
organization within their account of Danish grammar. These analyses show how a
set of values and their realizations can be organized within a repertoire without reducing
grammar to segmental morphology.

Paradigmhood doesn't define all grammar under the assembly-norm proposal.
Constructional dependencies can be productive without a closed set of values,
and item-specific relations can depend on more schematic licensing relations. The
assembly-norm account places these relations by asking what they contribute to
the formation of constructional tokens. Bounded alternatives, obligatoriness,
and markedness are powerful ways of organizing grammatical assembly, not universal
conditions on it.

\textcite{boyeharder2012} offer a different answer to norm constitution. On their
view, a grammatical expression is conventionally ancillary: it supports another
expression rather than carrying the main point of the utterance. Grammatical
meaning is \term{discursively secondary}, whereas lexical meaning is
capable of becoming primary. Closed paradigms and obligatoriness may correlate
with that status, but they don't define it.

Boye and Harder diagnose discourse status through focusability and addressability.
In \mention{Jones is gonna call her tomorrow}, for example, a reply such as
\mention{How?} can ask how Jones will call, but it can't independently question
the meaning expressed by \mention{gonna}. Nor does ordinary sentence stress make
\mention{gonna} the principal point. A lexical verb such as \mention{call} is more
readily focused or questioned. These contrasts support their analysis because
they show what can and can't become discourse-primary.

The assembly-norm proposal agrees that substance and frequency don't define
grammar, but it treats discourse status differently. Boye and Harder's
classification of English demonstratives makes the difference concrete.
\textcite[20--21]{boyeharder2012} classify free-standing \mention{that} as lexical
because it can be selectively focused and addressed, unlike grammatical
\mention{it}. The contrast concerns ordinary selective focus, not the mere
possibility of prosodic prominence. Boye and Harder exclude explicitly
contrastive and metalinguistic contexts from this diagnostic, so \mention{I want
IT, not THEM} isn't a counterexample on their terms. Their claim is that
\mention{that} can make its own contribution discourse-primary in ordinary use,
whereas \mention{it} conventionally can't.

Accepting that discourse diagnosis doesn't settle the status of every relation in
which the demonstrative participates. English \mention{this}, \mention{these},
\mention{that}, and \mention{those} form a system crossing proximal--distal deixis
with singular--plural number, and demonstrative determinatives function as
determiner or fused determiner--head (standing alone as the nominal head) in
nominal constructions \citep{huddleston2002}. Under the assembly criterion, those
deictic--number and nominal-construction relations are grammatical even if the
demonstrative expression is lexical by Boye and Harder's criterion. The two
accounts don't simply assign rival labels to one object: they also disagree about
which object should bear grammatical status.

The difference in object matters beyond demonstratives. Boye and Harder assign
lexical or grammatical status to expressions and meanings. The assembly-norm account
assigns grammatical status to a relation in which an expression participates. It
can accept their observation that \mention{that} can carry the principal point of
a performance while denying that this prominence makes every relation in which
the demonstrative participates lexical. Conversely, an ancillary parenthetical or
discourse marker isn't grammatical merely because it normally remains in the
background; it has to participate in an independently supported assembly
relation.

This disagreement about the proper object of classification doesn't make Boye and
Harder's diagnostics irrelevant. Conventional discourse-secondary status may
explain attention, processing, and the behaviour of many familiar grammatical
expressions. The constitutive question is whether it can be the sole identity
condition for grammar once a discourse-primary expression participates in
independently established constructional relations. The comparison has to examine
both kinds of evidence without defining either property from the status verdicts
to be explained.

\subsection{Multiple relations and epistemic profiles}
\label{sec:profiles}

One performance can have different statuses relative to different relations
without making evidence, reality, and judgment equally fuzzy. The relations can
have different functions~-- for example, a grammatical complement relation
together with a lexically restricted preposition~-- and different activities can
sustain different organizations. Item restriction, low productivity, and narrow
distribution don't by themselves make an assembly relation less grammatical. A
lexeme-specific relation can determinately belong to grammar if it determinately
realizes a grammatical value. Such properties weaken generality or evidential
support only when they also leave the contribution to expression formation
uncertain or the relation
unsettled across the declared repertoire.

Evidence and judgments vary separately. The analyst may have strong or weak
evidence that a relation exists, and a respondent may apply a categorical or
hesitant label. A sharp schoolroom correction doesn't prove a sharply grammatical
relation, just as uncertain corpus evidence doesn't prove a fuzzy norm.

An \term{assembly profile} is the analyst's estimate of norm organization.
It records evidence about whether form and value are coupled at a constructional
site, how alternatives and selection are organized, and where the relation is
licensed in the declared repertoire. Production, interpretation, substitution,
omission, productivity, and opportunity-normalized distribution bear on those
questions. A descriptive grammar contributes through the patterns it documents,
not by lending the analysis its prior category label.

\textcite{pullum2017}, adapting Goodman's method of reflective equilibrium,
argues that syntactic inquiry should seek coherence among theoretical principles,
corpus evidence, and considered judgments rather than grant any one source
absolute authority \citep{Goodman1955}. The profile is compatible with that
iterative method, but a held-out test imposes a temporary evidential partition:
the particular judgments or repairs to be predicted can't also fix the initial
profile. After the test, both the profile and the analysis remain revisable in
light of the full evidence.


\section{How tokens fail linguistic norms}
\label{sec:failure}

With the norm-constitution and individuation arguments from
Sections~\ref{sec:practice} and~\ref{sec:individuation} in place, token
application, the second of the six questions, begins only after a candidate
norm and repertoire have been identified. It asks which relation, if any, a
particular performance fails. The asterisk in *\mention{These books is useful}
doesn't answer that question. It tells us that someone has classified the example
negatively; the analyst still has to locate the conflict between plural subject and
singular finite verb.

An analyst can make a prior epistemic error by applying a norm from the wrong
community, activity, medium, or period. That mistake concerns repertoire scope;
it isn't a defect in the performance. Once the repertoire is specified,
Table~\ref{tab:token-failures} lists possible sites of token failure. It's a
diagnostic menu, not a sequence through which every performance passes. The
entries also
separate two senses of \enquote{form error}. A nonce word can violate ordinary
English phonotactics, in which case phonological form is at issue. Or a repertoire can
exclude one phonological form as the realization of a grammatical value, as when
*\mention{goed} uses the productive past exponent where the lexeme-specific
realization is \mention{went}. In the second example, phonological material is the
surface of a grammatical realization fault.

Value errors require particular care. Three cases have to remain separate.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An \term{internal value incompatibility} arises when linguistic material
    in the token supplies values that conflict: agreement features disagree, a
    temporal construction conflicts with an overt temporal specification, or a
    switch-reference marker conflicts with the subjects supplied by its clause
    chain. *\mention{These books is useful} is straightforward: plural
    \mention{these books} and singular \mention{is} supply incompatible number
    instructions. This can be a grammatical failure.
  \item In \term{external value misapplication}, a licensed grammatical value is
    successfully expressed but is false, misleading, or inappropriate for the
    described situation. A yesterday-past form used for an event that actually
    happened today needn't be ungrammatical. The grammatical tense choice may be
    false of the situation without conflicting with any other linguistic material.
  \item In a \term{message-selection error}, a speaker chooses a grammatical
    competitor that doesn't express the intended message. The production is
    mistaken, but the resulting expression can remain grammatical: saying
    \mention{She left} when one intended \mention{He left} doesn't make the
    produced sentence inherently grammatically defective.
\end{enumerate}

Of these three value cases, only internal incompatibility establishes a
grammatical fault by itself. The distinction
follows from the constitutive account: grammaticality concerns conformity to the
relations that assemble the linguistic token, not direct correspondence between
that token and the world or the speaker's private intention. Semantic and pragmatic
theories are needed to explain those further relations. This conclusion concerns
token status. What a respondent notices and why they react remain the separate
questions of target attribution and response causation.

Selecting an inapposite but licensed word supplies the lexical analogue. The sign keeps
its conventional identity and meaning, and the surrounding construction can be
well formed; the selected content is simply inapt. A register mismatch can
likewise preserve the proposition and the form's conventional social-indexical
value while deploying that value in an unsuitable situation. These choices may be
serious, accountable, or sharply sanctioned. Their social importance doesn't
move the failed relation into grammatical assembly.

Section~\ref{sec:semantics} turns from the location of these faults to the third
question: what such conformity or failure does to meaning and interaction.


\section{What semantic and pragmatic explanations explain}
\label{sec:semantics}

The third question concerns semantic and pragmatic consequence: what happens to
meaning and interaction when a performance conforms to or fails a linguistic
relation? This question occupies the third level in Table~\ref{tab:levels}, and its answer can't be read
off from the performance's grammatical-status classification. Semantic and
pragmatic theory enter here in two ways. First, they help
describe the content organized by grammar. Tense, definiteness, evidential source,
clause type, reference continuity, and commitment are examples rather than an
exhaustive list. The range matters because a grammatical relation can organize a
temporal or referential value, a clause's communicative type, or the continuity of
participants across clauses; grammatical content isn't limited in advance to one
semantic level. Second, semantic and pragmatic theories explain the consequences
of combining those values:
an attempted interpretation may conflict, remain underspecified, invite coercion,
or alter the course of a conversation. The first contribution helps specify what
a norm organizes; the second explains what conformity or failure does.

\subsection{Semantic repairability}
\label{sec:repairability}

\textcite[329--331, 344--348]{abrusan2019} asks a close relative of the present
question: why can a failed interpretation feel ungrammatical in one case,
semantically anomalous in another, and pragmatically infelicitous in a third? A
\term{negative-polarity item} such as \mention{anything}, which ordinarily requires
negation or another licensing environment, is often classified as ungrammatical
in *\mention{John saw anything} even though the proposed licensing condition is
semantic. By
contrast, \mention{My toothbrush is pregnant} is readily understood as a
selectional absurdity rather than as a syntactic failure. The contrast shows why
locating a source in semantics doesn't yet predict how speakers will classify the
fault.

\textcite{abrusanetal2021shift} explain an important part of that contrast through
type level, locality, and semantic shift. A local conflict involving a relatively
specific type can sometimes be repaired by adjusting a word's interpretation in
context. The adjective in \mention{heavy traffic}, for example, doesn't retain
exactly its ordinary physical-weight interpretation. But conflicts involving
high-level types such as question formation or determiner logic may leave no
nearby meaning-preserving shift. Because the type system and permitted
shifts are motivated independently of the eventual anomaly label, Abrus\'an and
colleagues explain rather than merely rename a classification distinction.

That semantic explanation has a defined explanatory domain. It can explain
responses where
composition or interpretation supplies the trouble. But it doesn't, by itself,
decide whether French article non-elision, a suppletive past form, a pure
phonotactic violation, or a spelling error belongs to grammar. Those examples
remain easy to understand because semantic composition has largely succeeded.
The assembly-norm proposal and the repairability account overlap when grammar
organizes semantic composition, but they answer different questions.

This difference also yields distinct failure conditions. If type level and
shiftability fully explain why respondents classify a semantic conflict as
ungrammatical rather than anomalous, an additional assembly-based explanation of
that response is unnecessary. But Abrus\'an's resources challenge the present
answer to norm constitution only if they also explain what makes the failed
linguistic relation grammatical. Explaining a response and constituting the norm
remain separate tasks.

This means that semantics is neither outside grammar nor identical to it. A tense
construction can belong to grammar partly because it conventionally assembles a
temporal value, and semantic theory explains what that value is. If two
linguistically encoded temporal instructions conflict, semantic theory may also
explain the resulting anomaly. But a further premise is still needed to show
whether the convention that assembled those instructions belongs to grammar,
lexical semantics, or a pragmatic practice.

\subsection{Discourse consequences without a unitary public state}
\label{sec:discourse}

Some grammatical choices do more than contribute to propositional content (the
situation an utterance depicts). They also help determine what conversational
move the speaker has made and what an interlocutor is now entitled, expected, or
able to do. An interrogative construction typically presents an issue for response, whereas
a declarative construction ordinarily presents a claim. An evidential marker can
present the speaker or someone else as the source of the information. A
switch-reference marker can indicate whether the next clause
continues with the same participant or shifts to another. I call these
\term{discourse consequences}: effects on the public organization of interaction,
not merely on the situation described.

Several traditions converge on this terrain from different directions.
\textcite{heltoft1981intersubjectivity} separates grammaticality from
comprehensibility in his treatment of epistemic verbs: a form can remain
understandable even when it fails a grammatical relation, and the grammatical
distinction can concern how the speaker positions a claim.
\textcite{heltoft2005modality} distinguishes the illocutionary role of mood (the
kind of communicative act performed) from the interlocutionary role of dialogic
particles, which helps organize the relation between one turn and the next.
\textcite{hansenheltoft2011gds} and \textcite{jakobsenheltoft2012paradigm}
develop the connection between paradigmatic organization and discourse function.
Interactional research starts from the organization of conversation and treats
grammar as a resource for constructing sequences of action and locating and
repairing trouble
\citep{foxetal1996repair,muntiglventola2010,dingemanseblythedirksmeyer2014}.

These traditions support the claim that grammatical organization can have public
consequences. They don't support the converse claim that anything with a public
consequence is grammatical. \textcite{ameka1992} analyzes interjections as a
distinct class of conventional expressions with their own semantic and pragmatic
properties. An item such as \mention{oh} can redirect the next turn, but that
interactional effect alone doesn't show that it participates in a grammatical
paradigm.
Conversely, a grammatical distinction can make little difference in a particular
performance. In \mention{These books are useful}, plurality is already expressed
by \mention{these} and \mention{books}, so the agreement realized by
\mention{are} adds little locally. But the agreement paradigm may matter
for participant or referent tracking in less redundant environments. And so
discourse consequence and grammatical status have to be identified independently.

Representing all discourse consequences as movement
along one scale, as though every grammatical choice simply changed more or less
of a single public information store, would also be misleading.
\textcite{bary2025speechacts} argues that at least three things need to be
separated: what interlocutors jointly treat as settled, which participant is
publicly committed to a proposition, and what institutional or performative
change an utterance itself brings about. \textcite{simons2025availability} presses
the separation further. Information can be available and appropriate for
interpretation without being mutually accepted as common ground, and the
information used to interpret a speech act needn't be identical to the information
that the act changes.

A grammatical choice can have several potentially independent discourse
effects. A \term{discourse-consequence profile} is the analyst's estimate of those
effects, not an additional property that constitutes grammatical status. Rather
than assigning a paradigm one number, the profile records evidence bearing on:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \term{Move type}: whether the utterance functions as an assertion,
  question, request, or some other move.
  \item \term{Commitment holder or source}: whether the speaker, another person,
  or no fully committed source is presented as responsible for the information.
  \item \term{Participant or referent continuity}: whether subsequent material is
  interpreted as continuing with the same person or shifting to another.
  \item \term{Response relevance}: which kinds of reply the utterance makes
  conditionally appropriate.
\end{itemize}
A paradigm may have a strong effect on one dimension and almost none on another.
The multidimensional discourse-consequence profile assumes neither a unitary
public state nor universal weights among these dimensions.

The discourse-consequence profile applies only to a grammatical choice already
identified on independent grounds. It isn't part of the definition of grammar.
Clause type, evidential anchoring, and switch-reference are plausible
high-consequence examples because changing their values can alter the move, source,
or participant carried forward. Ordinary allomorphy is usually a low-consequence
example. Replacing \mention{went} with *\mention{goed} violates a realization
relation but ordinarily leaves the intended past value and conversational move
intact. Interjections make the converse point: a resource can reshape uptake
without thereby forming part of grammatical assembly.

This separation permits a more specific repair prediction. Consider an
independently identified switch-reference paradigm whose values distinguish
continuation by the same subject from continuation by a different subject. Now
compare two failures. In the first, a same-subject value occurs in a clause chain
whose clauses specify different subjects. The performance supplies
incompatible grammatical instructions: one part indicates continuity, while
another indicates a change of participant. A hearer who detects the conflict may
need to clarify who performs the second action. In the second failure, the intended
different-subject value remains recoverable but its exponent has an excluded
phonological realization. The participant shift is already clear, so correcting
the form can leave the interpretation and discourse organization unchanged.

The prediction isn't that every value conflict provokes clarification or that
every realization failure receives an overt correction. Speakers can accommodate,
ignore, mishear, or respond to some other aspect of the utterance. The prediction
concerns the \term{locus of repair}: what a response attempts to settle. Internal
value incompatibilities should more often direct repair toward force, commitment,
participant role, or reference. Value-preserving realization failures should more
often direct repair toward form while preserving the intended discourse effect.
Across independently identified paradigms, this difference should grow with the
independently estimated discourse effects. The hypothesis concerns a consequence
of grammatical status: it predicts what different grammatical failures may do
rather than deciding what counts as grammatical.

The relevant information-theoretic intuition is that bounded alternatives create
redundancy and can make an intended value recoverable even when its exponent is
malformed. This intuition belongs at the consequence level, not the definition of
grammar. Suppose a paradigm contains
only same-subject and different-subject values. Even when the exponent is
malformed, the surrounding clause chain and the limited competitor set may make
the intended value recoverable. Entropy or divergence can represent how much
uncertainty about interpretation or response changes when one value is selected
rather than another \citep{shannon1948,coverthomas2006}.

But these measures apply only after linguistic analysis has identified the
alternatives and their conventional functions. An information-theoretic
calculation can quantify
how strongly same-subject and different-subject values constrain a response
space. It can't discover that these are the relevant alternatives, determine
whether they form a grammatical paradigm, establish that a performance fails a
form--value relation, or explain why that failure is accountable within a
community. The linguistic codebook is an input to the calculation, not its output.

The two failure conditions belong to different levels. If outcome-blind
distributional evidence supports only a general phonotactic relation, the
constitutive grammatical analysis loses. If the assembly relation remains
independently supported but respondents target phonology, the attribution
prediction loses or needs qualification. Evidence for both relations warrants a
multiple-relation analysis only when each relation is independently supported.

\subsection{Inflection, complementation, and lexical knowledge}
\label{sec:lexical-cases}

*\mention{Goed} conforms to the productive past-tense schema with respect to one
relation: past
is realized by \mention{-ed}. A more specific, lexeme-indexed relation realizes
\textsc{go.past} as \mention{went} and pre-empts the general realization for this
lexeme in the scoped repertoire. The form preserves past and satisfies
the productive schema while failing the more specific realization relation. The
two-relation analysis is supported independently by the productive schema and the
stable lexeme-specific opposition. The exponent isn't intrinsically excluded;
it's excluded for this lexeme.

*\mention{Depend of} falls under the dependencies developed in
Section~\ref{sec:individuation}. A complement construction introduces an argument
relation; that relation constrains a phrasal category; and the particular head
restricts the preposition. The more schematic dependencies independently
contribute to grammatical assembly. The present evidence doesn't settle whether the final
\mention{depend}--\mention{on} restriction is lexically indexed licensing or
collocation. Mixed attribution is informative only when compared with those
independently specified dependencies and their stated indeterminacy.

The lexicon more generally contains conventional form--meaning pairings. Choosing
an ill-fitting but licensed word normally preserves both the word's identity and
the larger construction. Calling a downpour \mention{strong rain}, for example,
doesn't by itself show that English adjective--noun modification has failed. The
combination is unusual because English convention favours a different lexical
collocation.

Selectional restrictions and valence frames are harder because lexical content
can constrain how a construction is assembled. Abrus\'an's type and shift
diagnostics help determine whether a semantic conflict is doing the work;
constructional recurrence and substitution show whether a conventional assembly
relation is also present. This evidence bears directly on grammaticality. If a
head-specific restriction helps realize an independently supported complement or
valence construction, its failure is grammatical because it concerns that
assembly relation. If
the restriction amounts only to an association between stored lexical items, the
failure is lexical. For \mention{depend on}, the argument-role and PP-category
dependencies have clearer constructional support than the final choice of
\mention{on}. On the evidence considered here, that item-specific relation remains
indeterminate between lexically indexed grammatical licensing and collocation.

\subsection{Deployment, representation, and grammaticalization}
\label{sec:deployment-cases}

Register, accent, and politeness marking, as in Japanese polite/plain-style verb
suffixation, can be highly systematic and socially consequential
\citep{agha2007,eckert2012,cook2008speechstyle,ide2005}.
A mismatch may preserve the form's conventional social value
perfectly and fail only because that value is unsuitable for the occasion. In that
case, assembly has succeeded and deployment is the fault. An honorific or style
contrast participates in grammatical assembly when it functions within a
repertoire as a recurrent constructional choice with a domain, alternatives, and
conditioned selection. The account doesn't exclude pragmatic or social content from grammar
in advance.

Orthography normally maps graphic forms to linguistic expressions. Consider a
writer who intends \mention{They're leaving now} but writes
*\mention{Their leaving now}. The spellings \mention{their} and
\mention{they're} distinguish a possessive form from the contraction of
\mention{they are}, even though they're homophonous for many speakers. A reader
may recover the intended clause immediately. On a purely representational
analysis, the writer has selected the wrong graphic form for an otherwise
recoverable linguistic expression; the fault is orthographic. But the spelling
also makes a different syntactic analysis available: \mention{their leaving}
can begin a gerund-participial clause with a genitive subject
\citep{huddleston2002}, so the written token may contain a secondary
constructional conflict. As a result, the analysis should identify the
graphic mapping and the clause structure separately rather than infer one fault
type from the fact that the sentence is written. More generally, a graphic
contrast is grammatical when it realizes a constructional value, but merely
orthographic when it represents an expression already identified. The medium
doesn't determine the norm type; the relation's contribution to expression
formation does.

These possibilities are also diachronic. Grammaticalization can recruit lexical or
pragmatic material into constructional and paradigmatic organization
\citep{hoppertraugott2003,lehmann2015grammaticalization,waltereit2011discourse,degandeversvermeul2015,jakobsenheltoft2012paradigm}.
Historical origin doesn't fix present status. The relevant question is how the
relation functions in the scoped repertoire now.

\subsection{A discourse-consequence application}
\label{sec:discourse-application}

Amele switch-reference illustrates the narrower distinction between an internal
value conflict and a value-preserving realization failure. \textcite[239--240,
298--300, 371]{roberts1987} describes a sequential paradigm contrasting
same-subject (SS) and different-subject (DS) linkage on medial verbs, with
person--number marking and vowel harmony in the DS form. Example~\ref{ex:amele-ds}
is Roberts's attested different-subject clause chain.

\ea\label{ex:amele-ds}
\gll \mention{Hina} \mention{ho-co-m} \mention{sab} \mention{je-i-a.} \\
     2\abbr{sg} come-\abbr{ds}-2\abbr{sg} food eat-3\abbr{sg}-\abbr{tod.pst} \\
\glt \textquoteleft You came and he ate the food.\textquoteright
\z

The medial verb \olang{ho-co-m} marks the subject of \enquote{come} as
second-person singular and signals that the next clause has a different subject.
The final verb \olang{je-i-a} supplies the third-person subject and today's-past
tense. Thus the DS form and the person marking converge on a change of
participant; the translation alone isn't being used to infer the paradigm.
Table~\ref{tab:amele-sr} starts from this attested conforming form and gives two
deliberately constructed candidates that would have to be validated before use
in a study.

The internal-conflict candidate constitutes a grammatical failure only if the
subject information is supplied by the clause chain and the SS value conflicts
with it. A speaker who
merely intends a different continuation after producing a
well-formed SS chain has made a message-selection error instead. The realization
condition preserves DS but violates the harmony relation described for its form.

Because SS and DS choice is sensitive to agentivity, clause level, time, place,
world, and pragmatic deixis, those factors can't be inferred from subject identity
alone. The published grammar also doesn't print the exact disharmonic stimulus.
The comparison is a source-grounded template that requires validation, not an
empirical result.

If the internal conflict is established, the two failures yield different
discourse predictions. An SS/DS conflict can unsettle reference continuity; a
disharmonic realization need not. Whether participants direct repair to reference
or form remains a separate causal and empirical question. The ontology permits
this cross-level inference; it isn't evidence used to
define the switch-reference relation as grammatical.


\section{Methodological consequences and failure conditions}
\label{sec:method}

Many studies begin with a judgment (a low rating, an asterisk, or a correction)
and then ask what caused it. This section returns to the three response and
evidence questions distinguished in Section~\ref{sec:levels}: target attribution,
response causation, and epistemic justification. Judgment evidence is useful, but
by itself it bundles three
of the questions distinguished above. The response has a target, such as article
formation or pronunciation; it has causes, such as linguistic knowledge,
processing, schooling, or stigma; and it gives the analyst fallible evidence
about the norm and the token. If an analyst uses the same response both to infer
the norm and to confirm that speakers identify it, the argument is circular. The
account requires a different evidential order.

A worked investigation would proceed in six steps. First, declare the relevant
community, activity, medium, and period. That scope can be refined from
outcome-blind evidence about interaction, exposure, production, and
interpretation.

Second, identify the proposed relation. State the constructional units, exponents, values, and
constructional site, using substitution, omission, productivity, contrast
organization, and distribution rather than prior grammaticality verdicts.

Third, state the closest alternative analysis. For French elision, the relevant
rivals are a general phonotactic relation and a graphic convention. The analysis
has to say what outcome-blind evidence would favour each account.

Fourth, freeze the analysis before examining the target judgments or repairs.
This includes the repertoire, the candidate relation, the uncertainty in its
assembly profile, and the conditions that would defeat it.

Fifth, collect response evidence from new tokens or a disjoint corpus partition.
Open descriptions and spontaneous corrections should precede forced choices
among \enquote{grammar}, \enquote{wording}, \enquote{style}, and related labels.

Finally, explain the responses. Code what each response targets separately from
what probably caused it. Schoolroom terminology, processing difficulty, stigma,
and task demands can shape a response without determining the token's grammatical
status.

The order doesn't make judgments irrelevant. It makes clear what they can show.
A spontaneous replacement of \olang{le} with \olang{l'} may indicate that a
respondent has located the trouble in article realization. An explanation such as
\enquote{two vowels can't occur together} may instead target phonology. Neither
response, by itself, decides which worldly relation is present. That question was
addressed in the outcome-blind stages; the response provides evidence about
whether the respondent identifies the proposed relation and how they describe it.
A construction-directed response
isn't automatically grammar-directed because lexical constructions are part of
the distinction at issue. Mixed and unresolved responses remain visible.

The comparison set has to challenge the constitutive distinction rather than
merely contrast easy grammatical examples with unrelated controls. It should
include strict paradigms, productive constructions, value-preserving realization
failures, item-specific licensing dependencies, pure phonotactic and orthographic
departures, inapposite but licensed lexical choices, social-indexical mismatches, and
violations of social acts. The items should remain interpretable, since the
question is why recoverable performances receive different fault
classifications. If the assembly analysis is adjusted after the responses are
known, the test has collapsed back into an account of labeling.

Rival explanations operate at different levels. Semantic repairability may explain
classifications where composition fails. Boye and Harder's discursive secondary
status may better identify grammatical norms. Frequency, surprisal, perceptual
salience, and processing can cause low ratings or rapid correction. Schooling,
stigma, participant role, and institutional authority can cause grammar-directed
labels or person-directed sanction. Activity, medium, register, community, and
period define the population of the grammatical-status claim rather than serving
as a bag of statistical controls. A good design restricts or matches these causes where possible and states
which remaining comparison is identified.

Three empirical consequences then become available. First, relations independently
identified as grammatical assembly norms should more consistently prompt speakers
to identify expression formation as the problem than
matched lexical, phonotactic, representational, deployment, and social-act faults.
Explicit grammar labels should correlate with that more specific fault
attribution, but the correlation is a further response-level prediction and may
be weakened by schooling or local metalinguistic practice.

Second, performances with independently supported relations of different types,
and relations changing over time, should produce different or changing
classifications among grammar, lexicon, phonology, and style. Third, among
paradigms that independently support both error types, a stronger
discourse-consequence profile should increase the difference in discourse-directed
repair between internal value conflicts and value-preserving realization failures.

These predictions are evidential consequences, not definitions. The constitutive
account fails if its functional criteria can't distinguish phonotactics, lexical identity,
representation, deployment, and mixed constructional cases without relying on the
responses to be explained. Its empirical reach fails if independently estimated
assembly organization adds no stable held-out expectation about fault attribution
or classification change beyond semantic repairability, discursive secondary status,
processing, schooling, and sanction. The discourse application fails if the
independently estimated consequence profile doesn't predict the repair-locus
difference in an identifying comparison.

Some outcomes are inconclusive rather than favourable or fatal. A corpus of
corrections contains only occasions on which someone corrected, so it can't by
itself estimate how a community classifies all comparable tokens. Naturally
occurring internal value conflicts and realization failures may also be too sparse
or uneven for the repair comparison. Such data can test search procedures, coding,
and candidate analyses without being promoted into a confirmatory result. The
account earns support only from comparisons whose norm evidence and outcome
evidence are separable in practice.


\section{Conclusion}

Why does one interpretable deviation count as ungrammatical while another does
not? My answer can now be stated in the order of its six questions.

First, as Section~\ref{sec:practice} argued, coordinated linguistic practice supplies a thin standard of correctness
within a scoped repertoire. This answers the question of \term{norm
constitution}. The standard belongs to grammar when it relates independently
supported constructional units, exponents, positions, or values in the recurrent assembly of
constructions. Lexical conventions, phonotactics, orthography, and usage norms are
equally conventional, but they ordinarily establish sign identity, constrain
phonological form, represent an expression, or govern its deployment.

Second, Section~\ref{sec:failure}'s question of \term{token application} asks whether a performance conforms to the
assembly standard. A performance can be defective with respect to one relation
while remaining recognizable as an attempted instance because other relations are
preserved. *\olang{Le hiver}, for example, preserves definiteness, number, gender,
and reference while failing the proposed article-realization relation.
Recoverability doesn't erase the fault, and failure of one relation doesn't
prevent the performance from being identified through the relations it preserves.

Third, Section~\ref{sec:semantics} showed why the \term{semantic or pragmatic consequence} of a grammatical fault
requires its own explanation. A failure may block composition, invite a semantic
repair, leave the proposition intact, or unsettle move type, commitment,
participant continuity, or response relevance. Abrus\'an's account explains an
important subset of anomaly classifications, and discourse theory explains why
some grammatical values reorganize interaction. Neither type of explanation
alone determines whether the failed relation belongs to grammar.

Fourth, the \term{target attribution} tested by the procedure in
Section~\ref{sec:method} concerns whether a respondent treats the trouble
as article formation, pronunciation, spelling, wording, style, or conduct. That
attribution may be mistaken.

Fifth, \term{response causation} asks why the respondent reacted. Processing
difficulty, frequency, perceptual salience, schooling, stigma, participant role,
and institutional authority can produce rejection or correction without
determining grammatical status. They can also prevent a speaker from noticing a
genuine grammatical fault.

Finally, \term{epistemic justification} requires the proposed relation, its
repertoire, and its closest rival to be individuated from outcome-blind production,
interpretation, substitution, omission, and distribution before held-out judgments
and repairs test whether speakers track it.

Norm types aren't assigned by expressive substance: phonological material can
realize grammar, pragmatic values can enter paradigms, and item-specific knowledge
can participate in constructional dependencies. Multiple-relation analysis is
justified only when each proposed relation recurs or contrasts independently and
predicts further cases; otherwise, the unit of analysis remains indeterminate.

The motivating French case has a deliberately conditional purpose. *\olang{Le
hiver} counts as a grammatical-form error only if the conditioned realization of
the article, rather than pure phonotactics or graphic convention, is what fails in
the scoped repertoire. The theory can be wrong about that case without redefining
grammar to save it. More generally, if the assembly criterion can't distinguish the
difficult cases without recycling their labels, the constitutive answer
fails and the narrower theory of metalinguistic attribution is all that remains.
