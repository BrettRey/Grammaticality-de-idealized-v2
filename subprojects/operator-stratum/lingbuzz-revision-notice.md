# Revision notice for _Why clause structure is judged like tense and agreement_

## LingBuzz/009706

In January 2026, I posted _Why clause structure is judged like tense and
agreement: a coordination account of grammaticality_ at LingBuzz/009706. The
paper raised a question I still think is genuine, but it overstated both the
novelty of its theoretical ingredients and the evidence for its answer. This
notice corrects the record.

The correction is not that Lars Heltoft and his collaborators had already
published the operator-stratum proposal under another name. Closer reading does
not support that claim. Their work does, however, establish much more of the
background than the January paper acknowledged. The remaining proposal is
therefore narrower, cumulative, and untested.

## The question

Words are conventional pairings of form and meaning, just as grammatical forms
are. Yet choosing the wrong word does not ordinarily make the construction that
contains it ungrammatical. By contrast, a wrong agreement value, clause pattern,
or obligatory marker may be rejected as ungrammatical even when the intended
message is recoverable. Accent and register mismatches are often criticized in
still other terms. The January paper asked what, if anything, explains that
selectivity across different expressive materials.

Its proposed answer combined two properties. First, the relevant alternatives
belong to a restricted conventional system: for example, affirmative versus
negative, declarative versus interrogative, or same-subject versus
different-subject marking. Second, choosing among them helps determine how an
utterance is to be taken up: what kind of move it makes, who is committed to
what, or how participants and referents are linked. I called the resulting
intersection the _operator stratum_. I then suggested that errors in this region
would have characteristic consequences for judgment, comprehension, repair,
processing, and preemption.

The paper reported no experiment or corpus study testing those consequences.

## What was already established

Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft (2015) provide the closest structural predecessor.
They define grammatical paradigms through a bounded set of alternatives, a
recurring domain, a language-specific semantic frame, obligatory value
selection, and markedness. Crucially, they extend paradigmatic organization
beyond inflection to word order, constructional syntax, and prosody. Their
analysis therefore already supplies a principled reason for treating some
clause patterns and morphological categories alike. The January paper should
not have presented that grouping as new.

Heltoft's broader programme also makes the interactional side much less novel
than the paper suggested. _Grammatik over det Danske Sprog_ treats grammar as a
system of conventional expression–content relations whose content can be
pragmatic or textual (Hansen and Heltoft 2011). Its analyses include word-order
patterns that frame an utterance as declarative or interrogative, mood contrasts
that constrain illocutionary interpretation, and dialogic particles that encode
assumptions about another participant's position or expected response. It also
distinguishes metacommunicative trouble concerning illocution, propositional or
lexical meaning, relevance, truth, sincerity, and social correctness. Jakobsen
and Heltoft (2012) likewise analyze mood paradigms whose values concern
directive force, commitment to represented reality, and responsibility for
reported speech. These are not merely disconnected observations that grammar
has meaning and conversation has structure. They are concrete cases in which
paradigmatically organized grammatical signs help organize public interaction.

Heltoft's 1981 study of epistemic verbs and focus and his 2005 study of modality
and dialogic particles are important antecedents to this programme. They show
how conventional form can locate viewpoint, responsibility, or a potential
dispute. They do not, however, explain why grammatical errors are categorized
differently from accent, register, or open lexical choice.

Boye and Harder (2012) address the grammar–lexicon distinction even more
directly, but offer a different answer. For them, grammatical expressions are
conventionally ancillary and therefore discursively secondary; closed paradigms
are incidental rather than constitutive. Their account and the
paradigmatisation account are principled alternatives. The January paper did
not resolve their disagreement by combining selected parts of each.

## What I am correcting

I withdraw the claim that _operator stratum_ names a newly discovered component
of grammar. I also withdraw any suggestion that the paper first connected
paradigmatic grammar with the public organization of utterances. Heltoft's
programme contains substantial, integrated precedents for both moves.

I am not withdrawing the motivating question on the ground that Heltoft already
answered it. The works reviewed above do not use the intersection of paradigm
structure and public-update contribution to explain selective grammaticality
judgments. They do not offer the January paper's three-way distinction among
expression-shape, operator, and payload mismatches. Nor do they distinguish a
wrong grammatical value from a value-preserving error in its conventional
expression and predict different repair loci for the two.

The January paper nevertheless treated conjectures as though they were an
explanation. In particular, its language of coordination “crashes” was too
strong: an ungrammatical utterance can be understood, and correction need not
show that its grammatical value disrupted uptake. Its information-theoretic
comparison was an undeveloped metaphor, not a measured model. Categorical
judgment, constructional licensing, exponent choice, processing difficulty,
schooling, and social sanction were also insufficiently separated.

## What remains open

A narrower empirical hypothesis remains worth testing. It has three stages:

1. Identify grammatical paradigms without using correction or repair as
   evidence. Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft's domain–frame architecture provides
   the structural scaffold.
2. In separate data, estimate how strongly each paradigm's values affect move
   type, commitment, participant or referent linking, and response relevance.
   This is an _operator profile_, not a new definition of grammar.
3. Within each paradigm, compare a wrong-value error with a matched error that
   preserves the intended value but uses the wrong exponent or allomorph. Then
   test whether the difference between update-directed and form-directed repair
   increases with the independently assigned operator profile.

Category placement, profile scoring, and the repair codebook would have to be
fixed before the outcomes were examined. Constructional licensing, frequency,
processing load, schooling, and social sanction would have to be matched or
accounted for. If the profile did not predict the held-out repair difference,
the explanatory hypothesis would fail even if the individual paradigm
descriptions remained sound.

I have not carried out that study. If suitable archival interaction data can be
found, it should begin as a separate empirical paper with its own preregistration
and version record. LingBuzz/009706 should not be cited as having established
the operator-stratum hypothesis. Its lasting contribution, if any, is the
question and a candidate test—not a demonstrated new theory of grammar.
