# The puzzle: why “grammar” picks out some things rather than others
Some mismatches are treated as straightforward errors of “grammar”: wrong agreement, illicit clausal morphosyntax, incompatible tense–aspect morphology, missing obligatory evidentials, or mis-selected question particles in systems where such particles are required. Other mismatches are treated as infelicities of social positioning: an accent that indexes the wrong stance, a style that fails to fit the activity type, or a form that sounds “too formal” or “too intimate”. Still others are treated as matters of lexical choice: poor perhaps, but not illicit.[^1]

A familiar temptation is to reduce this to a contrast between “structure” and “use”: morphosyntax counts as grammar; accent and lexical choice don’t. But this gloss is neither typologically adequate nor explanatory. Many languages use prosody or tone as obligatory exponents of grammatical contrasts (Hyman 2011; Ladd 2008). Many grammatical categories aren’t universal: a language may lack tense (Comrie 1985) or number (Corbett 2000) while sustaining categorical judgments elsewhere. And some lexical items enter operator contrasts, with constraints indistinguishable from inflectional morphology (Hopper and Traugott 2003; Bybee 2010).

Why do some form–value relations become non-negotiable parts of a community’s repertoire while others remain negotiable resources for stance, identity, and content selection?

I argue that one important part of this difference tracks a difference in value: what a contrast does in a system and what it rules in or out for uptake.[^2] Consider polarity: choosing _not_ over an affirmative changes what inferences are licensed. In the second type, a speaker uses an accent or register that doesn’t fit the setting; the hearer understands the utterance but reacts to the speaker’s social positioning: “Why are you talking like that?” What’s targeted is stance or persona, not the move itself.

I call this contrast public accountability. Interlocutors don’t merely exchange signals; they make commitments mutually recognizable and ratifiable (Stalnaker 1978, 2002; Clark and Brennan 1991; Clark 1996). Some grammatical contrasts tell them how to treat an utterance: as a question rather than an assertion, as committing one participant rather than another, or as linking a participant or referent across clauses. I call this contribution a public-update role. It’s public because the resulting move is available for acknowledgement, challenge, answer, or repair.

Repair practices make this selectivity visible. When an utterance isn’t understood, or is understood under a suspect analysis, interlocutors systematically mobilize repair (Schegloff et al. 1977). Repair is evidence about which dimension mattered in the interaction; it isn’t part of the definition of that dimension.

Public accountability isn’t the same as public consequence. Accent and style are central to social meaning, but they aren’t normally grammatical instructions for what an utterance counts as in the way that clause type, polarity, or evidential marking can be (Eckert 2008, 2012; Agha 2007; Coupland 2007). Lexical choice is similarly accountable in an ethical or epistemic sense: one can be challenged for using a slur or a misleading term. Those reactions differ from repair of the grammatical value that organizes the update.

Established paradigms can be assigned stronger or weaker public-update profiles. I use operator stratum as informal shorthand for the higher-profile portion of that ordering, not as a second membership category or a new definition of grammar. No threshold enters the empirical test.
# Paradigms, profiles, and the hypothesis
## The operator-profile hypothesis
Three parts have to remain separate.

(i) Paradigm. I adopt the paradigm architecture of Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft (2015, 262–63) as the structural scaffold. A paradigm has a closed set of alternatives, a recurring syntagmatic domain, and a language-specific semantic frame within which the alternatives contrast. Selection of a value is obligatory whenever the domain applies. Obligatoriness here concerns value selection, not an overt segment: a zero form, word-order pattern, intonational contour, or construction can select a value. Closure, domain, frame, and obligatory selection are constitutive in my use. Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft also require an asymmetry that distinguishes marked and unmarked members. I drop that fifth admission criterion and record marked–unmarked organization descriptively. This deliberately relaxes their strict definition.

Paradigmhood itself isn’t graded here. A candidate either clears those structural identification requirements in the conditioned repertoire under study or remains outside the comparison. Boundary uncertainty belongs in the evidence for that decision, not in a second taxonomy of “operator-like resources”. This restriction prevents an open set of interjections or discourse markers from entering the stratum merely because some of its members matter to interaction.

(ii) Operator profile. An identified paradigm is assigned a graded profile for the extent to which its values conventionally help determine what move is made, whose commitment is at issue, how participants or referents are linked, or what responses are relevant (Stalnaker 2002; Farkas and Bruce 2010; Murray 2014). Clause type, illocutionary force, evidential anchoring, and switch-reference are strong candidates. The profile applies to the paradigm across its ordinary occasions of use, not to the information carried by every token. An agreement marker can be redundant in one clause and decisive in another while its paradigm remains available for participant or reference tracking.

The profile concerns licensed update types rather than whether a claim is true or successful. A speaker can say _It’s true_ of a false situation: the assertion fails epistemically, but affirmative polarity and declarative force are licensed. By contrast, past-tense _Yesterday the bill passed_ selects a past-time anchor, while *_Yesterday the bill pass_ can preserve that intended value but fail to supply its licensed finite exponent. Temporal value and exponent licensing are distinct.

Context selects among independently licensed values, constructional frames, and ellipsis patterns; it doesn’t create a form–value relation ad hoc. _I have five years_ can succeed as an elliptical reply to “How long do you have left?” because the frame supplies the relevant duration. The same reply to “How old are you?” fails in ordinary English because age is expressed through the _be_ frame, not because the hearer lacks the intended meaning.

(iii) Operator-profile hypothesis. The empirical claim begins only after paradigm identification and operator profiling. The confirmatory prediction is continuous: the wrong-value versus value-preserving-exponent difference in update-directed repair should increase with the independently assigned operator profile. The within-paradigm contrast alone is a manipulation check because the stimulus definitions already distinguish value change from value preservation. Repair and comprehension are outcomes, not membership criteria. A high profile can fail to predict the proposed outcome without being redescribed after the fact.

Opportunity density remains separate. It affects how much evidence speakers encounter and how strongly competitors preempt one another, which can sharpen licensing profiles and resistance to exposure. A moribund paradigm can retain a high operator profile while its judgments grow less concentrated. A frequent collocation can be sharply conventional without forming a paradigm or receiving an operator profile.

The three-part architecture is synchronic. Grammaticalization and related discourse-functional research documents the recruitment of lexical and constructional material into grammatical and discourse functions (Hopper and Traugott 2003; Traugott and Dasher 2002; Lehmann 2015; Waltereit 2011; Degand and Evers-Vermeul 2015). It doesn’t thereby establish that public-update work preferentially creates closed paradigms. That stronger historical claim requires separate comparative evidence and can’t rescue a failed synchronic prediction. Once a paradigm exists, dense competitor evidence may stabilize its licensed exponents; population persistence requires a further account of adoption and retention (Reynolds 2026a).

Two dissociations follow. First, a locally redundant token can belong to a high-profile paradigm, as some agreement markers may. Second, an interactionally consequential resource can remain outside the paradigm comparison. English _wow_, for example, can configure assessment and uptake while competing with an expandable, gradient family of exclamatory resources (Ameka 1992).

Paradigms can be realized morphologically, syntactically, phonologically, prosodically, gesturally, or lexically. What matters for identification is the domain–frame organization, not the substance of the exponent. What matters for the operator profile is the conventional update contribution. Morphology and clausal architecture are privileged only where they instantiate that architecture.

Phonemic contrasts illustrate the division. They can form organized systems that keep expressions apart, but they don’t thereby configure an utterance as a public move. Paradigm structure and operator profile ask different questions.

Seven levels remain distinct: the historical recruitment of a resource; the present identification of its paradigm; its operator profile; the construction that embeds it; the value selected in a token; the exponent that realizes that value; and the extent to which a repertoire’s architecture is organized by high-profile paradigms. One high-profile paradigm inside a construction doesn’t make every constraint on that construction profile highly, and a construction that hosts several such paradigms doesn’t make every syntactic mismatch a high-profile value error.

Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft (2015, 276–78) are the closest architectural predecessor. Their Danish V2 analysis already treats word order as an obligatory assertivity paradigm, and their constructional cases extend paradigmatic organization beyond morphology. I claim neither move as new. This paper narrows the object: only an independently identified paradigm can receive an operator profile, and the profile matters explanatorily only if it predicts a later response contrast.

Boye and Harder (2012, 1, 7–8, 30–31) supply the principled opposition. On their account, closed paradigms and obligatoriness are incidental to grammatical status and grammaticalization, while conventional ancillarity and discursive secondary status are central. Their secondary status is relative to one or more syntagmatically related expressions; it isn’t equivalent to at-issue content. Nothing here decides that general dispute. Closure is constitutive only for the narrower comparison set used by the operator-profile hypothesis.

The interactional literature also sets a demanding baseline. Muntigl and Ventola (2010, 105–6, 111–19) show that nominalization, argument realization, and Theme selection can redirect later talk. On the definition adopted here, those productive resources are candidates for nonoperator controls wherever Stage 1 doesn’t identify a small closed paradigm; that classification is mine, not theirs. Fox et al. (1996, 192–95) connect syntax with same-turn self-repair across English and Japanese, but don’t pre-assign operator profiles or contrast a wrong value with a value-preserving exponent error. Their work is a methodological precedent, not evidence that the prediction is already true.

My use of operator remains continuous with Role and Reference Grammar, Functional Grammar, and Functional Discourse Grammar, where aspect, tense, modality, negation, and illocutionary force are operators over layers of clause structure (Hengeveld 1989; Van Valin and LaPolla 1997; Van Valin 2005; Hengeveld and Mackenzie 2008; Bentley et al. 2023). The operator profile isn’t a new clausal layer. It can apply across morphological, syntactic, tonal, prosodic, gestural, and lexical exponents.

Recent interactional-syntax proposals make the difference sharper. Wiltschko’s interactional spine grammatically represents speaker, addressee, grounding, and response structure, while Krifka’s layered analyses distinguish judgment, commitment, and speech-act projections (Wiltschko 2025; Krifka 2025). The operator-profile hypothesis concerns comparative profiles and held-out consequences across expressive substances. Levinson’s warning that speech acts can’t be reduced to context change is compatible with this scope (Levinson 2026): a public-update contribution is one conventional resource for action, not a complete theory of speech acts or conversation.

The cases make two boundaries especially clear.

Slurs satisfy the public-consequence test but usually fail paradigm identification. A racial slur used as a vocative, or a misogynistic slur used as a predicate, can radically alter the social action performed and can make the speaker answerable for that action. But the utterance normally remains structurally fileable as an address, assertion, accusation, or description. The move is morally or socially impermissible even though the hearer can identify its update type. The predicted response is sanction of the act or speaker, not repair of an operator value.

Interjections can configure uptake, stance, attention, repair, or floor management (Ameka 1992). English _huh?_, for example, can initiate repair, while _wow_ can display surprise or assessment; cross-linguistic repair formats show recurrent functional organization without reducing to a single closed grammatical value set (Dingemanse et al. 2014).

But many interjection tokens aren’t selected from a closed, domain-bound set with obligatory value selection. They can be repeated, stretched, blended, newly coined, or substituted without producing a categorical licensing failure. They receive no operator profile in this architecture. If an interjection-like resource becomes organized as a paradigm, it can enter the comparison; uptake configuration alone isn’t enough.

French *_le hiver_ is a value-preserving form error within the definite-determiner system. Its singular forms include _le_, _la_, and elided _l’_, with gender and morphophonological restrictions. French h muet words such as _hiver_ ‘winter’ pattern with vowel-initial words and trigger elision, whereas h aspiré words block it (Boersma 2007). The intended value in *_le hiver_ is still definite reference. What fails is its licensed realization: the required form is _l’hiver_. This token establishes neither the containing system’s paradigm status nor its operator profile.

*_Goed_ preserves past time by taking the productive past exponent _-ed_ where this lexeme requires the suppletive past allomorph _went_. The failure is lexeme-specific allomorph licensing. *_Depend of_ is analyzed at a different level. Complementation is organized by community conventions that are partly schematic and partly head-specific. A head can select a complement category or function while narrowing its realization to a PP headed by one or a few prepositions; English _depend_ licenses _on_ or _upon_. *_Depend of_ is a constructional-licensing failure in a dense niche, even though the dependency and intended meaning are recoverable. No public-update value has been mis-set. Its sharp correction illustrates why neither a categorical judgment nor a form-focused repair can equate high operator profiles with grammaticality as a whole.

The boundary generates the central matched test. Within each established paradigm, compare a value mismatch with a value-preserving exponent or allomorph mismatch as a manipulation check. Across paradigms, test whether that repair-locus difference increases with the independently measured operator profile. Opportunity, frequency, construction, processing load, schooling, and social sanction have to be matched or accounted for. If the profile adds no reach to held-out repair or comprehension, it may remain descriptively useful but the operator-profile hypothesis fails. Historical recruitment can’t be added after the fact to rescue it.
## Identification and outcome protocol
The design has two preregistered stages. Freezing category placement alone isn’t enough; the outcome codebook also has to be fixed before responses are inspected.

Stage 1a: paradigm identification. Descriptive and corpus evidence first specifies the conditioned repertoire, the recurring domain, the alternatives, and their semantic frame. It then tests whether the set is closed and whether a value has to be selected whenever the domain applies. Sharp judgments, correction, and the later repair task are withheld. Tense–aspect systems, agreement paradigms, switch-reference markers, question particles, and complementizer inventories are candidates, not presumed cases.

Closure requires a bounded value inventory that exhausts the choices found in the specified domain across additional corpus samples and consultant elicitation. Productive new exponents can realize existing values; a productive route to new values, or an open residual enquoteother response, defeats closure. The conditioned repertoire and stopping rule are fixed before the outcome task.

Obligatory selection requires every independently accepted token in the domain to receive one value from that inventory. A zero realization counts only where its distribution contrasts with overt members in the same frame and independently changes the frame interpretation. Mere lack of an overt form doesn’t establish a zero value; it may instead show that the proposed domain isn’t active.

Dropping Norgaard-Sorensen and Heltoft’s markedness criterion enlarges the possible comparison set. Each study reports the primary four-criterion analysis and a sensitivity analysis restricted to paradigms that also show their marked–unmarked asymmetry. A result that disappears in the strict subset supports only the relaxed comparison and can’t be attributed to their full architecture.

Stage 1b: operator profiling. For each identified paradigm, preregister the update variables its values might affect: move type, commitment holder or source, participant or referent linking, and response relevance. In a separate sample, present minimal value contrasts while holding at-issue content as constant as the language permits. Forced classification and interpretation tasks estimate how much changing the value changes each of the four variables. All four are scored on a common scale; no observed effect receives zero rather than being treated as inapplicable. The primary profile gives the standardized variables equal weight. Indicator coding, weights, missing-data rules, contexts, and uncertainty aggregation are frozen before the repair outcomes are inspected.

Stage 2 uses a different task format and new items, such as open next-turn responses or production repair, to reduce shared-task-method variance. A broad interpretive-consequence rating, collected in the same format as the profiling measures, enters as a rival covariate. A next-turn distribution can contribute to Stage 1b or serve as an outcome, but not both. If general interpretive consequence subsumes the profile interaction, the operator-specific attribution fails. Broad scope and interpersonal consequence are insufficient on their own.

Design covariates. Opportunity is the number of occasions on which a paradigm value and its competitors could plausibly have been selected. Exponent frequency, surprisal, constructional frame, phonetic salience, processing load, schooling or norm exposure, and social sanction are measured separately. These are commitments for matching or adjustment, not controls the theoretical paper claims already to have achieved. Opportunity predicts the concentration of licensing evidence, not paradigm status or operator profile (Reynolds 2026a).

Stage 2: frozen outcomes. Before coding held-out responses, define update confusion, update-directed repair, form correction, constructional correction, social sanction, and unresolved trouble. Update-directed repair targets force, commitment, role, or reference; form correction supplies a licensed exponent and preserves the intended update. Double-code ambiguous responses and report reliability rather than forcing every case into one bin.

The sole confirmatory test is the cross-paradigm interaction: does the wrong-value versus value-preserving-exponent difference in update-directed repair increase with the frozen operator profile after the measured design covariates are matched or accounted for? The within-paradigm difference is a manipulation check, repair locus is the primary outcome, and held-out comprehension is secondary. If the interaction is absent or smaller than the pre-specified minimum effect under the uncertainty rule, the operator-profile hypothesis fails even if paradigm descriptions and operator profiles remain useful.
# How clausal architecture hosts candidate paradigms
Treating clausal architecture as a matter of arranging words gives an English-biased picture. Across languages, clause type can be realized through particles, verbal morphology, word order, intonation, and manual or nonmanual marking in signed languages (Dryer 2013; Ladd 2008; Zeshan 2004). The relevant generalization concerns operator packaging rather than linearization.

Clausal architecture can host high-profile paradigms in clause type, argument linking, dependency scope, and reference tracking. Other syntactic constraints reflect expression-shape regularities, processing limits, or constructional conventionalization. A high-profile paradigm inside a construction doesn’t make every constraint on that construction profile highly.

The operator-profile hypothesis earns its keep only if it supports projectibility (what observing some features licenses us to predict about others; (Goodman 1955)). The prediction has to concern evidence not used for paradigm identification or operator profiling (Reynolds 2026b). The bearer is an identified paradigm in a community repertoire; the scope is a specified activity and speaker population. Its wrong-value versus value-preserving-exponent repair difference should grow with its frozen operator profile after the pre-specified rivals are matched or accounted for. The expected effect is distributional rather than exceptionless. Each study fixes a minimum effect of interest and an uncertainty rule before outcomes are inspected. If the held-out interaction disappears, the explanatory attribution fails even if the paradigm and its descriptive profile survive.

Clausal architecture supplies candidate operator paradigms in at least four domains.

Clause type and response space. Interrogatives, imperatives, declaratives, and related types are candidates for different update potentials and norms for a relevant next move. Languages grammaticalize interrogatives using heterogeneous resources (particles, morphology, intonation, or combinations) (Dryer 2013; Ladd 2008). Paradigm identification tests their organization; profiling measures how much a value change alters response relevance. A high profile predicts update confusion when a construction supplies the wrong clause-type value.

Argument linking and participant roles. Clausal organization encodes which participant is presented as actor, undergoer, experiencer, and so on. The implementation varies typologically, but independently identified case, agreement, or voice paradigms may constrain recoverable role assignments. Profiling tests how often a value change alters participant tracking; the semantic label alone supplies no repair prediction.

Dependency management and scope. Some complement contrasts package how information is nested, attributed, and scoped. _Kim knows that Pat left_ presents an embedded proposition, whereas _Kim wonders whether Pat left_ opens an embedded question. *_Kim believes whether Pat left_ misselects an interrogative complement for the intended belief attribution. Here constructional licensing and operator value intersect: the matrix head restricts the complement construction, and the complementizer helps identify whether the embedded contribution is propositional or interrogative (Stalnaker 2002; Farkas and Bruce 2010).

Reference tracking across clauses. Switch-reference is a strong paradigm candidate: a morphosyntactic system signals whether the subject of one clause is the same as or different from that of another (Gijn 2016). The adopted criteria establish the paradigm; separate profiling tests whether changing a value alters tracking across the multi-clause sequence. The relevant pivot varies across systems.

To illustrate the form of the claim, consider Amele, a Papuan language in which dependent switch-reference verbs in clause chains mark whether the relevant argument is maintained or changed across adjacent clauses (Roberts 1987, 1988, 2023). Roberts’s examples contrast forms such as _h-um-ig_ ‘come-ss.seq-1sg.nom’ and _ho-co-min_ ‘come-ds.seq-1sg.nom’ before a final tense-marked verb. The forms supply clause-linking values. If independent profiling assigns the paradigm a high score, the wrong marker should be more likely to disturb clause-linking interpretation than a matched value-preserving exponent error. Observing a repair wouldn’t by itself establish that attribution.

A second, different kind of illustration comes from Japanese sentence-final particles. In many registers, sentence-final _ka_ conventionally marks interrogative force, while declaratives are unmarked or carry other particles; intonation interacts with these choices, and colloquial speech allows more variation (Kuno 1973; Shibatani 1990). This conditioned clause-typing use, not every lexical or constructional use of _ka_, helps determine what kind of move the utterance counts as and what responses are licensed. A clause-typing paradigm can require selection of a value without requiring overt _ka_ in every construction. Its absence may select a declarative, an intonation-marked question, or another licensed alternative; its presence may select an unintended question or be blocked in combination with other particles, intonation, or morphosyntax. Paradigm-level obligatory choice doesn’t entail construction-level obligatoriness of one overt exponent (Reynolds 2026a).

Together, the Amele and Japanese cases separate two claims that are easily conflated. A paradigm requires value selection in its domain; a particular construction may still permit several values and several exponents. A high operator profile predicts only the matched, update-specific repair difference once a mismatch has been created, not whether the construction creates one. In both cases, clausal architecture matters because it packages values for public update, not because syntax is insulated from interaction or wholly composed of operators.

Complementation also shows why community convention and operator value remain separate. Complement constructions are schematic: they assign categories and grammatical functions, while particular heads may narrow a complement to a clause of a certain type or to a PP headed by one of a small set of prepositions. English _put_ conventionally requires a locative complement, and _depend_ selects _on_ or _upon_. These are constructional-licensing facts even when no public-update contrast is involved. Complementation can also host high-profile paradigms where its values configure commitment, clause type, scope, or participant linking, as in _that_ vs. _whether_. Schematic inheritance and lexically narrowed realization coexist.
# Why tense and number behave similarly without being universal
Tense and number enter grammaticality judgments through language-specific paradigms and constructional requirements. Only an independent operator profile establishes whether one of those paradigms also configures public update. In a language without obligatory tense marking, the mere absence of tense morphology supplies no tense-value error. Other constructional requirements can still make a clause unlicensed. This account avoids a common slippage between salience in familiar European languages and cross-linguistic necessity (Haspelmath 2007).

Tense marking is an especially clear example. Where tense is grammaticalized, it can form a paradigm for temporal anchoring; independent profiling then tests how strongly its values configure public update (Comrie 1985). In languages without obligatory tense, temporal anchoring can instead depend on aspect, temporal adverbials, discourse structure, and contextual inference (Lin 2006; Tonhauser 2011). Speakers in the latter case shouldn’t experience missing tense marking as a tense-value error, because their repertoire contains no obligatory tense contrast to leave unsatisfied.

Number behaves analogously only where independent evidence shows that a number paradigm contributes to reference tracking in public commitments. If a construction also makes number obligatory in agreement or nominal morphology, a mismatch becomes a paradigmatic licensing failure. In languages where number is optional or limited, or where its public-update contribution isn’t established, number mismatches can have a different status (Corbett 2000).

Operator repertoires vary across languages. Licensing failures can target a construction, a paradigm value, or its exponent; only independently high profiles predict the proposed update-directed repair difference.
# Gender and noun-class concord as a hard case
Gender and noun-class concord are a limiting case for the account. With nouns whose class isn’t independently informative in context (including many inanimates in familiar sex-based systems), a concord mismatch may have little or no effect on the interpretation of that token: *_le table_ normally doesn’t leave the hearer wondering which object is meant. But it’s treated as a central grammar error where gender concord is obligatory (Corbett 1991). The point concerns local redundancy in any noun-class system, not inanimacy as a universal organizing principle.

That pattern would be hard to explain if public-update contribution were required in every token. The relevant question is instead whether a specified concord paradigm, in the constructional domain under study, contributes to participant or reference tracking across its occasions of use. Where independent evidence establishes that role, a locally redundant exponent still belongs to the same paradigm. High opportunity density can then concentrate evidence for its licensed forms, so a wrong exponent can be corrected even when the local referent is obvious. Evidence from another agreement domain can’t supply the paradigm-level role by inheritance.

Public-update role remains empirically constrained. The Mahowald-style point is local redundancy: agreement can be redundant in most clauses while remaining decisive in the minority where lexical semantics, animacy, or discourse expectations underdetermine reference (Mahowald et al. 2023). Whether a particular gender system has that paradigm-level role is an empirical question for comprehension probes, not a fact licensed by the existence of categorical judgments. A diachronic claim that reference tracking favours the recruitment of concord systems remains a separate hypothesis.

The operational prediction has two levels. A concord manipulation on a locally uninformative noun may have no measurable effect on that token’s interpretation, while the independent profiling task still finds a paradigm-level contribution to reference tracking. Held-out probes can then test whether wrong values produce the predicted update-confusion profile. Sharp correction alone can’t settle either question, because constructional licensing and schooling can also produce it.
# Why accent and lexical choice are usually different
Accent and lexical choice typically have different kinds of value.
## Indexical value and negotiability
Accent, style, and register contribute indexical value: they position speakers relative to social categories, stances, and activity types (Silverstein 1976; Eckert 2008; Agha 2007; Coupland 2007). Indexical value is often rich and structured, but it isn’t typically implemented as a small closed paradigm tied to public update. Interlocutors can reinterpret an accent shift as play, accommodation, quotation, or stance work. The contrast remains socially accountable without thereby forming a high-profile paradigm.

Indexicality can be obligatory in social practice without forming a high-profile paradigm. Much indexical meaning is _meta-pragmatic_: it becomes salient through social ideologies and enregisterment processes (Agha 2007; Eckert 2012). That makes it both powerful and variable, but it rarely supplies a value that has to be selected for the utterance to count as a recognized move.

Japanese polite _desu/masu_ and plain style provide a useful closure test within a conditioned repertoire. Here _desu/masu_ is sociolinguistic shorthand: _-masu_ marks polite verbal predicates, while _desu_ is its polite copular or predicative counterpart. Speakers shift between polite and plain forms to present identities and stances within one interaction (Cook 2008; Ide 2005). Closure doesn’t make the politeness contrast profile highly. Unless an independent measure shows that it configures participant roles or update type, a correction of speech style is better attributed to indexical fit, constructional convention, or schooling. This differs from *_le hiver_, where definite reference is selected and only its phonologically licensed form fails; that example establishes no operator profile of its own. T/V pronouns are mixed cases because a single form can realize addressee reference and person–number features while also indexing social relations.
## Conceptual value and open classes
Open-class lexical items primarily contribute conceptual content, and their inventories tolerate expansion and innovation. Lexical choice can be infelicitous, misleading, or socially harmful without mis-setting a grammatical update. Lexically conditioned patterns can still be categorical: a head can impose constructional restrictions on its complements, as _depend_ does, while the open lexical inventory remains outside the paradigm comparison.

“Open-endedness” has two dimensions that matter here. Lexical inventories are open in the sense of expandable type-sets. Compositional semantics is open-ended in a different sense: unbounded productivity from combining a finite stock. Operators lack the first kind of openness, but they participate fully in the second: negation scopes, evidentials anchor commitments, and clause type constrains response space. Closed inventories make it easier for repeated choices to concentrate licensing expectations, but closure alone doesn’t supply an operator role.

That difference predicts why a wrong operator value and a poor lexical choice can invite different responses even when both are understood. The former can alter the public classification of the move; the latter usually invites negotiation about content. Constructionally excluded lexical combinations remain a third possibility.
## Cross-substance realization
Expressive substance doesn’t determine an operator profile. Phonological and lexical material can realize values in an identified paradigm; profiling separately tests the paradigm’s public-update role.

Systems in which tone marks tense, focus, or polarity are candidates for paradigms with phonological exponents (Hyman 2011). If the adopted paradigm criteria hold and independent profiling assigns a high public-update contribution, wrong tonal values should attract more update-directed repair than value-preserving tonal realization errors. Intonational contours can similarly realize values in an identified clause-typing paradigm; the same conditional prediction distinguishes them from accent (Ladd 2008). This aligns with work on rising declaratives and intonational commitments, where intonational tunes modify context-update functions rather than merely colouring pronunciation (Gunlogson 2003; Rudin 2022).

Signed languages supply candidates in another modality. In American Sign Language, a yes/no question can be marked by nonmanual cues such as brow raise over the relevant clause. Within an independently identified and profiled clause-typing paradigm, scopal nonmanual marking can realize the relevant value without a segmental word (Sandler and Lillo-Martin 2006).

Lexical items can come to realize values in paradigms through grammaticalization. Their meanings may become more schematic or conventionalized, while they become more tightly integrated into paradigms and constructional distributions (Hopper and Traugott 2003; Lehmann 2015; Bybee 2010). Function words, particles, auxiliaries, complementizers, and polarity items can realize values in high-profile paradigms. Lexical form alone is insufficient. A preposition selected by a particular head may realize a community-specific complement construction without configuring public update. Its lexical source, constructional licensing, and operator profile are separate questions.

Phonotactics sharpens the same point. Phonotactic constraints are conventionalized and often treated categorically (“not a possible word”), but they don’t satisfy the public-update condition. They regulate expression shape rather than the grammatical value of the move. The likely repair target differs: recognizability for phonotactics, update value for operators, conventional form for constructional or exponent licensing, and content or stance for many lexical and pragmatic mismatches. These are tendencies to test, not exclusive response bins.
## Scope and local redundancy
Operators often affect how other material is interpreted. Once an utterance is marked interrogative, the remaining material is most typically understood as the content of a question rather than an assertion.[^3] Polarity changes the inferential contribution of material in its scope; case and agreement can constrain role assignment. Operators don’t always reduce more uncertainty than lexical items: predicates such as _congratulate_ and _accuse_ can reshape continuations dramatically.

Scope can make the consequences of a value error non-local. A wrong clause-type, polarity, or reference-tracking value may prompt reanalysis beyond its exponent, while a value-preserving exponent error need not. But scope alone establishes neither an operator profile nor a processing mechanism. The matched prediction is the error-locus difference developed in §[3.4](#sec:identifying-operators) and tested in §[10](#sec:predictions).

This explains a puzzle: grammatical marking is often locally redundant.[^4] Mahowald et al. (2023) show that across 30 languages, subjecthood in simple transitive clauses can usually be inferred from lexical content alone. Overt grammatical cues are redundant in a majority of everyday cases, but decisive for the minority where lexical semantics doesn’t settle the roles (reversible events, non-prototypical assignments). Operators can be locally redundant but globally update-relevant. Their contribution isn’t to maximize information in each occurrence, but to supply a codebook whose values remain available when context, lexical semantics, or discourse expectations underdetermine the update.

Frequent, predictable grammatical material often undergoes phonetic reduction. Reduced exponents can still belong to an identified paradigm because its operator profile depends on the paradigm’s contribution, not on phonetic invariance.

Opportunity structure supplies a separate prediction. Repeated, attributable competitor choices can concentrate evidence for a licensing boundary (Bybee 2006, 2010; Reynolds 2026a). Wrong operator values may prompt broader reanalysis of material in their scope; value-preserving exponent errors instead predict form correction with the intended update preserved. This error-locus split is the processing claim.
# Typological consequences: which paradigms receive high profiles
As a comparative concept, the operator profile identifies an interactional role that can be realized by different structures in different languages (Haspelmath 2007). Typological diversity concerns which language-specific systems form paradigms and how strongly those paradigms configure public update, not whether English categories are universal.

Four domains are particularly instructive.
## Instructive domains of variation
Evidentiality and epistemic authority. A language-specific evidential system enters the comparison only if closure, domain, semantic frame, and obligatory value selection are independently established (Aikhenvald 2004). Profiling then tests whether its values configure whose commitment is at issue and on what evidential basis. If the profile is high and a construction requires a particular value, the hypothesis predicts update-directed repair for wrong values. Lexical or periphrastic evidential meanings can instead be challenged as deceptive or inappropriate without establishing a paradigm or its profile.

Egophoricity and perspective management. Language-specific perspective and ego-involvement systems are candidates for paradigm identification (Tournadre 2008; Floyd et al. 2018). If all four adopted criteria hold, profiling can test whether the values configure whose epistemic position anchors a clause’s update potential. A high frozen profile predicts that a constructionally induced wrong value will receive more update-directed repair than a value-preserving realization error. Such systems are candidates for high operator profiles, not operators by semantic label alone.

Where perspective doesn’t form an identified, high-profile paradigm, similar meanings remain expressible but the operator-profile hypothesis makes no update-directed repair prediction.

Phonological exponents vs. accent. Tonal systems provide a strong test of the cross-substance claim (Hyman 2011). Once a tonal tense, aspect, or polarity system is independently identified as a paradigm and assigned a high profile, wrong tonal values should pattern like wrong morphosyntactic values rather than accent shifts. A language may distinguish tense values primarily through tonal melodies on the verb (one pattern for remote past, another for recent past), with minimal segmental differentiation between the forms.

A concrete case is Ngwi, a West-Coastal Bantu language whose Tense–Aspect–Mood–Polarity system includes grammatical tone in a compact set of verbal conjugations (Pacchiarotti and Bostoen 2024). Its grammatical-tone values can be tested against the paradigm criteria and profiled separately from sociophonetic variation that indexes region or age. A high-profile result predicts “did you mean X or Y?” for a wrong value and form correction for a wrong realization of the intended value. Grammatical function and tonal substance don’t settle the classification in advance.

Switch-reference as instruction. Switch-reference systems show how clausal architecture can package a candidate paradigm beyond simple word order. In Amele, for example, a dependent verb in a clause chain can mark same-subject vs. different-subject linkage before the final tense-marked verb (Roberts 1987, 1988, 2023). The following layouts are schematic, not quoted Amele examples.

_Verb_-ss.seq … _Verb_ (instruction: keep same participant active) _Verb_-ds.seq … _Verb_ (instruction: switch participant role)

The schematic contrast identifies reference-tracking values. If independent profiling assigns the paradigm a high score, the hypothesis predicts that a value mismatch (using the same-subject marker when the subjects are different) will disrupt reference assignment and clause linkage more than a matched value-preserving form error. The repair locus remains an attribution question, not a consequence read off the label.
## The boundary case: T/V and grammaticalized social deixis
If an indexical distinction such as addressee status forms a paradigm, it becomes eligible for an operator profile; it doesn’t thereby receive a high one. T/V distinctions test this boundary (Brown and Gilman 1960; Helmbrecht 2013). Four levels are at issue: the addressee’s identity and cardinality; the pronoun’s grammatical person–number value; its T/V social-index value; and the agreement it licenses. French _tu_ and respectful singular _vous_ can keep one addressee fixed while contrasting grammatical number and social index, so obligatory choice in the pronoun slot doesn’t show that the social contrast configures update type. A mismatch can combine reference, agreement, and indexicality, but the contributions require separate measures. Japanese _desu/masu_ can shift social presentation without changing addressee reference, while *_le hiver_ preserves reference and definiteness but fails morphophonological form licensing (§[7.1](#sec:indexicality)). None of these repair loci follows from paradigmhood alone.
# Grammaticality judgments as a social technology
Grammaticality judgments can function as a social technology: they let speakers and institutions mark forms as belonging to, or falling outside, a repertoire (Cameron 2012). When such judgments concern high-profile paradigms, three variables organize the practice.

The community’s operator inventory records which paradigms exist and the profiles assigned to them. Activation conditions determine which contrasts are in play in a register, activity type, or genre: a formal written register may activate a subjunctive contrast that’s dormant in casual speech. Accountability conditions record how participant roles calibrate correction: L2 learners, children, and visitors to an unfamiliar dialect region may be evaluated differently even when the same local inventory is active. The inventory and operator profile concern paradigm and repertoire levels; activation conditions concern constructions in activities; accountability conditions concern speaker-level read-out. Neither set of conditions creates a paradigm’s public-update contribution token by token.

Situation/ascription/identification conditioning gives these conditions their social architecture. The immediate communicative situation selects the activity, medium, footing, and task; speaker ascription supplies durable baselines such as region, generation, institutional role, or learner status; and identification tracks the norm-centre participants orient to as relevant. These dimensions condition both which repertoires are available and how departures are treated. A form can be licensed for participants oriented to one norm-centre and unlicensed for participants oriented to another, without either side being confused about its form or meaning (Reynolds 2026c). Negative concord illustrates the point: _I didn’t see nobody_ is licensed in some English repertoires and communicative situations but excluded when a standardized-school norm is activated, even though the intended negative meaning is readily recoverable.

These conditions help explain why grammatical conventions can be stable without making every correction communicatively necessary. Signaling-game models show that coordination problems can produce sharp local conventions and arbitrary exclusions (O’Connor 2019). They don’t establish that actual communities converge by that route. The relevant accountability is practice-constitutive: participating in a linguistic practice can make speakers answerable to its constraints (Millar 2004), but it doesn’t entail moral obligation or a corrective-control mechanism.

Repair fits the same pattern. It treats some departures as requiring correction and others as negotiable (Schegloff et al. 1977). A wrong operator value can prompt clarification of the move; a value-preserving exponent error can prompt correction of the form; and a constructional error can be corrected even without an operator contrast.

This framing also clarifies why judgment sharpness is common but not inevitable. Some high-profile paradigms are supported by dense opportunities and tightly constrained constructions, which makes judgments highly concentrated. Others have smaller opportunity counts or are contested across norm centres, which makes judgments more variable. The same dynamics can make non-operator constructions appear categorical, so sharpness is predicted to covary with concentrated licensing evidence rather than diagnose an operator profile.

Speakers frequently label accent or dialect choices as “bad grammar” to enforce social hierarchies. Everyday metalinguistic use, a syntactician’s technical use, and the comparative concept used here needn’t have the same extension; the issue is field-relative vocabulary, not field-relative licensing facts. The paper doesn’t legislate the one true meaning of “grammar”. It asks whether the target of a response is an update value, an exponent, a construction, or a social index. “Did you mean past time?” may target an operator value; “you mean _went_” may target its licensed allomorph; “you mean _depend on_” may target a complement construction. Ideological correction instead targets the speaker’s social index while the utterance remains understood (Agha 2007; Eckert 2012). These response types can overlap, so researchers need to code them rather than assume them.

Slurs and taboo words remain boundary cases for the same reason. They’re publicly accountable in a strong sense, but the typical sanction targets an impermissible action rather than a defective update instruction. The same surface reaction, “you can’t say that”, can block a social move or reject a linguistic instrument.

I address one target of grammaticality talk, not how such talk is socially deployed. High-profile paradigms provide material for language ideology, but that doesn’t make judgments politically neutral. Who speaks for “the community”, who can correct, and with what consequences are questions about the social life of the practice.

Operator-directed grammaticality talk isn’t identical to the output of a judgment task. Paradigm identification, operator profiling, and licensing concern population-level properties of a conditioned repertoire. Corpus counts and comprehension probes estimate those properties; felt ungrammaticality and correction are speaker-level read-outs shaped by exposure, processing, social penalty, task design, and metalinguistic training. Evidence, estimate, and read-out shouldn’t be substituted for the world-side target (Reynolds and Miller 2026; Reynolds 2026a).
# Predictions and research strategies
The protocol yields one primary confirmatory test and two secondary generalizations. All are conditional on the pre-outcome identification and coding stages in §[3.4](#sec:identifying-operators).

(1) The operator-profile-by-error-locus interaction. Conversation favours self-repair over other-correction: other-correction is dispreferred, while other-initiation of repair is common and usually gives the original speaker an opportunity to repair (Schegloff et al. 1977; Dingemanse et al. 2015). Within each identified paradigm, wrong-value items should disproportionately produce clarification of force, commitment, role, or reference; value-preserving exponent errors should disproportionately produce form correction with the intended update retained. That within-paradigm difference checks the manipulation. The confirmatory coefficient is its interaction with the independently assigned operator profile across paradigms.

The prediction is more specific than a higher correction rate for high-profile paradigms. *_Depend of_ can elicit “you mean X” through constructional licensing, and a socially marked form can elicit person-targeted sanction. The confirmatory outcome is the locus of the response, not correction rate alone. Cross-linguistic repair research supplies broader coding precedents but not validation of the proposed interaction; Fox and colleagues specifically code same-turn syntactic self-repair rather than the held-out next-turn categories used here (Fox et al. 1996; Dingemanse et al. 2014, 2015). Adult interaction with little formal-school correction can reduce one prescriptive rival, but dialect, learner status, institutional role, and task format remain measured accountability conditions.

(2) The profile should survive local redundancy. In gender, agreement, and reference-tracking systems, a manipulation can leave one token’s interpretation unchanged while the paradigm remains informative across its opportunities. After Stage 1 has assigned the profile, held-out contexts should show token-level null effects where lexical semantics already determines the referent and positive effects where reference is underdetermined. The local null is predicted; reassigning the profile after seeing it isn’t permitted. A low-profile paradigm that produces the same value-versus-exponent repair difference as a high-profile paradigm counts against the hypothesis.

(3) The interaction should cross expressive substance. Where tone, intonation, or nonmanual marking realizes values in an identified paradigm, the same profile-by-error-locus interaction should appear after opportunity, frequency, and perceptual salience are matched (Hyman 2011; Ladd 2008). Tsiwah et al. (2021) report a P600-like response to one temporal-agreement violation in Akan, where time reference is expressed through grammatical tone; this prediction concerns the broader matched repair profile, not a unique ERP component. Compare tonal tense mismatches, segmental tense or agreement mismatches, and sociophonetic tone shifts. Wrong values may prompt reanalysis of material in scope; value-preserving exponent errors should concentrate correction on the form. This interaction is more specific than the familiar contrast between morphosyntactic and conceptual anomaly (Kutas and Hillyard 1980; Osterhout and Holcomb 1992; Friederici 2011).

Two expectations remain corollaries rather than discriminating tests. Resistance to satiation should increase with independently measured evidence concentration and effective preemption for operator and nonoperator constructions alike (Bybee 2006, 2010; Snyder 2000; Sprouse 2009; Reynolds 2026a). Accent and register should show systematic indexical fields (Eckert 2008; Agha 2007), but an indexical system receives an operator profile only after it independently qualifies as a paradigm.

The failure condition belongs to the contribution statement. If the frozen operator profile doesn’t predict the held-out value-versus-exponent repair difference after opportunity, competitor strength, surprisal, prosody, processing load, schooling, social sanction, and task are matched or accounted for, the operator-profile hypothesis adds no explanatory value for those outcomes. The paradigm descriptions and operator profiles may remain useful; the proposed attribution doesn’t.

These tests require no new simulation or proof. They require independent paradigm identification, operator profiling, opportunity measurement, and outcome coding.
# Conclusion
The proposal is cumulative rather than a new theory of grammar. It adopts Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft’s domain–frame paradigm architecture as a scaffold, while declining to make it a general definition of grammatical status. Paradigm identification is categorical for this comparison; public-update contribution is the separately measured, graded operator profile.

Clausal architecture can host high-profile paradigms for clause type, commitment, role allocation, scope, or reference tracking. Tone, gesture, particles, word order, and inflection can realize their values. Complementation can instead impose a community-specific constructional convention, as with _depend on_, without configuring public update. One high-profile paradigm never makes every constraint on its containing construction, or on clause structure as a whole, profile highly.

The empirical contribution is the repair-locus hypothesis. Within an identified paradigm, the wrong-value versus value-preserving-exponent difference checks the manipulation; the sole confirmatory claim is that this difference increases with the frozen operator profile across paradigms. Category placement, profile aggregation, and outcome coding precede the test. If the interaction disappears after the pre-specified structural and social rivals are matched or accounted for, the paradigm and profile descriptions may survive, but the explanatory hypothesis fails.

Constructional embedding, value selection, exponent licensing, opportunity, schooling, and sanction remain rival or complementary causes. A locally redundant concord marker can belong to a high-profile reference-tracking system; *_goed_ and *_le hiver_ can preserve an intended value while selecting a lexeme-specific or morphophonologically excluded allomorph. The account proposes no corrective-control mechanism (Weinberger 2026; Reynolds 2026b). Grammaticalization and related discourse-functional research supports recurrent recruitment into interactionally consequential functions (Waltereit 2011; Degand and Evers-Vermeul 2015), but the stronger suggestion that public-update work preferentially creates paradigms remains an unconfirmed historical hypothesis.

# When clause structure is judged like tense and agreement
## Paradigm structure, public update, and repair locus
Brett Reynolds — Humber Polytechnic & University of Toronto
## Abstract
Why do some readily understood mismatches prompt clarification of a speaker’s move, while others prompt correction of a form, construction, or style? I adopt Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft’s paradigm architecture as a structural scaffold: a domain-bound, semantically framed, closed alternative set in which a value has to be selected. I then assign each such paradigm an independently measured **operator profile**: the degree to which its values conventionally configure clause type, commitment, participant linking, or reference tracking. The **operator-profile hypothesis** predicts a repair-locus interaction. The wrong-value versus value-preserving-exponent contrast is a manipulation check; the confirmatory prediction is that its update-directed repair difference increases with the pre-assigned operator profile. Category placement, profile scoring, and the repair codebook are fixed before outcomes are examined. If the predicted difference disappears after structural and social rivals are accounted for, the descriptive profile may survive, but the explanatory hypothesis fails.
