# When clause structure is judged like tense and agreement
## A paradigm-based repair hypothesis

Brett Reynolds — Humber Polytechnic & University of Toronto

## Abstract

Why do readily understood mismatches in clause structure, inflection,
wording, accent, and style attract different responses? Building on
Heltoft’s functional programme and Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft’s paradigm
architecture, I separate paradigm identification, public-update profile,
mismatch locus, and response cause. The **operator-profile hypothesis**
predicts that the repair-locus difference between wrong-value and
value-preserving-exponent mismatches grows with a profile fixed independently
across paradigms. The profile measures effects on move type, commitment,
discourse continuity, and response relevance. Identification, profiling, rival
measures, and outcome coding precede the test. If general interpretive
consequence, discursive secondary status, structural licensing, or social
sanction subsumes the interaction, the explanatory hypothesis fails even if
the descriptive profile survives.

**Keywords:** grammaticality; operator profile; public update; repair;
paradigm; clausal architecture

# Selective grammaticality and public accountability

Some mismatches are treated as straightforward errors of “grammar”:
wrong agreement, illicit clausal morphosyntax, incompatible tense–aspect
morphology, missing obligatory evidentials, or mis-selected
interrogative particles in systems where such particles are required.
Other mismatches are treated as infelicities of social positioning: an
accent that indexes the wrong stance, a style that fails to fit the
activity type, or a form that sounds “too formal” or “too intimate”.
Still others are treated as matters of lexical choice: poor perhaps, but
not illicit.[^1]

A familiar temptation is to reduce this to a contrast between
“structure” and “use”: morphosyntax counts as grammar; accent and
lexical choice don’t. But this gloss is neither typologically adequate
nor explanatory. Many languages use prosody or tone as obligatory
exponents of grammatical contrasts (Hyman 2011; Ladd 2008). Many
grammatical categories aren’t universal: a language may lack tense
(Comrie 1985) or number (Corbett 2000) while sustaining categorical
judgments elsewhere. And lexical material can be recruited into closed
grammatical paradigms, with constraints indistinguishable from
inflectional morphology (Hopper and Traugott 2003; Bybee 2010).

Why do communities treat some recoverable departures as problems in
grammatical value while locating others in wording, accent, or style?

I argue that one important part of this difference tracks a difference
in <span class="smallcaps">value</span>: what a contrast does in a
system and what it rules in or out for uptake.[^2] Consider polarity:
choosing *not* over an affirmative changes what inferences are licensed.
Choosing an interrogative particle rather than a declarative ending
changes what kind of move the utterance counts as. Such contrasts can
help determine how a contribution enters the evolving discourse record.
I call that conventional contribution a
<span class="smallcaps">public-update role</span>. The question pursued
here is whether its strength, measured independently within an
established paradigm, predicts the locus of later comprehension trouble
and repair.

The account doesn’t explain every sharp judgment. A community can
exclude a construction such as \**depend of* $`+`$
<span class="smallcaps">obj</span> without any public-update value being
wrong. Conversely, an intended value can be preserved while its
realization is excluded: \**goed* expresses past time through a
lexeme-inappropriate allomorph, and a concord error can be locally
redundant while violating an established pattern. Paradigm
identification, operator profiling, constructional licensing, token
value, and exponent choice remain separate. A high profile also doesn’t
establish what caused a particular judgment or repair. That attribution
requires an independent comparison.

The account builds on established work. Heltoft’s programme separates
grammaticality from comprehensibility, analyzes grammatical resources
for illocutionary and interlocutionary organization, and treats
expression and language-specific content paradigmatically (Heltoft 1981,
2005; Hansen and Heltoft 2011; Jakobsen and Heltoft 2012).
Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft extend domain-bound paradigms beyond
morphology to word order and constructions (Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft
2015); Boye and Harder instead make closedness incidental to grammatical
status (Boye and Harder 2012). Interactional research has likewise long
treated grammar as a resource for sequential action and repair (Fox et
al. 1996; Muntigl and Ventola 2010). What remains open is whether
paradigms that more strongly configure public update show a distinctive
response to value errors, once exponent choice, constructional
exclusion, general interpretive consequence, discursive status, and
correction practice are kept apart. The paper turns that residual
question into a prospective cross-paradigm test; it reports no repair
experiment as a completed result.

The answer to the title question is conditional rather than
substance-based. Clause pattern, tense, agreement, tone, or gesture can
pattern alike when they realize values in established paradigms with
strong public-update profiles. A sharp response elsewhere may instead be
due to constructional licensing, conventional form, schooling, or social
sanction. The test below asks whether the profile distinguishes those
possibilities in held-out data.

## Public accountability

A deliberately non-idealized view of grammar starts from a community’s
repertoire of conventionalized form–value relations rather than strings
or derivations alone. Those relations enable coordination in interaction
(Heltoft 1981; Hansen and Heltoft 2011; Clark and Brennan 1991).

Consider two schematic misfires. In the first, a speaker uses a
same-subject marker where a different-subject marker is required, and
the hearer responds “Wait, who did what?”; or the speaker selects a
declarative complement where an interrogative one is intended, and the
hearer asks “Did you mean *that* or *whether*?” In the second, a speaker
uses an accent or register that doesn’t fit the setting; the hearer
understands the utterance but asks “Why are you talking like that?” The
imagined replies aren’t data. They distinguish candidate response loci:
clarification of the contribution in the first case, negotiation of
stance or persona in the second.

I call this contrast <span class="smallcaps">public
accountability</span>. Interlocutors don’t merely exchange signals; they
make commitments mutually recognizable and ratifiable (Stalnaker 1978,
2002; Clark and Brennan 1991; Clark 1996). Some grammatical contrasts
conventionally help file a contribution in that public record: as a
question rather than an assertion, as committing one participant rather
than another, as maintaining or shifting a discourse referent, or as
making a particular next response relevant. I use
<span class="smallcaps">public update</span> in this narrower sense. It
excludes effects on truth conditions, interpretation, social relations,
or conduct merely because those effects are public.

Repair practices make this selectivity visible. When an utterance isn’t
understood, or is understood under a suspect analysis, interlocutors
systematically mobilize repair (Schegloff et al. 1977). Repair is
evidence about which dimension mattered in the interaction; it isn’t
part of the definition of that dimension.

Public accountability isn’t the same as public consequence. Accent and
style are central to social meaning, but they aren’t normally
grammatical instructions for what an utterance counts as in the way that
clause type, polarity, or evidential marking can be (Eckert 2008, 2012;
Agha 2007; Coupland 2007). Lexical choice is similarly accountable in an
ethical or epistemic sense: one can be challenged for using a slur or a
misleading term. Those reactions differ from repair of the grammatical
value that organizes the update.

Established paradigms can be assigned stronger or weaker public-update
profiles. I retain <span class="smallcaps">operator stratum</span> as a
mnemonic for the higher-profile region of that ordering, not as a second
membership category or a new definition of grammar. No threshold enters
the empirical test.

# Paradigms, profiles, and the hypothesis

## The operator-profile hypothesis

Three parts have to remain separate.

<span class="smallcaps">(i) Paradigm.</span> I adopt the architecture of
Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft (2015, 262–63) as the structural scaffold.
A paradigm has a closed, or bounded, set of alternatives, a recurring
syntagmatic <span class="smallcaps">domain</span>, and a
language-specific semantic <span class="smallcaps">frame</span> within
which the alternatives contrast. Selection of a value is obligatory
whenever the domain applies. Obligatoriness concerns value selection,
not an overt segment: a zero form, word-order pattern, intonational
contour, or construction can select a value. Closure, domain, frame, and
obligatory selection are the study-inclusion criteria used here.
Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft also require an asymmetry between marked
and unmarked members. Because the repair hypothesis doesn’t depend on
that asymmetry, the primary comparison records markedness without making
it an admission rule; a required sensitivity analysis restores their
full five-criterion definition.

I treat paradigmhood as categorical only for study inclusion. A
candidate either clears the structural requirements in the conditioned
repertoire or remains outside the comparison. Grammatical categories in
general can still have prototype and radial organization, as Hansen and
Heltoft (2011, vols 1, 39–41) explicitly allow. Boundary uncertainty
belongs in the evidence for the study decision, not in a second taxonomy
of “operator-like resources”. This restriction prevents an open set of
interjections or discourse markers from entering the stratum merely
because some of its members matter to interaction.

<span class="smallcaps">(ii) Operator profile.</span> An identified
paradigm receives a graded profile for the extent to which its values
conventionally configure four aspects of public update: move type,
commitment holder or source, discourse-participant or referent
continuity, and response relevance (Stalnaker 2002; Farkas and Bruce
2010; Murray 2014). The components form a profile vector rather than
four additional membership conditions. Clause type, illocutionary force,
evidential anchoring, and switch-reference are strong candidates;
thematic-role assignment by itself isn’t. The profile applies to the
paradigm across its ordinary occasions of use, not to the information
carried by every token. An agreement marker can be redundant in one
clause and decisive in another while its paradigm remains available for
participant or reference tracking.

The profile concerns licensed update types rather than whether a claim
is true or successful. A speaker can say *It’s true* of a false
situation: the assertion fails epistemically, but affirmative polarity
and declarative force are licensed. By contrast, past-tense *Yesterday
the bill passed* selects a past-time anchor, while \**Yesterday the bill
pass* can preserve that intended value but fail to supply its licensed
finite exponent. Temporal value and exponent licensing are distinct.

Context selects among independently licensed values, constructional
frames, and ellipsis patterns; it doesn’t create a form–value relation
ad hoc. *I have five years* can succeed as an elliptical reply to “How
long do you have left?” because the frame supplies the relevant
duration. The same reply to “How old are you?” fails in ordinary English
because age is expressed through the *be* frame, not because the hearer
lacks the intended meaning.

<span class="smallcaps">(iii) Operator-profile hypothesis.</span> The
empirical claim begins only after paradigm identification and profiling.
For identified paradigms in a specified community repertoire and
activity range, observing a stronger frozen profile licenses the
expectation of a larger held-out difference in update-directed repair
between wrong-value and value-preserving-exponent mismatches. The
minimum effect, uncertainty rule, and any narrower fallback scope are
fixed before outcomes are coded. The within-paradigm contrast alone
checks the manipulation because the item definitions already distinguish
value change from value preservation. Repair and comprehension are
outcomes, not profile criteria. A high profile can fail to predict them
without being redescribed after the fact.

Opportunity density remains separate. It affects how much evidence
speakers encounter and how strongly competitors preempt one another,
which can sharpen licensing profiles and resistance to exposure. A
moribund paradigm can retain a high operator profile while its judgments
grow less concentrated. A frequent collocation can be sharply
conventional without forming a paradigm or receiving an operator
profile.

The three-part architecture is synchronic. Grammaticalization and
related discourse-functional research documents the recruitment of
lexical and constructional material into grammatical and discourse
functions, including reorganization into language-specific semantic
frames (Hopper and Traugott 2003; Traugott and Dasher 2002; Lehmann
2015; Waltereit 2011; Degand and Evers-Vermeul 2015; Jakobsen and
Heltoft 2012). It doesn’t thereby establish that public-update work
preferentially creates paradigms. That stronger historical claim
requires separate comparative evidence and can’t rescue a failed
synchronic prediction. Once a paradigm exists, dense competitor evidence
may stabilize its licensed exponents; population persistence requires a
further account of adoption and retention (Reynolds 2026a).

Two dissociations follow. First, a locally redundant token can belong to
a high-profile paradigm, as some agreement markers may. Second, an
interactionally consequential resource can remain outside the paradigm
comparison. English *wow*, for example, can configure assessment and
uptake while competing with an expandable, gradient family of
exclamatory resources (Ameka 1992).

Paradigms can be realized morphologically, syntactically,
phonologically, prosodically, gesturally, or lexically. What matters for
identification is the domain–frame organization, not the substance of
the exponent. What matters for the operator profile is the conventional
update contribution. Morphology and clausal architecture are privileged
only where they instantiate that architecture.

Phonemic contrasts illustrate the division. They can form organized
systems that keep expressions apart, but they don’t thereby configure an
utterance as a public move. Paradigm structure and operator profile ask
different questions.

Seven levels remain distinct: the historical recruitment of a resource;
the present identification of its paradigm; its operator profile; the
construction that embeds it; the value selected in a token; the exponent
that realizes that value; and the extent to which a repertoire’s
architecture is organized by high-profile paradigms. One high-profile
paradigm inside a construction doesn’t give every constraint on that
construction a high profile, and a construction that hosts several such
paradigms doesn’t make every syntactic mismatch a high-profile value
error.

Identification, profiling, and attribution are different tests.
Identification asks whether a contrast forms a paradigm. Profiling asks
how strongly that paradigm configures public update. Attribution asks
whether the profiled role, rather than constructional exclusion,
exponent licensing, frequency, processing difficulty, prescriptive
training, or social sanction, produced an observed judgment or repair. A
high-profile paradigm can be corrected mainly because of schooling; an
open discourse resource can prompt update-directed repair; and a
construction can realize a high-profile contrast while imposing
unrelated constraints of its own. The first two rows come first, but
neither explains a token outcome by itself. The remaining rows locate
rival causes for the attribution test.

Heltoft’s functional programme is the closest conceptual predecessor.
The 1981 analysis explicitly denies a direct relation between
grammaticality and comprehensibility while asking how conventional form
identifies the communicative dimension under dispute (Heltoft 1981). The
later modality analysis gives mood a role in illocutionary
responsibility and dialogic particles a distinct role in
interlocutionary organization (Heltoft 2005). Hansen and Heltoft (2011,
vols 1, 32–63) make pragmatic and textual distinctions part of grammar’s
coded sign system.

Across the grammar’s descriptive chapters, illocutionary-force
indicators and metacommunicative trouble receive dedicated analysis
(Hansen and Heltoft 2011, vols 1, 43–63), along with topological mood
(Hansen and Heltoft 2011, vols 2, 725–767) and dialogic particles
(Hansen and Heltoft 2011, vols 2, 1033–1108), all within
language-specific paradigmatic organization. Jakobsen and Heltoft (2012)
give the convergence its clearest compact form: separate mood paradigms
organize directive force, speaker vouching, and responsibility for
quotation through their own domains and semantic frames. Paradigm
structure and public interaction are inherited commitments of this
paper.

Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft (2015, 276–78) provide the closest
architectural precedent. Their Danish V2 analysis treats word order as
an obligatory assertivity paradigm, and their constructional cases
extend paradigmatic organization beyond morphology. The three-volume
*Grammatik over det Danske Sprog* (GDS) anticipates the proposed
intersection extensionally in several Danish systems, while the 2015
chapter supplies its general paradigm architecture. Neither work defines
degree of public-update contribution as a cross-paradigm profile,
distinguishes that profile from the locus and cause of a token response,
or uses the distinction for a frozen comparative prediction. The
residual contribution is both theoretical and empirical: a
reclassification of established paradigms for one explanatory purpose,
plus a test that can make that reclassification fail.

Boye and Harder (2012, 1, 7–8, 30–31) supply the principled opposition.
On their account, closed paradigms and obligatoriness are incidental to
grammatical status and grammaticalization, while conventional
ancillarity and discursive secondary status are central; focusability
and addressability provide synchronic diagnostics. Secondary status is
relative to one or more syntagmatically related expressions, so it isn’t
equivalent either to being non-at-issue or to configuring public update.
Nothing here decides their general dispute with paradigm-based
definitions. For the narrower repair hypothesis, their diagnostics
become rival measures: if discursive secondary status subsumes the
profile interaction, public-update profiling adds no explanation.

The interactional literature also sets a demanding baseline. Muntigl and
Ventola (2010, 105–6, 111–19) show that nominalization, argument
realization, and Theme selection can redirect later talk. On the
definition adopted here, those productive resources are candidates for
nonoperator controls wherever Stage 1 doesn’t identify a small closed
paradigm; that classification is mine, not theirs. Fox et al. (1996,
192–95) connect syntax with same-turn self-repair across English and
Japanese, but don’t pre-assign operator profiles or contrast a wrong
value with a value-preserving exponent error. Their work is a
methodological precedent, not evidence that the prediction is already
true.

My use of <span class="smallcaps">operator</span> remains continuous
with Role and Reference Grammar, Functional Grammar, and Functional
Discourse Grammar, where aspect, tense, modality, negation, and
illocutionary force are operators over layers of clause structure
(Hengeveld 1989; Van Valin and LaPolla 1997; Van Valin 2005; Hengeveld
and Mackenzie 2008; Bentley et al. 2023). The operator profile isn’t a
new clausal layer. It can apply across morphological, syntactic, tonal,
prosodic, gestural, and lexical exponents.

Recent interactional-syntax proposals make the difference sharper.
Wiltschko’s interactional spine grammatically represents speaker,
addressee, grounding, and response structure, while Krifka’s layered
analyses distinguish judgment, commitment, and speech-act projections
(Wiltschko 2025; Krifka 2025). The operator-profile hypothesis concerns
comparative profiles and held-out consequences across expressive
substances. Levinson’s warning that speech acts can’t be reduced to
context change is compatible with this scope (Levinson 2026): a
public-update contribution is one conventional resource for action, not
a complete theory of speech acts or conversation.

The cases make two boundaries especially clear.

Slurs satisfy the public-consequence test but usually fail paradigm
identification. A racial slur used as a vocative, or a misogynistic slur
used as a predicate, can radically alter the social action performed and
can make the speaker answerable for that action. But the utterance
normally remains structurally fileable as an address, assertion,
accusation, or description. The move is morally or socially
impermissible even though the hearer can identify its update type. The
predicted response is sanction of the act or speaker, not repair of a
paradigm value.

Interjections can configure uptake, stance, attention, repair, or floor
management (Ameka 1992). English *huh?*, for example, can initiate
repair, while *wow* can display surprise or assessment; cross-linguistic
repair formats show recurrent functional organization without reducing
to a single closed grammatical value set (Dingemanse et al. 2014).

But many interjection tokens aren’t selected from a closed, domain-bound
set with obligatory value selection. They can be repeated, stretched,
blended, newly coined, or substituted without producing a categorical
licensing failure. They receive no operator profile in this
architecture. If an interjection-like resource becomes organized as a
paradigm, it can enter the comparison; uptake configuration alone isn’t
enough.

French \**le hiver* is a value-preserving form error within the
definite-determiner system. Its singular forms include *le*, *la*, and
elided *l’*, with gender and morphophonological restrictions. French
<span class="smallcaps">h muet</span> words such as *hiver* ‘winter’
pattern with vowel-initial words and trigger elision, whereas
<span class="smallcaps">h aspiré</span> words block it (Boersma 2007).
The intended value in \**le hiver* is still definite reference. What
fails is its licensed realization: the required form is *l’hiver*. This
token establishes neither the containing system’s paradigm status nor
its operator profile.

\**Goed* preserves past time by taking the productive past exponent
*-ed* where this lexeme requires the suppletive past allomorph *went*.
The failure is lexeme-specific allomorph licensing. \**Depend of* $`+`$
<span class="smallcaps">obj</span> is analyzed at a different level.
Complementation is organized by community conventions that are partly
schematic and partly head-specific. A head can select a complement
category or function while narrowing its realization to a PP headed by
one or a few prepositions (Hansen and Heltoft 2011, vols 1, 81–111);
English *depend* licenses *on* or *upon*. \**Depend of* $`+`$
<span class="smallcaps">obj</span> is a constructional-licensing failure
in a dense niche, even though the dependency and intended meaning are
recoverable. No public-update value has been mis-set. Its sharp
correction illustrates why neither a categorical judgment nor a
form-focused repair can equate high operator profiles with
grammaticality as a whole.

Heltoft’s recent reanalysis of Danish case reinforces the need for this
separation: a paradigm’s content frame, its syntagmatic domain, the
exponents that realize its values, and socially conditioned variation in
their distribution aren’t interchangeable levels (Heltoft 2025). That
distinction supports the design architecture; it doesn’t supply evidence
for the predicted repair interaction.

These boundaries motivate the matched design in
§<a href="#sec:identifying-operators" data-reference-type="ref"
data-reference="sec:identifying-operators">2.4</a>: compare value and
value-preserving form mismatches within paradigms, then ask across
paradigms whether their repair-locus difference increases with a profile
fixed in advance. Historical recruitment can’t be added after the fact
to rescue a failed result.

## Identification and outcome protocol

The design has two preregistered stages. Freezing paradigm
identification and profile construction isn’t enough; the outcome
codebook also has to be fixed before responses are inspected. A
prospective elicitation study and a corpus study with disjoint profiling
and outcome partitions can implement the same logic.

<span class="smallcaps">Stage 1a: paradigm identification.</span>
Descriptive and corpus evidence first specifies the conditioned
repertoire, the recurring domain, the alternatives, and their semantic
frame. It then tests whether the set is closed and whether a value has
to be selected whenever the domain applies. Sharp judgments, correction,
and the later repair task are withheld. Tense–aspect systems, agreement
paradigms, switch-reference markers, interrogative particles, and
complementizer inventories are candidates, not presumed cases.

Closure requires a bounded value inventory that exhausts the choices
found in the specified domain across additional corpus samples and,
where needed, consultant elicitation. Productive new exponents can
realize existing values; a productive route to new values, or an open
residual “other” response, defeats closure. The conditioned repertoire
and stopping rule are fixed before the outcome task.

Obligatory selection requires every independently accepted token in the
domain to receive one value from that inventory. A zero realization
counts only where its distribution contrasts with overt members in the
same frame and independently changes the frame interpretation. Mere lack
of an overt form doesn’t establish a zero value; it may instead show
that the proposed domain isn’t active.

Dropping Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft’s markedness criterion enlarges
the possible comparison set. Each study reports the primary
four-criterion analysis and a sensitivity analysis restricted to
paradigms that also show their marked–unmarked asymmetry. A result that
disappears in the strict subset supports only the relaxed comparison and
can’t be attributed to their full architecture.

<span class="smallcaps">Stage 1b: operator profiling.</span> For each
identified paradigm, preregister the update variables its values might
affect: move type, commitment holder or source, discourse-participant or
referent continuity, and response relevance. A separate sample or corpus
partition supplies minimal value contrasts while holding at-issue
content as constant as the language permits. Classification,
interpretation, or independently coded distributional measures estimate
how much changing the value changes each component.

The result is a four-component vector. For the single confirmatory
coefficient, the primary scalar summary is the equally weighted
root-mean-square of the standardized component effects; this measures
the magnitude of the update shift without requiring a paradigm to affect
all four components. Zero effects remain zero rather than being dropped
as “inapplicable”. Indicator coding, scaling, missing-data rules,
contexts, and uncertainty aggregation are frozen before repair outcomes
are inspected, and component-wise results remain visible.

Stage 2 uses a different task format and new items, such as open
next-turn responses, production repair, or a held-out interactional
corpus partition, to reduce shared-task-method variance. Two theoretical
rivals are measured in Stage 1: a broad interpretive-consequence rating
and Boye and Harder’s discursive-secondary-status diagnostics. A
next-turn distribution can contribute to Stage 1b or serve as an
outcome, but not both. If general interpretive consequence or discursive
secondary status subsumes the profile interaction, the operator-specific
attribution fails. Broad scope and interpersonal consequence are
insufficient on their own.

<span class="smallcaps">Design covariates.</span> Opportunity is the
number of occasions on which a paradigm value and its competitors could
plausibly have been selected. Exponent frequency, surprisal,
constructional frame, phonetic salience, processing load, schooling or
norm exposure, and social sanction are measured separately. These are
commitments for matching or adjustment, not controls the theoretical
paper claims already to have achieved. Opportunity predicts the
concentration of licensing evidence, not paradigm status or operator
profile (Reynolds 2026a).

<span class="smallcaps">Stage 2: frozen outcomes.</span> Before coding
held-out responses, define <span class="smallcaps">update
confusion</span>, <span class="smallcaps">update-directed repair</span>,
<span class="smallcaps">form correction</span>,
<span class="smallcaps">constructional correction</span>,
<span class="smallcaps">social sanction</span>, and
<span class="smallcaps">unresolved trouble</span>. Update-directed
repair targets move type, commitment, discourse-participant or referent
continuity, or response relevance; form correction supplies a licensed
exponent and preserves the intended update. Double-code ambiguous
responses and report reliability rather than forcing every case into one
bin. The confirmatory comparison includes only paradigms for which
independent evidence supports both a genuine value mismatch and a
plausible value-preserving exponent mismatch; the design can’t
manufacture the latter merely to retain a paradigm.

The sole confirmatory test is the cross-paradigm interaction: does the
wrong-value versus value-preserving-exponent difference in
update-directed repair increase with the frozen operator profile after
the measured design covariates are matched or accounted for? The
within-paradigm difference is a manipulation check, repair locus is the
primary outcome, and held-out comprehension is secondary. If the
interaction is absent or smaller than the pre-specified minimum effect
under the uncertainty rule, the operator-profile hypothesis fails even
if paradigm descriptions and operator profiles remain useful.

# How clausal architecture hosts candidate paradigms

Treating clausal architecture as a matter of arranging words gives an
English-biased picture. Across languages, clause type can be realized
through particles, verbal morphology, word order, intonation, and manual
or nonmanual marking in signed languages (Dryer 2013; Ladd 2008; Zeshan
2004). The relevant generalization concerns operator packaging rather
than linearization.

Clausal architecture can host high-profile paradigms in clause type,
argument linking, dependency scope, and reference tracking. Other
syntactic constraints reflect expression-shape regularities, processing
limits, or constructional conventionalization. A high-profile paradigm
inside a construction doesn’t make every constraint on that construction
profile highly.

The paper’s projective claim runs from the frozen profile of an
identified paradigm in a specified repertoire to its held-out
repair-locus difference for a stated activity and population (Goodman
1955; Reynolds 2026b). Its warrant comes from the pre-specified
comparison and rivals, not from the precision of that declaration or
from an identified mechanism. Failure demotes the explanatory hypothesis
without erasing the paradigm description or authorizing post-hoc
narrowing.

Clausal architecture supplies candidate paradigms in at least four
domains. None receives a high profile from its grammatical label alone.

<span class="smallcaps">Clause type and response space.</span>
Interrogatives, imperatives, declaratives, and related types are
candidates for different update potentials and norms for a relevant next
move. Languages grammaticalize interrogatives using heterogeneous
resources (particles, morphology, intonation, or combinations) (Dryer
2013; Ladd 2008). Paradigm identification tests their organization;
profiling measures how much a value change alters response relevance. A
wrong clause-type value in a high-profile paradigm should increase
update confusion as a secondary outcome and update-directed repair in
the confirmatory comparison.

<span class="smallcaps">Participant linking and continuity.</span> Case,
agreement, and voice can help identify which discourse participant is
maintained, promoted, backgrounded, or linked across an utterance (Van
Valin and LaPolla 1997; Corbett 2006). Merely changing a thematic
relation or truth condition doesn’t establish a public-update role.
Profiling tests continuity and public role allocation separately from
conceptual interpretation; the labels “case”, “agreement”, and “voice”
supply no repair prediction.

<span class="smallcaps">Dependency management and scope.</span> Some
complement contrasts package how information is nested, attributed, and
scoped. *Kim knows that Pat left* presents an embedded proposition,
whereas *Kim wonders whether Pat left* opens an embedded question.
\**Kim believes whether Pat left* misselects an interrogative complement
for the intended belief attribution. Here constructional licensing and
public-update value intersect: the matrix head restricts the complement
construction, and the complementizer helps identify whether the embedded
contribution is propositional or interrogative (Stalnaker 2002; Farkas
and Bruce 2010).

<span class="smallcaps">Reference tracking across clauses.</span>
Switch-reference is a strong paradigm candidate: a morphosyntactic
system signals whether the subject of one clause is the same as or
different from that of another (Gijn 2016). The adopted criteria
establish the paradigm; separate profiling tests whether changing a
value alters tracking across the multi-clause sequence. The relevant
pivot varies across systems.

To illustrate the form of the claim, consider Amele, a Papuan language
in which dependent switch-reference verbs in clause chains mark whether
the relevant argument is maintained or changed across adjacent clauses
(Roberts 1987, 1988, 2023). Roberts’s examples contrast forms such as
*h-um-ig*
‘come-<span class="smallcaps">ss.seq</span>-1<span class="smallcaps">sg.nom</span>’
and *ho-co-min*
‘come-<span class="smallcaps">ds.seq</span>-1<span class="smallcaps">sg.nom</span>’
before a final tense-marked verb. The forms supply clause-linking
values. If independent profiling assigns the paradigm a high score, the
wrong marker should be more likely to disturb clause-linking
interpretation than a matched value-preserving exponent error. Observing
a repair wouldn’t by itself establish that attribution.

A second, different kind of illustration comes from Japanese
sentence-final particles. In many registers, sentence-final *ka*
conventionally marks interrogative force, while declaratives are
unmarked or carry other particles; intonation interacts with these
choices, and colloquial speech allows more variation (Kuno 1973;
Shibatani 1990). This conditioned clause-typing use, not every lexical
or constructional use of *ka*, helps determine what kind of move the
utterance counts as and what responses are licensed. A clause-typing
paradigm can require selection of a value without requiring overt *ka*
in every construction. Its absence may select a declarative, an
intonation-marked question, or another licensed alternative; its
presence may select an unintended question or be blocked in combination
with other particles, intonation, or morphosyntax. Paradigm-level
obligatory choice doesn’t entail construction-level obligatoriness of
one overt exponent (Reynolds 2026a).

Together, the Amele and Japanese cases separate two claims that are
easily conflated. A paradigm requires value selection in its domain; a
particular construction may still permit several values and several
exponents. A high operator profile predicts only the matched,
update-specific repair difference once a mismatch has been created, not
whether the construction creates one. In both cases, clausal
architecture matters by packaging values for public update; syntax
needn’t be insulated from interaction or wholly composed of operators.

Complementation also shows why community convention and public-update
value remain separate. Complement constructions are schematic: they
assign categories and grammatical functions, while particular heads may
narrow a complement to a clause of a certain type or to a PP headed by
one of a small set of prepositions. English *put* conventionally
requires a locative complement, and *depend* selects *on* or *upon*.
These are constructional-licensing facts even when no public-update
contrast is involved. Complementation can also host high-profile
paradigms where its values configure commitment, clause type, scope, or
participant linking, as in *that* vs. *whether*. Schematic inheritance
and lexically narrowed realization coexist.

## Tense and number without universality

Tense and number enter grammaticality judgments through
language-specific paradigms and constructional requirements. Only an
independent operator profile establishes whether one of those paradigms
also configures public update. In a language without obligatory tense
marking, the mere absence of tense morphology supplies no tense-value
error. Other constructional requirements can still make a clause
unlicensed. This account avoids a common slippage between salience in
familiar European languages and cross-linguistic necessity (Haspelmath
2007).

Tense marking is an especially clear example. Where tense is
grammaticalized, it can form a paradigm for temporal anchoring;
independent profiling then tests how strongly its values configure
public update (Comrie 1985). In languages without obligatory tense,
temporal anchoring can instead depend on aspect, temporal adverbials,
discourse structure, and contextual inference (Lin 2006; Tonhauser
2011). Speakers in the latter case shouldn’t experience missing tense
marking as a tense-value error, because their repertoire contains no
obligatory tense contrast to leave unsatisfied.

Number behaves analogously only where independent evidence shows that a
number paradigm contributes to reference tracking in public commitments.
If a construction also makes number obligatory in agreement or nominal
morphology, a mismatch becomes a paradigmatic licensing failure. In
languages where number is optional or limited, or where its
public-update contribution isn’t established, number mismatches can have
a different status (Corbett 2000).

Operator repertoires vary across languages. Licensing failures can
target a construction, a paradigm value, or its exponent; only
independently high profiles predict the proposed update-directed repair
difference.

## Gender and noun-class concord as a hard case

Gender and noun-class concord are a limiting case for the account. With
nouns whose class isn’t independently informative in context (including
many inanimates in familiar sex-based systems), a concord mismatch may
have little or no effect on the interpretation of that token: \**le
table* normally doesn’t leave the hearer wondering which object is
meant. But it’s treated as a central grammar error where gender concord
is obligatory (Corbett 1991). The point concerns local redundancy in any
noun-class system, not inanimacy as a universal organizing principle.

That pattern would be hard to explain if public-update contribution were
required in every token. The relevant question is instead whether a
specified concord paradigm, in the constructional domain under study,
contributes to participant or reference tracking across its occasions of
use. Where independent evidence establishes that role, a locally
redundant exponent still belongs to the same paradigm. High opportunity
density can then concentrate evidence for its licensed forms, so a wrong
exponent can be corrected even when the local referent is obvious.
Evidence from another agreement domain can’t supply the paradigm-level
role by inheritance.

Public-update role remains empirically constrained. The Mahowald-style
point is local redundancy: agreement can be redundant in most clauses
while remaining decisive in the minority where lexical semantics,
animacy, or discourse expectations underdetermine reference (Mahowald et
al. 2023). Whether a particular gender system has that paradigm-level
role is an empirical question for comprehension probes, not a fact
licensed by the existence of categorical judgments. A diachronic claim
that reference tracking favours the recruitment of concord systems
remains a separate hypothesis.

The operational prediction has two levels. A concord manipulation on a
locally uninformative noun may have no measurable effect on that token’s
interpretation, while the independent profiling task still finds a
paradigm-level contribution to reference tracking. Held-out probes can
then test whether wrong values produce the predicted update-confusion
profile. Sharp correction alone can’t settle either question, because
constructional licensing and schooling can also produce it.

# Why accent and lexical choice are usually different

Accent and lexical choice typically have different kinds of value.

## Indexical value and negotiability

Accent, style, and register contribute indexical value: they position
speakers relative to social categories, stances, and activity types
(Silverstein 1976; Eckert 2008; Agha 2007; Coupland 2007). Indexical
value is often rich and structured, but it isn’t typically implemented
as a small closed paradigm tied to public update. Interlocutors can
reinterpret an accent shift as play, accommodation, quotation, or stance
work. The contrast remains socially accountable without thereby forming
a high-profile paradigm.

Indexicality can be obligatory in social practice without forming a
high-profile paradigm. Much indexical meaning is *meta-pragmatic*: it
becomes salient through social ideologies and enregisterment processes
(Agha 2007; Eckert 2012). That makes it both powerful and variable, but
it rarely supplies a value that has to be selected for the utterance to
count as a recognized move.

Japanese polite *desu/masu* and plain style provide a useful closure
test within a conditioned repertoire. Here *desu/masu* is
sociolinguistic shorthand: *-masu* marks polite verbal predicates, while
*desu* is its polite copular or predicative counterpart. Speakers shift
between polite and plain forms to present identities and stances within
one interaction (Cook 2008; Ide 2005). Closure doesn’t make the
politeness contrast profile highly. Unless an independent measure shows
that it configures participant roles or update type, a correction of
speech style is better attributed to indexical fit, constructional
convention, or schooling. This differs from \**le hiver*, where definite
reference is selected and only its phonologically licensed form fails;
that example establishes no operator profile of its own. T/V pronouns
are mixed cases because a single form can realize addressee reference
and person–number features while also indexing social relations.

## Conceptual value and open classes

Open-class lexical items primarily contribute conceptual content, and
their inventories tolerate expansion and innovation. Lexical choice can
be infelicitous, misleading, or socially harmful without mis-setting a
grammatical update. Lexically conditioned patterns can still be
categorical: a head can impose constructional restrictions on its
complements, as *depend* does, while the open lexical inventory remains
outside the paradigm comparison.

“Open-endedness” has two dimensions that matter here. Lexical
inventories are open in the sense of expandable type-sets. Compositional
semantics is open-ended in a different sense: unbounded productivity
from combining a finite stock. Operators lack the first kind of
openness, but they participate fully in the second: negation scopes,
evidentials anchor commitments, and clause type constrains response
space. Closed inventories make it easier for repeated choices to
concentrate licensing expectations, but closure alone doesn’t supply an
operator role.

That difference predicts why a wrong value in a high-profile paradigm
and a poor lexical choice can invite different responses even when both
are understood. The former can alter the public classification of the
move; the latter usually invites negotiation about content.
Constructionally excluded lexical combinations remain a third
possibility.

## Cross-substance realization

Expressive substance doesn’t determine an operator profile. Phonological
and lexical material can realize values in an identified paradigm;
profiling separately tests the paradigm’s public-update role.

Systems in which tone marks tense, focus, or polarity are candidates for
paradigms with phonological exponents (Hyman 2011). If the adopted
paradigm criteria hold and independent profiling assigns a high
public-update contribution, wrong tonal values should attract more
update-directed repair than value-preserving tonal realization errors.
Intonational contours can similarly realize values in an identified
clause-typing paradigm; the same conditional prediction distinguishes
them from accent (Ladd 2008). This aligns with work on rising
declaratives and intonational commitments, where intonational tunes
modify context-update functions rather than merely colouring
pronunciation (Gunlogson 2003; Rudin 2022).

Signed languages supply candidates in another modality. In American Sign
Language, a yes/no question can be marked by nonmanual cues such as brow
raise over the relevant clause. Within an independently identified and
profiled clause-typing paradigm, scopal nonmanual marking can realize
the relevant value without a segmental word (Sandler and Lillo-Martin
2006).

Lexical items can come to realize values in paradigms through
grammaticalization. Their meanings may become more schematic or
conventionalized, while they become more tightly integrated into
paradigms and constructional distributions (Hopper and Traugott 2003;
Lehmann 2015; Bybee 2010). Function words, particles, auxiliaries,
complementizers, and polarity items can realize values in high-profile
paradigms. Lexical form alone is insufficient. A preposition selected by
a particular head may realize a community-specific complement
construction without configuring public update. Its lexical source,
constructional licensing, and operator profile are separate questions.

Phonotactics sharpens the same point. Phonotactic constraints are
conventionalized and often treated categorically (“not a possible
word”), but they don’t satisfy the public-update condition. They
regulate expression shape rather than the grammatical value of the move.
The likely repair target differs: recognizability for phonotactics,
update value for operators, conventional form for constructional or
exponent licensing, and content or stance for many lexical and pragmatic
mismatches. These are tendencies to test, not exclusive response bins.

## Scope and local redundancy

Operators often affect how other material is interpreted. Once an
utterance is marked interrogative, the remaining material is most
typically understood as the content of a question rather than an
assertion.[^3] Polarity changes the inferential contribution of material
in its scope; case and agreement can constrain role assignment.
Operators don’t always reduce more uncertainty than lexical items:
predicates such as *congratulate* and *accuse* can reshape continuations
dramatically.

Scope can make the consequences of a value error non-local. A wrong
clause-type, polarity, or reference-tracking value may prompt reanalysis
beyond its exponent, while a value-preserving exponent error need not.
But scope alone establishes neither an operator profile nor a processing
mechanism. The matched prediction is the error-locus difference
developed in
§<a href="#sec:identifying-operators" data-reference-type="ref"
data-reference="sec:identifying-operators">2.4</a> and tested in
§<a href="#sec:predictions" data-reference-type="ref"
data-reference="sec:predictions">7</a>.

This explains a puzzle: grammatical marking is often locally
redundant.[^4] Mahowald et al. (2023) show that across 30 languages,
subjecthood in simple transitive clauses can usually be inferred from
lexical content alone. Overt grammatical cues are redundant in a
majority of everyday cases, but decisive for the minority where lexical
semantics doesn’t settle the roles (reversible events, non-prototypical
assignments). Operators can be locally redundant but globally
update-relevant. Their contribution isn’t to maximize information in
each occurrence, but to supply a codebook whose values remain available
when context, lexical semantics, or discourse expectations
underdetermine the update.

Frequent, predictable grammatical material often undergoes phonetic
reduction (Bybee 2006). Reduced exponents can still belong to an
identified paradigm because its operator profile depends on the
paradigm’s contribution, not on phonetic invariance.

Opportunity structure supplies a separate prediction. Repeated,
attributable competitor choices can concentrate evidence for a licensing
boundary (Bybee 2006, 2010; Reynolds 2026a). Wrong values in
high-profile paradigms may prompt broader reanalysis of material in
their scope; value-preserving exponent errors instead predict form
correction with the intended update preserved. The claim concerns
response profiles, not an identified processing mechanism.

# Typological consequences: which paradigms receive high profiles

As a comparative concept, the operator profile identifies an
interactional role that can be realized by different structures in
different languages (Haspelmath 2007). Typological diversity concerns
which language-specific systems form paradigms and how strongly those
paradigms configure public update, not whether English categories are
universal.

Four domains are particularly instructive.

## Instructive domains of variation

<span class="smallcaps">Evidentiality and epistemic authority.</span> A
language-specific evidential system enters the comparison only if
closure, domain, semantic frame, and obligatory value selection are
independently established (Aikhenvald 2004). Profiling then tests
whether its values configure whose commitment is at issue and on what
evidential basis. If the profile is high and a construction requires a
particular value, the hypothesis predicts update-directed repair for
wrong values. Lexical or periphrastic evidential meanings can instead be
challenged as deceptive or inappropriate without establishing a paradigm
or its profile.

<span class="smallcaps">Egophoricity and perspective management.</span>
Language-specific perspective and ego-involvement systems are candidates
for paradigm identification (Tournadre 2008; Floyd et al. 2018). If all
four adopted criteria hold, profiling can test whether the values
configure whose epistemic position anchors a clause’s update potential.
A high frozen profile predicts that a constructionally induced wrong
value will receive more update-directed repair than a value-preserving
realization error. Such systems are candidates for high operator
profiles, not operators by semantic label alone.

Where perspective doesn’t form an identified, high-profile paradigm,
similar meanings remain expressible but the operator-profile hypothesis
makes no update-directed repair prediction.

<span class="smallcaps">Phonological exponents vs. accent.</span> Tonal
systems provide a strong test of the cross-substance claim (Hyman 2011).
Once a tonal tense, aspect, or polarity system is independently
identified as a paradigm and assigned a high profile, wrong tonal values
should pattern like wrong morphosyntactic values rather than accent
shifts. A language may distinguish tense values primarily through tonal
melodies on the verb (one pattern for remote past, another for recent
past), with minimal segmental differentiation between the forms.

A concrete case is Ngwi, a West-Coastal Bantu language whose
Tense–Aspect–Mood–Polarity system includes grammatical tone in a compact
set of verbal conjugations (Pacchiarotti and Bostoen 2024). Its
grammatical-tone values can be tested against the paradigm criteria and
profiled separately from sociophonetic variation that indexes region or
age. A high-profile result predicts “did you mean X or Y?” for a wrong
value and form correction for a wrong realization of the intended value.
Grammatical function and tonal substance don’t settle the classification
in advance.

<span class="smallcaps">Switch-reference as instruction.</span>
Switch-reference systems show how clausal architecture can package a
candidate paradigm beyond simple word order. In Amele, for example, a
dependent verb in a clause chain can mark same-subject
vs. different-subject linkage before the final tense-marked verb
(Roberts 1987, 1988, 2023). The following layouts are schematic, not
quoted Amele examples.

<div class="xlist">

*Verb*-<span class="smallcaps">ss.seq</span> … *Verb* (instruction: keep
same participant active) *Verb*-<span class="smallcaps">ds.seq</span> …
*Verb* (instruction: switch participant role)

</div>

The schematic contrast identifies reference-tracking values. If
independent profiling assigns the paradigm a high score, the hypothesis
predicts that a value mismatch (using the same-subject marker when the
subjects are different) will disrupt reference assignment and clause
linkage more than a matched value-preserving form error. The repair
locus remains an attribution question, not a consequence read off the
label.

## The boundary case: T/V and grammaticalized social deixis

If an indexical distinction such as addressee status forms a paradigm,
it becomes eligible for an operator profile; it doesn’t thereby receive
a high one. T/V distinctions test this boundary (Brown and Gilman 1960;
Helmbrecht 2013). Four levels are at issue: the addressee’s identity and
cardinality; the pronoun’s grammatical person–number value; its T/V
social-index value; and the agreement it licenses. French *tu* and
respectful singular *vous* can keep one addressee fixed while
contrasting grammatical number and social index, so obligatory choice in
the pronoun slot doesn’t show that the social contrast configures update
type. A mismatch can combine reference, agreement, and indexicality, but
the contributions require separate measures. Japanese *desu/masu* can
shift social presentation without changing addressee reference, while
\**le hiver* preserves reference and definiteness but fails
morphophonological form licensing
(§<a href="#sec:indexicality" data-reference-type="ref"
data-reference="sec:indexicality">4.1</a>). None of these repair loci
follows from paradigmhood alone.

# Grammaticality judgments as a social technology

Grammaticality judgments can function as a social technology: they let
speakers and institutions mark forms as belonging to, or falling
outside, a repertoire (Cameron 2012). When such judgments concern
high-profile paradigms, three variables organize the practice.

The community’s paradigm inventory records which paradigms exist;
profiling records their public-update contributions. Activation
conditions determine which contrasts are in play in a register, activity
type, or genre: a formal written register may activate a subjunctive
contrast that’s dormant in casual speech. Accountability conditions
record how participant roles calibrate correction: L2 learners,
children, and visitors to an unfamiliar dialect region may be evaluated
differently even when the same local inventory is active. Inventory and
profile concern paradigm and repertoire levels; activation conditions
concern constructions in activities; accountability conditions concern
speaker-level read-out. Neither set of conditions creates a paradigm’s
public-update contribution token by token.

Situation/ascription/identification conditioning gives these conditions
their social architecture. The immediate communicative situation selects
the activity, medium, footing, and task; speaker ascription supplies
durable baselines such as region, generation, institutional role, or
learner status; and identification tracks the norm-centre participants
orient to as relevant. These dimensions condition both which repertoires
are available and how departures are treated. A form can be licensed for
participants oriented to one norm-centre and unlicensed for participants
oriented to another, without either side being confused about its form
or meaning (Reynolds 2026c). Negative concord illustrates the point: *I
didn’t see nobody* is licensed in some English repertoires and
communicative situations but excluded when a standardized-school norm is
activated, even though the intended negative meaning is readily
recoverable.

These conditions help explain why grammatical conventions can be stable
without making every correction communicatively necessary.
Signaling-game models show that coordination problems can produce sharp
local conventions and arbitrary exclusions (O’Connor 2019). They don’t
establish that actual communities converge by that route. The relevant
accountability is practice-constitutive: participating in a linguistic
practice can make speakers answerable to its constraints (Millar 2004),
but it doesn’t entail moral obligation or a corrective-control
mechanism.

Repair fits the same pattern. It treats some departures as requiring
correction and others as negotiable (Schegloff et al. 1977). A wrong
value in a high-profile paradigm can prompt clarification of the move; a
value-preserving exponent error can prompt correction of the form; and a
constructional error can be corrected without a public-update contrast.

This framing also clarifies why judgment sharpness is common but not
inevitable. Some high-profile paradigms are supported by dense
opportunities and tightly constrained constructions, which makes
judgments highly concentrated. Others have smaller opportunity counts or
are contested across norm centres, which makes judgments more variable.
The same dynamics can make non-operator constructions appear
categorical, so sharpness is predicted to covary with concentrated
licensing evidence rather than diagnose an operator profile.

Speakers frequently label accent or dialect choices as “bad grammar” to
enforce social hierarchies. Everyday metalinguistic use, a
syntactician’s technical use, and the comparative concept used here
needn’t have the same extension; the issue is field-relative vocabulary,
not field-relative licensing facts. The paper doesn’t legislate the one
true meaning of “grammar”. It asks whether the target of a response is
an update value, an exponent, a construction, or a social index. “Did
you mean past time?” may target a paradigm value; “you mean *went*” may
target its licensed allomorph; “you mean *depend on*” may target a
complement construction. Ideological correction instead targets the
speaker’s social index while the utterance remains understood (Agha
2007; Eckert 2012). These response types can overlap, so researchers
need to code them rather than assume them.

Slurs and taboo words remain boundary cases for the same reason. They’re
publicly accountable in a strong sense, but the typical sanction targets
an impermissible action rather than a defective update instruction. The
same surface reaction, “you can’t say that”, can block a social move or
reject a linguistic instrument.

I address one target of grammaticality talk, not how such talk is
socially deployed. High-profile paradigms provide material for language
ideology, but that doesn’t make judgments politically neutral. Who
speaks for “the community”, who can correct, and with what consequences
are questions about the social life of the practice.

Operator-directed grammaticality talk isn’t identical to the output of a
judgment task. Paradigm identification, operator profiling, and
licensing concern population-level properties of a conditioned
repertoire. Corpus counts and comprehension probes estimate those
properties; felt ungrammaticality and correction are speaker-level
read-outs shaped by exposure, processing, social penalty, task design,
and metalinguistic training. Evidence, estimate, and read-out shouldn’t
be substituted for the world-side target (Reynolds and Miller 2026;
Reynolds 2026a).

# Predictions and research strategies

The protocol yields one primary confirmatory test and two secondary
generalizations. All are conditional on the pre-outcome identification
and coding stages in
§<a href="#sec:identifying-operators" data-reference-type="ref"
data-reference="sec:identifying-operators">2.4</a>.

<span class="smallcaps">(1) The operator-profile-by-error-locus
interaction.</span> Conversation favours self-repair over
other-correction: other-correction is dispreferred, while
other-initiation of repair is common and usually gives the original
speaker an opportunity to repair (Schegloff et al. 1977; Dingemanse et
al. 2015). Within each identified paradigm, wrong-value items should
disproportionately produce clarification of force, commitment, role, or
reference; value-preserving exponent errors should disproportionately
produce form correction with the intended update retained. That
within-paradigm difference checks the manipulation. The confirmatory
coefficient is its interaction with the independently assigned operator
profile across paradigms.

The prediction is more specific than a higher correction rate for
high-profile paradigms. \**Depend of* $`+`$
<span class="smallcaps">obj</span> can elicit “you mean X” through
constructional licensing, and a socially marked form can elicit
person-targeted sanction. The confirmatory outcome is the locus of the
response, not correction rate alone. Cross-linguistic repair research
supplies broader coding precedents but not validation of the proposed
interaction; Fox and colleagues specifically code same-turn syntactic
self-repair rather than the held-out next-turn categories used here (Fox
et al. 1996; Dingemanse et al. 2014, 2015). Adult interaction with
little formal-school correction can reduce one prescriptive rival, but
dialect, learner status, institutional role, and task format remain
measured accountability conditions.

<span class="smallcaps">(2) The profile should survive local
redundancy.</span> In gender, agreement, and reference-tracking systems,
a manipulation can leave one token’s interpretation unchanged while the
paradigm remains informative across its opportunities. After Stage 1 has
assigned the profile, held-out contexts should show token-level null
effects where lexical semantics already determines the referent and
positive effects where reference is underdetermined. The local null is
predicted; reassigning the profile after seeing it isn’t permitted. A
low-profile paradigm that produces the same value-versus-exponent repair
difference as a high-profile paradigm counts against the hypothesis.

<span class="smallcaps">(3) The interaction should cross expressive
substance.</span> Where tone, intonation, or nonmanual marking realizes
values in an identified paradigm, the same profile-by-error-locus
interaction should appear after opportunity, frequency, and perceptual
salience are matched (Hyman 2011; Ladd 2008). Tsiwah et al. (2021)
report a P600-like response to one temporal-agreement violation in Akan,
where time reference is expressed through grammatical tone; this
prediction concerns the broader matched repair profile, not a unique ERP
component. Compare tonal tense mismatches, segmental tense or agreement
mismatches, and sociophonetic tone shifts. Wrong values may prompt
reanalysis of material in scope; value-preserving exponent errors should
concentrate correction on the form. This interaction is more specific
than the familiar contrast between morphosyntactic and conceptual
anomaly (Kutas and Hillyard 1980; Osterhout and Holcomb 1992; Friederici
2011).

Two expectations remain corollaries rather than discriminating tests.
Resistance to satiation should increase with independently measured
evidence concentration and effective preemption for operator and
nonoperator constructions alike (Bybee 2006, 2010; Snyder 2000; Sprouse
2009; Reynolds 2026a). Accent and register should show systematic
indexical fields (Eckert 2008; Agha 2007), but an indexical system
receives an operator profile only after it independently qualifies as a
paradigm.

The failure condition belongs to the contribution statement. If the
frozen operator profile doesn’t predict the held-out
value-versus-exponent repair difference after general interpretive
consequence, discursive secondary status, opportunity, competitor
strength, surprisal, prosody, processing load, schooling, social
sanction, and task are matched or accounted for, the operator-profile
hypothesis adds no explanatory value for those outcomes. The paradigm
descriptions and component profiles may remain useful; the proposed
attribution doesn’t.

The same inferential order applies whether the first test uses new
elicitation or disjoint partitions of existing interactional corpora:
identify paradigms, freeze profiles and rivals, and only then code the
held-out outcomes.

# Conclusion

The proposal is cumulative rather than a new theory of grammar.
Heltoft’s work already shows that paradigmatic grammar can organize
illocutionary responsibility, interlocutionary relations, and other
aspects of public interaction. Nørgård-Sørensen and Heltoft supply the
domain–frame architecture used here, though I don’t adopt it as a
general definition of grammatical status. The theoretical contribution
is to reclassify identified paradigms by a separate, graded
public-update profile and to keep that profile distinct from mismatch
locus and response cause.

Clausal architecture can host high-profile paradigms for clause type,
commitment, role allocation, scope, or reference tracking. Tone,
gesture, particles, word order, and inflection can realize their values.
Complementation can instead impose a community-specific constructional
convention, as with *depend on*, without configuring public update. One
high-profile paradigm never makes every constraint on its containing
construction, or on clause structure as a whole, profile highly.

The projective contribution is the repair-locus hypothesis. Within an
identified paradigm, the wrong-value versus value-preserving-exponent
difference checks the manipulation; the sole confirmatory claim is that
this difference increases with the frozen operator profile across
paradigms. Paradigm identification, profile construction, rival
measures, and outcome coding precede the test. If the interaction
disappears after the pre-specified semantic, grammatical-status,
structural, and social rivals are matched or accounted for, the paradigm
and profile descriptions may survive, but the explanatory hypothesis
fails.

Constructional embedding, value selection, exponent licensing,
opportunity, schooling, and sanction remain rival or complementary
causes. A locally redundant concord marker can belong to a high-profile
reference-tracking system; \**goed* and \**le hiver* can preserve an
intended value while selecting a lexeme-specific or morphophonologically
excluded allomorph. Grammaticalization and related discourse-functional
research supports recurrent recruitment into interactionally
consequential functions (Waltereit 2011; Degand and Evers-Vermeul 2015),
but the stronger suggestion that public-update work preferentially
creates paradigms remains an unconfirmed historical hypothesis.
