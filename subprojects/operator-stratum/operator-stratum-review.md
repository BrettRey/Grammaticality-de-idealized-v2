# Why clause structure is judged like tense and agreement
## Public-update operators and grammaticality
Brett Reynolds — Humber Polytechnic & University of Toronto
## Abstract
Why do speakers treat some readily understood forms as errors of grammar, while treating others as matters of wording, accent, or style? I argue that one major class of grammatical contrasts tells interlocutors how to treat an utterance: as a question rather than an assertion, as committing one participant rather than another, or as linking a participant or referent across clauses. I call this their **public-update role**. A contrast belongs to the operator stratum when it has such a role and forms a small, conventional paradigm. The definition applies to the paradigm, not to every token. A gender marker can be redundant in one clause but informative elsewhere; _goed_ can select the intended past value but use an excluded exponent. Opportunity density then affects how concentrated and resistant to change the licensing pattern becomes, not whether the contrast is an operator. The account also separates operator errors from other grammatical failures. English excludes _depend of_ through a community-specific complement construction even though no public-update value has been mis-set. This distinction yields three discriminating tests: tonal, prosodic, and gestural operators should pattern with segmental ones; value errors and value-intact exponent errors should invite different kinds of repair; and locally redundant tokens should prompt update-oriented repair only where their paradigm has an independently measurable public-update role.

**Keywords:** grammaticality, operator stratum, public update, licensing, preemption, clausal architecture
# The puzzle: why “grammar” picks out some things rather than others
{--The asterisk is selective.--}{id="s1" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:34:11.605Z"} Some mismatches are treated as straightforward errors of “grammar”: wrong agreement, illicit clausal morphosyntax, incompatible tense–aspect morphology, missing obligatory evidentials, or mis-selected question particles in systems where such particles are required. Other mismatches are treated as infelicities of social positioning: an accent that indexes the wrong stance, a style that fails to fit the activity type, or a form that sounds “too formal” or “too intimate”. Still others are treated as matters of lexical choice: poor perhaps, but not illicit.[^1]

A familiar temptation is to reduce this to a contrast between “structure” and “use”: morphosyntax counts as grammar; accent and lexical choice don’t. But this gloss is neither typologically adequate nor explanatory. Many languages use prosody or tone as obligatory exponents of grammatical contrasts (Hyman 2011; Ladd 2008). Many grammatical categories aren’t universal: a language may lack tense (Comrie 1985) or number (Corbett 2000) while sustaining categorical judgments elsewhere. And some lexical items enter operator contrasts, with constraints indistinguishable from inflectional morphology (Hopper and Traugott 2003; Bybee 2010).

The core question concerns why some form–value relations become non-negotiable parts of a community’s repertoire while others remain negotiable resources for stance, identity, and content selection.

The argument developed here is that one important part of this difference tracks a difference in value: what a contrast does in a system and what it rules in or out for uptake.[^2] Consider polarity: choosing _not_ over an affirmative changes what inferences are licensed. A question particle rather than a declarative ending changes what kind of move the utterance counts as. Such contrasts tell interlocutors how to treat the utterance. I call that contribution a public-update role, and a closed paradigm with that role an operator paradigm.

The claim is deliberately narrower than an account of every asterisk. A community can exclude a construction such as *_depend of_ {+++ Obj++}{id="s2" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:35:14.604Z"} without any operator value being wrong. Conversely, an operator value can be right while its exponent is wrong: *_goed_ expresses past time through an excluded form, and a concord error can be locally redundant while violating an established paradigm. Constructional licensing, operator value, and exponent choice remain separate. The sections that follow define that distinction, show how clause structure can realize operator paradigms, and derive empirical comparisons with accent, lexical choice, and constructional convention.
# Why some contrasts are publicly accountable
A deliberately non-idealized view of grammar starts from a community’s repertoire of conventionalized form–value relations, not only its strings or derivations. Those relations enable coordination in interaction.

In the first, a speaker uses a same-subject marker where a different-subject marker is required, {++and the++}{id="s3" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:37:16.398Z"} {++hearer responds “Wait, who did what?”++}{id="s4" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:37:26.430Z"} {--o--}{id="s6" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:39:11.505Z"}{++O++}{id="s7" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:39:12.897Z"}r {++the speaker++}{id="s5" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:37:32.374Z"} selects a declarative complement where an interrogative one is intended. The hearer responds “Wait, who did what?” or “Did you mean _that_ or _whether_?” Repair targets the grammatical contrast because it affects how the contribution is to be understood. In the second {++type of misfire++}{id="s10" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:40:00.276Z"}, a speaker uses an accent or register that doesn’t fit the setting; the hearer understands the utterance but reacts to the speaker’s social positioning: “Why are you talking like that?” What’s targeted is stance or persona, not the move itself.

This contrast reflects what I’ll call public accountability. Interlocutors don’t merely exchange signals; they make commitments mutually recognizable and ratifiable (Stalnaker 1978, 2002; Clark and Brennan 1991; Clark 1996). Some grammatical contrasts tell them how to treat an utterance: as a question rather than an assertion, as committing one participant rather than another, or as linking a participant or referent across clauses. I call this contribution a public-update role. It’s public because the resulting move is available for acknowledgement, challenge, answer, or repair.

Repair practices make this selectivity visible. When an utterance isn’t understood, or is understood under a suspect analysis, interlocutors systematically mobilize repair (Schegloff et al. 1977). Repair is evidence about which dimension mattered in the interaction; it isn’t part of the definition of that dimension.

Public accountability isn’t the same as public consequence. Accent and style are central to social meaning, but they aren’t normally grammatical instructions for what an utterance counts as in the way that clause type, polarity, or evidential marking can be (Eckert 2008, 2012; Agha 2007; Coupland 2007). Lexical choice is similarly accountable in an ethical or epistemic sense: one can be challenged for using a slur or a misleading term. Those reactions differ from repair of the grammatical value that organizes the update.

The contrasts that combine public-update work with a closed conventional paradigm constitute what I’ll call the operator stratum.
# The operator stratum
## The operator-stratum hypothesis
An operator paradigm is a small, conventional menu of grammatical values that helps determine how an utterance enters public interaction. I use operator narrowly: not for everything called an operator in grammatical theory, and not for every construction that speakers judge categorically. {==Membership has two conditions==}{>>This doesn't fit well with my usual approach to categories. There's a need to balance consistency with expositional simplicity and clarity.<<}{id="c1" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:43:44.159Z"}.

(i) Paradigm-level public update. Taken across its ordinary occasions of use, the paradigm conventionally helps determine what move is made, whose commitment is at issue, how participants or referents are linked, or what responses are relevant (Stalnaker 2002; Farkas and Bruce 2010; Murray 2014). Clause type, illocutionary force, evidential anchoring, and switch-reference are central cases. The condition applies to the paradigm, not to the information carried by every token. An agreement marker can be redundant in one clause and decisive in another while the agreement paradigm remains a resource for participant or reference tracking.

Membership is a fact about a community’s repertoire; experiments and corpora provide evidence about it. Comprehension probes can ask whether changing the value changes who did what, with what force, or under whose commitment. Next-turn distributions offer a coarser diagnostic when comparable tokens vary naturally. Questions should alter rates of answers and counter-questions, for example. Such distributions need to distinguish uptake responses from sanction, stance, and assessment. Neither diagnostic defines the world-side category it estimates. The companion model represents the relevant target as an expected difference in public update (Reynolds 2026a).

The condition concerns licensed update types rather than whether a claim is true or successful. A speaker can say _It’s true_ of a false situation: the assertion fails epistemically, but affirmative polarity and declarative force are licensed. By contrast, {++past-tense++}{id="s11" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:49:13.768Z"} _Yesterday the bill passed_ selects a past-time anchor; {++present-tense++}{id="s12" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:49:25.271Z"} *_Yesterday the bill pass_ misselects its realization {--in varieties where finite past morphology is obligatory--}{id="s13" by="user" at="2026-08-23T12:49:46.876Z"}.

Contextual anchoring is constrained. Context selects among independently licensed operator values, constructional frames, and ellipsis patterns; it doesn’t create a form–value relation ad hoc. _I have five years_ can succeed as an elliptical reply to “How long do you have left?” because the frame supplies the relevant duration. The same reply to “How old are you?” fails in ordinary English because age is expressed through the _be_ frame, not because the hearer lacks the intended meaning.

(ii) Paradigmatic closure. The values belong to a small, conventionalized contrast set. They recur in describable structural environments, their alternatives are mutually exclusive there, and the set isn’t freely expanded token by token. Tense paradigms, agreement systems, switch-reference systems, and complementizer inventories are central cases. Idioms and fixed collocations may be rigid, but their words don’t thereby form a grammatical value paradigm.

Opportunity density modulates the resulting licensing profile. It affects how much evidence speakers encounter and how strongly competitors preempt one another, which can make the profile more categorical, stable, and resistant to exposure. A moribund case contrast can remain an operator while its judgments grow less concentrated. A frequent collocation can be sharply conventional without becoming an operator.

The cases make two boundaries especially clear.

Slurs satisfy the public-consequence test but usually fail the operator test. A racial slur used as a vocative, or a misogynistic slur used as a predicate, can radically alter the social action performed and can make the speaker answerable for that action. But the utterance normally remains structurally fileable as an address, assertion, accusation, or description. The move is morally or socially impermissible even though the hearer can identify its update type. The predicted response is sanction of the act or speaker, not repair of an operator value.

Interjections sit closer to the operator stratum. They can configure uptake, stance, attention, repair, or floor management (Ameka 1992). In the English cases discussed by Reynolds (2026d), _oh_ can mark a change in epistemic state, _huh?_ can initiate repair, _mmhm_ can acknowledge or continue a turn, _uh_ can delay while holding the floor, and _wow_ can display surprise or assessment.

But many interjection tokens aren’t selected from a closed set of mutually exclusive values in the way tense, polarity, or clause type are. They can be repeated, stretched, blended, newly coined, or substituted without producing a categorical licensing failure. In those cases, an odd interjection may disturb uptake or stance without leaving the utterance outside the operator repertoire. If an interjection-like form is conventionalized into a closed, distributionally accountable paradigm, it can enter the operator stratum; uptake configuration alone isn’t enough.

French *_le hiver_ is an operator-exponent case. The definite determinative contributes reference setting; its singular forms are realized as _le_, _la_, or elided _l’_ under morphophonological and lexically conditioned restrictions. French h muet words such as _hiver_ pattern with vowel-initial words and trigger elision, whereas h aspiré words block it (Boersma 2007). The intended value in *_le hiver_ is still definite reference. What fails is the licensed realization of that value: the required form is _l’hiver_. {==The pattern is analogous to a Turkish plural or past suffix whose grammatical value is recoverable but whose harmonic allomorph is excluded (Reynolds 2026a).==}{>>is this sufficiently useful? ## Identifying operator paradigms
The two membership conditions require independent evidence. Judgments and correction practices are consequences to be predicted, not shortcuts to membership.

Paradigmatic closure. Corpus and descriptive evidence should identify a small, enumerable value set recurring in specified structural environments: polarity contrasts, evidential sets, tense–aspect systems, agreement paradigms, switch-reference markers, question particles, or complementizer inventories. Closure isn’t inferred from the asterisk.

Public-update contribution. Comprehension probes should test whether changing a value alters clause type, commitment, role assignment, reference tracking, scope, or the relevant response space while conceptual content is held constant as far as possible. Broad scope is common because many such contrasts organize the clause or a multi-clause sequence, but scope alone doesn’t establish membership.

Opportunity count and preemption. Opportunity is measured as the number of occasions on which a candidate and its competitors could plausibly have been selected. High counts concentrate evidence for operator exponents such as _went_, but they also concentrate evidence for non-operator constructions such as _depend on_. Opportunity predicts the strength of a licensing boundary, not operator membership. A population-level exclusion also depends on how speakers adopt and retain variants; updating alone doesn’t produce it (Reynolds 2026a).

Left-branch extraction illustrates the resulting division of labour. English speakers repeatedly ask about modifier–noun packages, and _Which car did you buy?_ occupies the interrogative niche in which *_Which did you buy car?_ might otherwise appear (Reynolds 2026e). Whether the latter is a preempted gap rather than a structurally starved option requires independent niche norming; it can’t be read from absence alone (Reynolds 2026a). If that norming succeeds, repeated attributable competitor wins explain the concentrated evidence against the variant. Whether the construction belongs to the operator stratum is a separate question about its role in interrogative packaging.

The identification order is thus fixed before outcomes are inspected: establish the value set and its environments, test its paradigm-level update contribution, and count its opportunities. Categorical judgments, repair profiles, and processing measures can then test what that independently identified contrast predicts. Because the diagnostics track contribution rather than substance, tonal, prosodic, gestural, and segmental exponents are assessed on the same terms.
# Why clausal architecture belongs to the operator stratum
Treating clausal architecture as a matter of arranging words gives an English-biased picture. Across languages, operator exponents include affix order in polysynthetic systems, templatic morphology, clitic clusters, tone melodies, nonmanual marking, rigid word order, flexible order coupled with case, and mixtures of these resources. The relevant generalization concerns operator packaging rather than linearization.

The thesis is narrower than “all syntax belongs to the operator stratum”. Clausal architecture belongs with tense and agreement when it realizes publicly accountable operator contrasts: clause type, argument linking, dependency scope, and reference tracking. Some syntactic constraints may instead reflect expression-shape regularities, processing limits, or constructional conventionalization. The syntax most readily experienced as grammatical mismatch is predicted to be operator-rich, but not every syntactic asterisk is an operator mismatch.

The relevant claim concerns projectibility: what observing some features licenses us to predict about others (Goodman 1955). Once a closed paradigm with a public-update role has been identified, observing its value and opportunity profile should license further expectations about correction, response type, and resistance to exposure within a stated community and activity range (Reynolds 2026b). Projectibility is an analyst-side relation between observed and expected features. It doesn’t cause a speaker’s feeling; concentrated exposure and task-sensitive read-out explain that response.

Clausal architecture packages operator contrasts in at least four domains.

Clause type and response space. Interrogatives, imperatives, declaratives, and related types encode different update potentials and different norms for what counts as a relevant next move. The contrast is interactionally basic, and languages grammaticalize interrogatives using heterogeneous resources (particles, morphology, intonation, or combinations) (Dryer 2013; Ladd 2008). Where a language requires an interrogative exponent for the intended question, declarative morphology leaves the addressee to decide whether to answer or merely acknowledge; the intended public update is unsettled.

Argument linking and participant roles. Clausal organization encodes which participant is presented as actor, undergoer, experiencer, and so on. The implementation varies typologically, but closed case, agreement, or voice paradigms can constrain the recoverable role assignments in a publicly accountable way. If the wrong role is signalled, the hearer may track the wrong participant as agent or patient, derailing downstream inference.

Dependency management and scope. Some complement contrasts package how information is nested, attributed, and scoped. _Kim knows that Pat left_ presents an embedded proposition, whereas _Kim wonders whether Pat left_ opens an embedded question. *_Kim wonders that Pat left_ misselects the complement type for the intended update. Here constructional licensing and operator value intersect: the matrix head restricts the complement construction, and the complementizer helps identify whether the embedded contribution is propositional or interrogative (Stalnaker 2002; Farkas and Bruce 2010).

Reference tracking across clauses. Many languages treat cross-clausal coherence as an operator contrast. Switch-reference is a textbook case: a morphosyntactic system signals whether the subject of one clause is the same as or different from that of another (Gijn 2016). A wrong marker licenses the wrong tracking relation or leaves tracking unresolved, which can derail the whole multi-clause sequence. The key point here isn’t the particular pivot (which varies) but the fact that a community can grammaticalize the tracking problem as operator value.

To illustrate the form of the claim, consider Amele, a Papuan language in which dependent switch-reference verbs in clause chains mark whether the relevant argument is maintained or changed across adjacent clauses (Roberts 1987, 1988, 2023). Roberts’s examples contrast forms such as _h-um-ig_ “come-ss.seq-1sg.nom” and _ho-co-min_ “come-ds.seq-1sg.nom” before a final tense-marked verb. The operator contrast isn’t “a content word”; it supplies a clause-linking operator value. Operator mismatches are predicted to be treated as structural mismatches: the wrong marker licenses the wrong clause-linking update, or leaves the intended update unresolved.

A second, different kind of illustration comes from Japanese sentence-final particles. In many registers, _ka_ conventionally marks interrogative force, while declaratives are unmarked or carry other particles; intonation interacts with these choices, and colloquial speech allows more variation (Kuno 1973; Shibatani 1990). The particle helps determine what kind of move the utterance counts as and what responses are licensed. Membership in a clause-typing paradigm doesn’t make every omission an error. An omitted _ka_ may yield a declarative construal, an intonation-marked question, or another licensed move. It becomes relevant to ungrammaticality only where the construction makes an interrogative value obligatory, excludes a competing value, or blocks a combination with other particles, intonation, or morphosyntax. Obligatoriness is a separate, historically conditioned property of the construction rather than an automatic consequence of operator membership (Reynolds 2026a).

Together, the Amele and Japanese cases separate two claims that are easily conflated. Some clausal operators generate clear structural mismatches when the wrong value is selected; others, like _ka_, show operator membership without automatic failure. In both cases, clausal architecture matters because it packages values for public update, not because syntax is insulated from interaction.

Complementation also shows why community convention and operator value remain separate. Complement constructions are schematic: they assign categories and grammatical functions, while particular heads may narrow a complement to a clause of a certain type or to a PP headed by one of a small set of prepositions. English _put_ conventionally requires a locative complement, and _depend_ selects _on_ or _upon_. These are constructional-licensing facts even when no public-update contrast is involved. Complementation intersects the operator stratum where its values also configure commitment, clause type, scope, or participant linking, as in _that_ vs. _whether_. Schematic inheritance and lexically narrowed realization coexist.
# Why tense and number behave similarly without being universal
Tense and number enter grammaticality judgments through the operator paradigms that particular languages grammaticalize. In a language without obligatory tense marking, the mere absence of tense morphology supplies no operator-value error. Other constructional requirements can still make a clause unlicensed. This account avoids a common slippage between salience in familiar European languages and cross-linguistic necessity (Haspelmath 2007).

Tense marking is an especially clear example. Where tense is grammaticalized, it can form an operator contrast for temporal anchoring, and wrong values are often treated as licensing failures (Comrie 1985). Where tense isn’t grammaticalized, temporal anchoring is managed through aspect, evidential access, discourse particles, and contextual inference. Speakers in the latter case shouldn’t experience missing tense marking as an operator error, because their repertoire contains no obligatory tense contrast to leave unsatisfied.

Number behaves analogously. In languages where number is obligatory in agreement or nominal morphology, it’s part of the operator repertoire for tracking reference in public commitments, and a number mismatch is a paradigmatic licensing failure. In languages where number is optional or limited, other contrasts fill the reference-tracking role, and number mismatches have different status (Corbett 2000).

Operator repertoires vary across languages; where grammaticality judgments track licensing failures, they target operator contrasts _wherever those contrasts occur_.
# Gender and noun-class concord as a hard case
Gender and noun-class concord are the limiting case for the account. With many inanimate nouns, a gender mismatch has little or no effect on the interpretation of that token: *_le table_ normally doesn’t leave the hearer wondering which object is meant. But it’s treated as a central grammar error where gender concord is obligatory (Corbett 1991).

That pattern would be hard to explain if public-update value were a token-by-token membership condition. The relevant question is instead whether the concord paradigm contributes to participant or reference tracking across its occasions of use. Where it does, a locally redundant marker can inherit the role of the paradigm, just as an identifiable past-tense exponent remains part of the tense system. High opportunity density then concentrates evidence for the licensed concord forms, so a wrong exponent can be corrected even when the local referent is obvious.

Public-update role remains empirically constrained. The Mahowald-style point is local redundancy: agreement can be redundant in most clauses while remaining decisive in the minority where lexical semantics, animacy, or discourse expectations underdetermine reference (Mahowald et al. 2023). Whether a particular gender system has that paradigm-level role is an empirical question for comprehension probes, not a fact licensed by the existence of categorical judgments. A diachronic claim that reference tracking favours the recruitment of concord systems remains a separate hypothesis.

The operational prediction has two levels. A gender manipulation on an inanimate noun may have no measurable effect on that token’s interpretation, while probes spanning the paradigm still reveal a positive contribution to reference tracking. The theory is challenged by a closed, dense paradigm whose contribution remains near zero across those probes but whose wrong values produce the same comprehension and repair profile as independently identified operators. Categorical correction alone isn’t enough to falsify the boundary, because constructional licensing can also be categorical.
# Why accent and lexical choice are usually different
Accent and lexical choice typically have different kinds of value.
## Indexical value and negotiability
Accent, style, and register contribute indexical value: they position speakers relative to social categories, stances, and activity types (Silverstein 1976; Eckert 2008; Agha 2007; Coupland 2007). Indexical value is often rich and structured, but it isn’t typically implemented as a small closed paradigm tied to public update. Interlocutors can reinterpret an accent shift as play, accommodation, quotation, or stance work. The contrast remains socially accountable without thereby becoming an operator contrast.

Indexicality can be obligatory in social practice without becoming an operator licensing condition. Much indexical meaning is _meta-pragmatic_: it becomes salient through social ideologies and enregisterment processes (Agha 2007; Eckert 2012). That makes it both powerful and variable, but it rarely supplies an operator value that has to be selected for the utterance to count as a recognized move.
## Conceptual value and open classes
Open-class lexical items primarily contribute conceptual content, and their inventories tolerate expansion and innovation. Lexical choice can be infelicitous, misleading, or socially harmful without mis-setting a grammatical update. Lexically conditioned patterns can still be categorical: a head can impose constructional restrictions on its complements, as _depend_ does, while the open lexical inventory remains outside the operator stratum.

“Open-endedness” has two dimensions that matter here. Lexical inventories are open in the sense of expandable type-sets. Compositional semantics is open-ended in a different sense: unbounded productivity from combining a finite stock. Operators lack the first kind of openness, but they participate fully in the second: negation scopes, evidentials anchor commitments, and clause type constrains response space. Closed inventories make it easier for repeated choices to concentrate licensing expectations, but closure alone doesn’t supply an operator role.

That difference predicts why a wrong operator value and a poor lexical choice can invite different responses even when both are understood. The former can alter the public classification of the move; the latter usually invites negotiation about content. Constructionally excluded lexical combinations remain a third possibility.
## Cross-substance operators
Operator status is a role, not a substance. Phonological material can be an operator exponent, and lexical items can enter operator contrasts when they grammaticalize.

Systems in which tone marks tense, focus, or polarity are paradigmatic cases of phonological material realizing operator value, and they should attract operator-directed repair where the relevant value is obligatory or mutually exclusive (Hyman 2011). Intonational contours that conventionalize clause type are operator exponents; when they’re required or exclusionary, they should pattern like question particles rather than like accent (Ladd 2008). This aligns with work on rising declaratives and intonational commitments, where intonational tunes modify context-update functions rather than merely colouring pronunciation (Gunlogson 2003; Rudin 2022).

Signed languages make the same point in another modality. In American Sign Language, a yes/no question can be marked by nonmanual cues such as brow raise over the relevant clause. The operator value is carried by scopal nonmanual marking, not by a segmental word, but it still configures clause type and response space (Sandler and Lillo-Martin 2006).

Lexical items can enter operator contrasts through grammaticalization. Paradigms shrink, content fades, and licensing constraints emerge (Hopper and Traugott 2003; Bybee 2010). Function words, particles, auxiliaries, complementizers, and polarity items can realize operator values. Lexical form alone is insufficient. A preposition selected by a particular head may realize a community-specific complement construction without configuring public update. Its lexical source, constructional licensing, and operator role are separate questions.

Phonotactics sharpens the same point. Phonotactic constraints are conventionalized and often treated categorically (“not a possible word”), but they don’t satisfy the public-update condition. They regulate expression shape rather than the grammatical value of the move. The likely repair target differs: recognizability for phonotactics, update value for operators, conventional form for constructional or exponent licensing, and content or stance for many lexical and pragmatic mismatches. These are tendencies to test, not exclusive response bins.
## A scope analogy
Operators often affect how other material is interpreted. Once an utterance is marked interrogative, the remaining material is understood as the content of a question rather than an assertion. Polarity changes the inferential contribution of material in its scope; case and agreement can constrain role assignment. Operators don’t always reduce more uncertainty than lexical items: predicates such as _congratulate_ and _accuse_ can reshape continuations dramatically.

The protocol-header comparison is useful only as a scope analogy. Operator values can organize the interpretation of material in their domain: assertion vs. question, self-commitment vs. attributed commitment, or one role assignment vs. another. A wrong value can prompt reanalysis beyond the exponent itself. But linguistic operators needn’t precede their scope, and value-intact exponent errors needn’t produce global reanalysis. The analogy doesn’t provide a processing mechanism or an information-theoretic derivation.

This explains a puzzle: grammatical marking is often locally redundant.[^3] Mahowald et al. (2023) show that across 30 languages, subjecthood in simple transitive clauses can usually be inferred from lexical content alone. Overt grammatical cues are redundant in a majority of everyday cases, but decisive for the minority where lexical semantics doesn’t settle the roles (reversible events, non-prototypical assignments). Operators can be locally redundant but globally update-relevant. Their contribution isn’t to maximize information in each occurrence, but to supply a codebook whose values remain available when context, lexical semantics, or discourse expectations underdetermine the update.

Frequent, predictable grammatical material often undergoes phonetic reduction. Reduced exponents can still belong to a closed paradigm because operator membership depends on the paradigm’s contribution, not on phonetic invariance.

Opportunity structure supplies a separate prediction. Repeated, attributable competitor choices can concentrate evidence for a licensing boundary (Bybee 2006, 2010; Reynolds 2026a). Wrong operator values may prompt broader reanalysis of material in their scope; value-intact exponent errors instead predict form correction with the intended update preserved. This split, not the header metaphor itself, is the processing claim.
# Typological consequences: which contrasts qualify as operators
The operator stratum is a comparative concept: it identifies an interactional role that can be realized by different structures in different languages (Haspelmath 2007). Typological diversity then concerns which contrasts meet the two conditions, not whether English categories are universal.

Four domains are particularly instructive.
## Instructive domains of variation
Evidentiality and epistemic authority. If evidentiality forms a closed paradigm in a language, its values should configure whose commitment is at issue and on what evidential basis (Aikhenvald 2004). Where a construction also makes one value obligatory, wrong values should invite repair of that commitment structure. Where evidential meanings remain lexical or periphrastic, comparable claims can be challenged as deceptive or inappropriate without establishing an operator paradigm.

Egophoricity and perspective management. If a language grammaticalizes perspective or ego-involvement as a closed paradigm, then perspective mismatches should pattern like agreement mismatches where the paradigm makes one value obligatory or excludes another: repair targets the operator value. Tibetic languages and others have been argued to do exactly this (Tournadre 2008; Floyd et al. 2018). Regardless of the preferred analysis, these contrasts are prime candidates for operator-stratum membership: they govern whose epistemic position is encoded as the relevant anchor for a clause’s update potential.

Where perspective doesn’t form a closed update-configuring paradigm, similar meanings remain expressible but are more likely to be negotiated as pragmatic alignment than repaired as an operator value.

Phonological exponents vs. accent. If tone marks tense, aspect, or polarity in a language, then tonal mismatches should pattern like morphosyntactic mismatches when the intended operator value is fixed by the construction or context, not like accent shifts. That prediction makes tonal languages a crucial test case (Hyman 2011). A language may distinguish tense values primarily through tonal melodies on the verb (one pattern for remote past, another for recent past), with minimal segmental differentiation between the forms.

A concrete case is Ngwi, a West-Coastal Bantu language whose Tense–Aspect–Mood–Polarity system includes grammatical tone in a compact set of verbal conjugations (Pacchiarotti and Bostoen 2024). In such systems, tone can realize an operator value rather than merely index accent. A wrong tonal value may invite “did you mean X or Y?”; a wrong realization of the intended value may instead invite form correction. Sociophonetic tonal variation can index region or age without changing either. The diagnostic belongs at the paradigm level: does the tonal contrast encode TAMP values that configure the update, or an indexical field that identifies the speaker?

Switch-reference as instruction. Switch-reference systems exemplify how clausal architecture can package an operator contrast beyond simple word order. In Amele, for example, a dependent verb in a clause chain can mark same-subject vs. different-subject linkage before the final tense-marked verb (Roberts 1987, 1988, 2023).

_Verb-ss.seq … Verb_ (instruction: keep same participant active) _Verb-ds.seq … Verb_ (instruction: switch participant role)

This marker choice is an explicit instruction for reference tracking: it constrains the assignment of participant roles in the upcoming increment. An operator mismatch (using the same-subject marker when the subjects are different) disrupts reference assignment and clause linkage; repair targets reference tracking, not stance or style.
## The boundary case: T/V and grammaticalized social deixis
If an indexical distinction such as addressee status becomes obligatory and paradigmatically closed, some mismatches should pattern less like free register choice and more like operator-value errors. T/V distinctions test this prediction (Brown and Gilman 1960; Helmbrecht 2013). French _tu_ and _vous_, for instance, can be negotiated in some relationships but tightly constrained in institutional ones. The resulting overlap is expected: a wrong form may be socially sanctionable as address behaviour and structurally repairable as the wrong paradigm value. The contrast qualifies as an operator only where the closed paradigm itself configures participant roles in the public update.
# Grammaticality judgments as a social technology
Grammaticality judgments can function as a social technology: they let speakers and institutions mark forms as belonging to, or falling outside, a repertoire. When such judgments concern operators, three variables organize the practice.

The operator repertoire records which contrasts exist and what values they conventionally take in the community. The activation profile records which contrasts are in play in a register, activity type, or genre: a formal written register may activate a subjunctive contrast that’s dormant in casual speech. The accountability profile records how participant roles calibrate correction: L2 learners, children, and visitors to an unfamiliar dialect region may be evaluated differently even when the same local repertoire is active.

Situation/ascription/identification conditioning gives these profiles their social architecture. The immediate situation selects the activity, medium, footing, and task; speaker ascription supplies durable baselines such as region, generation, institutional role, or learner status; and identification tracks the norm-centre participants orient to as relevant. These dimensions condition both which repertoires are available and how departures are treated. A form can be licensed for participants oriented to one norm-centre and unlicensed for participants oriented to another, without either side being confused about its form or meaning (Reynolds 2026c). Negative concord illustrates the point: _I didn’t see nobody_ is licensed in some English repertoires and excluded by standardized-school norms, even when the intended negative meaning is readily recoverable.

These profiles help explain why grammatical conventions can be stable without making every correction communicatively necessary. Signaling-game models show that coordination problems can produce categorical local conventions and arbitrary exclusions (O’Connor 2019). They don’t establish that actual communities converge by that route. Speakers also often remain understandable when they depart from grammar (Pullum 2019). The relevant accountability is practice-constitutive: participating in a linguistic practice can make speakers answerable to its constraints (Millar 2004), but it doesn’t entail moral obligation or a corrective-control mechanism.

Repair fits the same pattern. It treats some departures as requiring correction and others as negotiable (Schegloff et al. 1977). A wrong operator value can prompt clarification of the move; a value-intact exponent error can prompt correction of the form; and a constructional error can be corrected even without an operator contrast.

This framing also clarifies why categoricality is common but not inevitable. Some operator contrasts are supported by dense opportunities and tightly constrained constructions, which makes judgments sharp. Others have smaller opportunity counts or are contested across norm centres, which makes judgments more variable. The same dynamics can make non-operator constructions categorical, so categoricality is a projection of concentrated licensing evidence rather than a diagnostic of operator value.

Speakers frequently label accent or dialect choices as “bad grammar” to enforce social hierarchies. Overt correction is rare, but its target can still distinguish practices. “Did you mean the past form?” targets an operator value; “you mean _went_” targets its exponent; “you mean _depend on_” targets a complement construction. Ideological correction instead targets the speaker’s social index while the utterance remains understood (Agha 2007; Eckert 2012). These response types can overlap, so they must be coded rather than assumed.

Slurs and taboo words remain boundary cases for the same reason. They’re publicly accountable in a strong sense, but the typical sanction targets an impermissible action rather than a defective update instruction. The same surface reaction, “you can’t say that”, can block a social move or reject a linguistic instrument.

The analysis addresses one target of grammaticality talk, not how such talk is socially deployed. The operator stratum provides material for language ideology, but that doesn’t make judgments politically neutral. Who speaks for “the community”, who can correct, and with what consequences are questions about the social life of the practice.

The target of operator-directed grammaticality talk isn’t identical to the output of a judgment task. Operator membership and licensing are population-level properties of a conditioned repertoire. Corpus counts and comprehension probes estimate those properties; felt ungrammaticality and correction are speaker-level read-outs shaped by exposure, processing, social penalty, task design, and metalinguistic training. Evidence, estimate, and read-out shouldn’t be substituted for the world-side target (Reynolds and Miller 2026; Reynolds 2026a).
# Predictions and research strategies
The strongest tests compare contrasts after closure, opportunity, and competitor strength have been measured independently. Otherwise a closure-and-frequency account predicts most of the same outcomes.

(1) Repair targets should follow the error locus. Overt repair is rare overall, but wrong operator values should disproportionately invite clarification of force, commitment, role, or reference. Value-intact exponent errors should invite correction of the form with the intended update preserved. Constructional errors such as *_depend of_ can produce the same surface format, “you mean X”, without an operator-value repair. Indexical mismatches should more often invite stance negotiation, accommodation, or person-targeted sanction (Schegloff et al. 1977).

Operationally, corpora should code value, exponent, constructional, and indexical mismatches separately, with the target independently recoverable and participant access controlled. Adult interaction in communities without formal schooling is especially informative because it limits the prescriptive-ideology rival. Dialect, learner status, institutional role, and task format remain part of the accountability profile rather than noise to be ignored.

(2) Paradigm-level contribution should survive token redundancy. In gender, agreement, and reference-tracking systems, a manipulation can leave one token’s interpretation unchanged while the paradigm remains informative across its opportunities. Comprehension probes should find token-level null effects alongside a positive paradigm-level contribution and concentrated correction. A closed, dense contrast with a near-zero paradigm-level contribution that produces the same value-confusion and repair profile as an operator would falsify the proposed boundary. Historical recruitment can’t be invoked after that result to preserve membership.

(3) Operator profiles should cross expressive substance. In languages where tone, intonation, or nonmanual marking realizes an operator value, wrong values should pattern with segmental operator errors more closely than with matched accent or stance variation (Hyman 2011; Ladd 2008). The comparison has to match opportunity and frequency. Tsiwah et al. (2021) report a P600-like response to one temporal-agreement violation in Akan, where time reference is expressed through grammatical tone; the prediction concerns the broader matched response profile, not a unique ERP component.

Operationally, compare tonal tense mismatches, segmental tense or agreement mismatches, and sociophonetic tone shifts. Wrong values may prompt reanalysis of material in scope; value-intact exponent errors should concentrate correction on the form. This error-locus split is more specific than the familiar contrast between morphosyntactic and conceptual anomaly (Kutas and Hillyard 1980; Osterhout and Holcomb 1992; Friederici 2011).

Two further expectations are corollaries rather than discriminating tests. First, resistance to satiation should increase with independently measured evidence concentration and effective preemption, for operator and non-operator constructions alike (Bybee 2006, 2010; Snyder 2000; Sprouse 2009; Reynolds 2026a). Left-branch extraction belongs in this comparison only if niche norming confirms it as a preempted gap. Second, accent and register should show systematic indexical fields (Eckert 2008; Agha 2007); they qualify as operators only if they also form closed paradigms with public-update roles.

The disconfirmation condition compares contrasts after matching closure, opportunity, competitor strength, surprisal, prosody, and task. Contrasts with and without a measured public-update role should differ in comprehension or repair profile. If they don’t, public-update role adds no explanatory value beyond constructional licensing and entrenchment.

These predictions don’t require new simulation or proof. They require independent measurement of paradigm closure, opportunity count, public-update contribution, and error locus.
# Conclusion
Clausal architecture often enters grammaticality judgments in much the same way as tense or number marking, while accent and much lexical choice are treated differently. Operator structure explains part of that pattern. Constructional licensing, operator membership, value selection, and exponent licensing remain separable dimensions.

Clausal architecture belongs to the operator stratum where a closed paradigm configures clause type, commitment, role allocation, scope, or reference tracking. Tense and number qualify where their paradigms do that work; tone, gesture, particles, word order, and inflection can realize the same values. Complementation can instead be a community-specific constructional convention, as with _depend on_, without thereby becoming an operator contrast.

The public-update condition applies to the paradigm, not every token. A locally redundant concord marker can belong to a reference-tracking system; *_goed_ and *_le hiver_ can preserve the intended value while selecting an excluded exponent. Opportunity density sharpens these licensing patterns but doesn’t create operator membership, and population persistence requires more than speaker-level updating.

The asterisk is evidence, not a definition. The projective claim is that independently identified operator paradigms support further expectations about comprehension, repair, and cross-substance response profiles within specified communities and activities. The account proposes no corrective-control mechanism (Weinberger 2026; Reynolds 2026b). Its diachronic suggestion – that public-update work favours recruitment into closed paradigms – remains a testable support hypothesis, not a synchronic membership condition.
