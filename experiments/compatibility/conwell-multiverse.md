# What survives changing the assumptions about word-order learning?
Two learners can both remember which order they heard more often with a particular verb. One gives every verb a graded preference. The other rules out one order for some verbs and learns graded preferences for the rest. On the proposed two-verb experiment, their predicted _difference between verbs_ agrees across the multiverse. Their predicted overall use of agent-first can differ enough to be informative if that baseline is independently constrained. This qualifies the earlier 55% model-recovery figure. [Original design and learners](./conwell-learning.md)
## Production after both orders have been demonstrated
Every participant sees the same two new contact verbs. One occurs twice agent-first and once patient-first, the other the reverse. Each participant produces one randomly assigned verb, with equal numbers per group. The outcome is agent-first production among the two orders. I used the following choices for a synthetic sensitivity analysis. The numerical endpoints are illustrative.

| Choice | Values and rationale |
| --- | --- |
| Weight of the original training videos | Count each distinct video once, or count all three presentations. The six later probe demonstrations stay fixed. |
| Strength of the verb-specific beta prior | Concentration ½, 1 or 3. These span a prior weaker than one demonstration through a prior as strong as all three demonstrations of a probe verb. Both learners use the same value for verbs with graded preferences. |
| Response process | Follow the learned probabilities, or guess at random on 20% of responses. Guessing is independent of verb and learner. |
| Total independent learners | 24, 96 or 192. Each row below keeps its own sample size. |

For each of the 36 combinations, I enumerated every possible pair of response counts under both learners. I scored the same counts with three analyses. The first gives the classifier the correct branch parameters. The second always uses the original specification of one effective video presentation, prior concentration 1 and zero guessing. The third estimates the overall agent-first rate separately under each candidate, allowing the full feasible range but keeping that candidate's predicted between-verb difference fixed. Model choice uses equal prior odds where the two laws are fully specified.

| Total learners | Chance of detecting the verb difference | Best possible accuracy distinguishing the learners with the correct branch parameters |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 24  | 4.9–19.1% | 50.5–59.7% |
| 96  | 19.8–75.6% | 51.0–68.8% |
| 192 | 40.5–97.4% | 51.4–75.5% |

The first column of probabilities gives the range across both generators and all branches for the original two-sided Fisher test at 5%. The second gives the equally weighted probability of choosing the true learner with the full pair of counts. The ranges describe choices in the grid. They have no interpretation as confidence intervals or probabilities over plausible worlds.

The detection result has a simple explanation. Hearing both orders establishes that both are available at these probes under the hard account's error-free learning model. At prior concentration $k$ and guessing rate $e$, both learners then predict the same contrast, $(1-e)/(3+k)$, or **13.3–28.6 percentage points** here. They can still differ on the two absolute rates. Once the analysis fits a common midpoint across verbs under each candidate, both allow exactly the pairs $(m+d/2,m-d/2)$ for $d/2\leq m\leq1-d/2$. Every data table therefore gives a profile likelihood ratio of one. This equivalence would be an inappropriate assumption if a separate control had established the midpoint precisely.

The consequences of using the wrong baseline are directional. At 192 learners, counting all three presentations, with concentration 3 and zero guessing, the correctly specified rule distinguishes the accounts 75.5% of the time. The original fixed rule selects the graded account in **97.5%** of studies generated by the hard learner in this branch. More participants can make an uncalibrated preference look like stronger evidence about grammar.
## Comprehension at first encounter
In a second synthetic experiment, four independent groups give one first production or one role-reversal interpretation, each for a fresh restricted-class or alternating-class verb. Both interpretation groups hear the patient-first order and choose between videos differing only in who acts on whom. The rivals match the two production probabilities and alternating-control comprehension so that the comparison measures what the restricted comprehension trial adds. Matching production is an explicit conditioning assumption. This calculation does not fit a complete graded acquisition model to four response cells or estimate a within-person association. [Proposed task and limits of the available human records](./conwell-snedeker-feasibility.md)

I crossed the two video weights, three prior strengths and three sample sizes with two probabilities of decoding correctly when an order is accessible (80%, 95%), three probabilities of recovering an inaccessible order (0, ½, 1), and two assumptions about timing. One listener evaluates access using its experience _before_ the utterance. Another treats hearing a reliable tutor use the order as evidence that the order is available before choosing the video. The second timing assumption and perfect recovery each eliminate the deficit, so many of the 216 rows coincide.

When access is assessed from earlier experience, the hard learner assigns probability $p/2$ to patient-first being unavailable, where $p$ is its predicted fresh-verb agent-first production. Half the verbs have single-order repertoires, and an agent-only repertoire has probability $p$ within that group. If ordinary decoding succeeds with probability $c$ and recovery succeeds with probability $u$ (restoring ordinary decoding), its role accuracy is $c-(c-1/2)(1-u)p/2$. The resulting control advantage ranges from **0 to 8.8 points**. With the original training assumptions, 95% ordinary decoding, and no recovery, it predicts **83.8%** on alternating controls and **76.1%** on restricted verbs. A listener using order and _ka_ equally well across verbs predicts **83.8%** on both after matching the control.

A listener with **graded sensitivity to the role cue** can reproduce every one of these pairs. Let its accuracy increase linearly with predicted patient-first use, anchored at the alternating control. A slope of $(c-1/2)(1-u)/2$ matches the first timing rule for every $p$. A zero slope matches immediate updating. This is an explicit counterexample conditional on a matched production law. It makes the identifying assumption visible. Independent calibration of how verb frequency affects role decoding could give the held-out restricted verb a distinguishing prediction.

**Implication for the human analysis.** Preserve the first-encounter contrast and the role-reversal foil. A later encounter answers a question about adaptation, and an action or noun foil can be solved without recovering the roles. Trial records would allow the same eligible observations to be analysed under defensible coding choices, with an explicit denominator for other productions and omissions. The calculations here compare simulated designs and analysis rules. The empirical data-processing multiverse remains prospective. [Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman and Vanpaemel (2016)](https://stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/research/published/multiverse_published.pdf)
## Reproduce
Each command prints one labelled CSV row per branch, including the generating assumptions. The production output gives the fixed-reference classifier's accuracy separately for each true learner. The comprehension output gives the prediction in each observable cell and the ideal pairwise accuracy at equal odds. All probabilities use binomial count enumeration.

```sh
python3 experiments/compatibility/conwell_multiverse.py production
python3 experiments/compatibility/conwell_multiverse.py comprehension
```
