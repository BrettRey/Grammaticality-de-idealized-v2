# Can a learner understand an order they rarely produce?

Conwell and Snedeker (2024) offer a human comparison of production and judgment that we can use immediately. The published paper also establishes that the same learners took a comprehension test with role-reversed videos. It leaves open whether they heard a **new verb in its class-disfavoured order on a role-reversal trial**. That particular intersection is what tests whether a learner can understand an order they rarely use. [Author manuscript, Method and Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11588301/) · [Published article](https://doi.org/10.1080/15475441.2024.2342873)

## What the design preserves

The two structures put the verb first. One places the agent before the patient. The other places the patient before the agent and adds *ka*. In the semantics condition, the meaning of an action predicted whether its verb occurred in one order or both. The assignment of meanings to orders varied across participants. In the statistics-only condition the semantic and structural classes were crossed. Both orders described the same kinds of scenes.

|---|---|---|
| Training | Twelve participants per condition. Each learned four verbs in each of three structural classes and encountered two further verbs per class at test. The training videos repeated once per day for three days. Days 1–3 also included three recorded 30-trial tasks per day. The judgment and comprehension tasks could themselves expose the learner to a form. |

Twenty-one of 24 participants used both orders for alternating verbs. Published order shares distinguish conditions and subclasses. | Counts for each learner, verb and day, with other productions and omissions retained. The grouped order shares do not report that denominator. Overall production accuracy also includes lexical errors. |

The authors already linked production to false acceptance by participant and subclass. | Individual accept/reject responses and stimuli. Table 3 pools familiarity and supplies no raw trial counts. The existing cross-task analysis does not pair role interpretation with production for a particular verb. |
| Interpretation | A listener selected one of two videos. The distractor changed the noun, the action or the agent–patient assignment. The overall correct-response rates were 94% and 91% in the statistics-only and semantics groups. | A test manifest crossing verb, presented order, structural subclass, familiarity and foil type. Only the role-reversal foil forces the listener to recover who did what to whom. Group accuracy alone cannot recover a learner-specific association. |

Each condition supplies at most **24 learner–verb pairs for one restricted novel subclass** (12 people × 2 verbs), before filtering by foil, presented order or first encounter. The paper gives neither the eligible trial count nor evidence that it is positive. One learner was excluded because more than half of their test productions were inconsistent with the training language. The paper does not classify those errors, and the reported sample is conditional on that performance criterion. [Participants, procedure, Table 3 and production results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11588301/)

## A comparison the published numbers already allow

I used the alternating verbs to calibrate a preference for agent-first within each learning condition. I then used the published **judgments** on each restricted subclass to predict **production** in that subclass, without fitting to its production share. These are retrospective development checks. The following response rules all assume matching trial populations across tasks, and the two-order calculation treats the reported productions as a binary choice.

- **Categorical availability.** For this calculation only, treat the acceptance rates for the two orders, $a$ and $b$, as the fractions of occasions on which each order is available. Assume at least one order is available on every occasion. Then $1-b$ is the fraction with only agent-first, and $a+b-1$ is the fraction with both. An agent-first preference $r$ when both are available predicts $1-b+r(a+b-1)$.
- **Graded competition.** Take the two acceptance rates as positive choice weights. An agent-first preference multiplier $k$ gives probability $ka/(ka+b)$. This is one explicit graded rule. A learner might relate acceptability to choice through a different function.
- **Order preference alone.** Transfer the alternating-verb production rate to all verbs in that condition.

| Condition and verb subclass | Categorical | Graded | Preference alone | Reported agent-first |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Statistics only, agent-first verbs | 79.2% | 76.0% | 68% | 82% |
| Statistics only, patient-first verbs | 57.4% | 63.5% | 68% | 59% |
| Semantics, agent-first verbs | 87.2% | 86.5% | 73% | 92% |
| Semantics, patient-first verbs | 35.4% | 56.1% | 73% | 43% |

The last row is the informative one. Re-expressed as patient-first productions, the three predictions are **64.6%, 43.9%, and 27%**. The authors report **57%**. Both rules using judgments improve on the general order preference in this row, and their predictions fall on opposite sides of the observation. Neither reproduces it to rounding. The gap could arise from how the rules convert judgments into choices, from changing preferences across verbs, or from different trial denominators. Participant uncertainty and the effects of familiarity cannot be estimated from these percentages alone. This calculation compares response rules supplied with human judgment rates. A model of acquisition still has to predict those rates from exposure. ### Can differences in personal order preference account for the gap?

Keep the graded rule's judgment weights common across learners, but let each learner have any stable preference for agent-first. If a learner chooses agent-first on alternating verbs with probability $t$, multiplying their odds by the subclass's relative judgment weight $R=(a/b)/(a_0/b_0)$ changes their probability to $Rt/(1-t+Rt)$. The subscript 0 denotes alternating verbs. This curve is concave for $R>1$ and convex for $R<1$. It joins zero to one. Consequently, with the same learner weights in both subclasses, average production lies between the alternating-verb share and the homogeneous graded prediction. The preference distribution can narrow the subclass difference all the way to zero.

All four reported restricted-subclass shares lie beyond the homogeneous prediction, in the direction of a *larger* subclass difference. For patient-first verbs in the semantics condition, the graded rule allows **27.0–43.9% patient-first production**, compared with **57% reported**. Even choosing the most favourable values within the printed rounding intervals gives an upper bound below 44.5%, assuming rounding to the nearest displayed unit. This is a bound on model expectations calculated from published summaries. Sampling uncertainty, unequal learner weights across tasks, and individual differences in the judgment weights themselves remain open. Trial records would let us test which of these matters. ## One prediction to fix before seeing further responses

The [first-production comparison](conwell-learning.md) gives competing learners the same exposure history and derives their predictions for a new verb. It also isolates what production can reveal about verb-specific preferences after both orders have been heard.

In the semantics condition, take a novel verb whose class favours agent-first. Compare two role-reversal trials that both present the *patient-first* order, one with that restricted verb and one with a novel alternating verb. Match the role display, counterbalance the correct side, and preserve lexical and task history. Use independent production observations to establish whether learners actually avoided patient-first with the restricted class.

A learner who decodes roles from the order and *ka* alone predicts the same accuracy in the two cells after matching. A learner who needs access to the verb–order combination predicts a deficit for the restricted verbs **if** some occasions lack access and recovery is imperfect. In a simple version, let $c$ be correct decoding in the matched alternating control, $h$ the chance of access in the restricted cell and $u$ the chance of recovering an inaccessible combination. With random guessing otherwise, the predicted control advantage is $(c-1/2)(1-h)(1-u)$. A freely chosen rare order gives no independent estimate of access. Calibrate access and recovery separately, or keep the contrast at the behavioural level. An association-based decoder that weights role cues by verb can also predict a deficit.

There is a design constraint even with individual records. All 18 verbs appeared in all tasks on both testing days, and the tasks changed order. A learner's first production and first comprehension of the same verb cannot both precede their first test encounter. For a prediction about fresh learning, use day-four first encounters across counterbalanced learners if the cells exist. For the within-learner question, predict the later response conditional on the actual earlier trials. A day-five response can already reflect day-four exposure without any explicit feedback.

**Decision.** Use the published comparison to constrain the link between judgments and choices. The smallest further evidence needed is the item-by-list test manifest, including the foil and order of each comprehension item. If it contains the critical trials, de-identified trial order and response codes can establish how many participant–verb pairs survive. If it contains none, the original experiment cannot estimate this contrast. Use the paired-role design as a proposed new observation. The authors offer data and code on request.
