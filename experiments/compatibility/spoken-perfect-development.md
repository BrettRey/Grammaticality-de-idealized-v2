# What speakers dated with the English present perfect

A British speaker reports having just finished a course of antibiotics and
dates the completion to the previous morning. Another reports an outing in
a car and dates it to the previous day. These are positive cases for an event
reading: *yesterday* tells the hearer when the reported event happened.
The recordings and transcripts offer different evidence about how the date
entered the utterance. Neither exchange elicits a choice between past and
present topic time. [Oxford transcript, KCS 2556–60](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/SpokenBNCdata/KCS.html) · [Hundt and Smith (2009), pp. 56–57, examples 18–27](https://icame.info/icame_static/ij33/ij33-45-64.pdf)

## What can be heard or read in context

**Finishing the antibiotics, KCS 2556.** During a conversation about illness,
the speaker uses *I've just finished*, names the medication, and begins a
time phrase with *on er*. The next words date the completion to yesterday
morning. The Oxford alignment places 2.25 seconds between the end of the
hesitation and the start of *yesterday*. It puts **zero** gap between the
object *antibiotics* and *on*, where a separate time phrase could already
have begun. A rule that measures silence immediately before the day word
would locate the boundary after the preposition and hesitation. On the original recording, median
energy in that interval is lower than in both adjacent stretches (−36.1 dBFS,
against −31.4 for the preposition and hesitation and −28.1 for the date).
This is a break in delivery. The speaker had already begun an expression
that could supply a date before the break. We cannot tell which expression
they initially intended. The interlocutor acknowledges the report, then
comments on the daily dose after the speaker gives it. Nothing in that
response settles the grammatical attachment of the date. [Transcript](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/SpokenBNCdata/KCS.html) · [Oxford recording, side B, relevant stretch at 1433.5–1442 seconds](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/SpokenBNCdata/021A-C0897X0630XX-ABZZP0.wav)

**Taking the car out, KBE 3003.** The speakers' outing is the event that
the date locates. The following words evaluate the car's condition. A
past-dated outing can supply evidence for that evaluation under either account.
The archived word tier puts *yesterday* directly against the preceding pronoun
and the following word, with no intervening pause interval. I verified the
published token and the alignment. I could not verify the boundary against
the waveform. The Oxford alignments are automatic. Even a genuine absence
of silence would leave pitch, lengthening and discourse structure available
to mark a boundary. [Hundt and Smith, example 19](https://icame.info/icame_static/ij33/ij33-45-64.pdf) · [Coleman, Baghai-Ravary, Pybus and Grau (2012), Audio BNC](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/AudioBNC)

**A control supplied by the conversation, KBT 319–23.** A speaker describes
a performance yesterday in the preterite and follows it with a perfect
about additional performance dates. The hearer asks about the number of
new dates. The word tier puts the end of *yesterday* at the start of the
next subject. Here syntax and uptake independently distinguish the clauses
despite the lack of an aligned gap. In another conversation, a report that
a shop lacked an item yesterday prompts the hearer to ask whether the shop
had it today (KBH 205–06). An ordinary preterite can thus prompt a shift to
current circumstances. Neither control estimates how often a shift occurs.
[KBT transcript](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/SpokenBNCdata/KBT.html) · [BNC Baby](https://hdl.handle.net/20.500.14106/2553)

### Other published positives and ambiguous cases

I checked all seven British *yesterday* examples in Hundt and Smith's
discussion (18–23 and 27). These examples were selected for their form.
They cannot estimate a rate of choosing the perfect. The matching BNC Baby
transcript, published quotation and forced alignment of one recording all
describe the **same** observation. [Source and example numbers](https://icame.info/icame_static/ij33/ij33-45-64.pdf)

| BNC unit | Evidence bearing on interpretation |
|---|---|
| KBD 6028, meeting | The speaker reformulates a chat as a meeting, locates it yesterday and describes a coming business initiative. The date is followed by a phrase specifying what the meeting was about. An anonymized name immediately precedes the date. The hearer's acknowledgment and later reference to a reception concern the plans. |
| KCM 137, driving test | A perfect in a relative clause describes questions an examiner asked. A matrix preterite describes answering them. The date could locate the asking or the answering, and the events belong to the same episode. Transcript word order cannot choose between those readings. |
| KCC 480, calls | A plural count in the perfect is immediately restated in the preterite with a different count. The speaker goes on to distinguish calls on different days. This is relevant evidence about reformulation and fails the single-event inclusion criterion. |
| KCW 1441, road closure | The date follows a passive perfect. The ensuing words bring up tonight, an accident and a reopening. The identity and chronology of the incidents are uncertain. This exchange cannot establish that one and the same closure had ended before the report. |
| KB9 3305, telephone contact | The published transcript repeats the word for the telephone before the date. I found no matching word pair in the 41 available grids for this document. Boundary timing is unavailable. |
| KPV 6109, finishing lectures | The speaker restates a preterite as a perfect, supplies the date and explains the ensuing break from lectures. The direction of the reformulation matters: the speaker introduces the perfect on the restart. The speaker supplies the consequence. The published passage gives no hearer interpretation, and KPV has no grid in this release. |

KBE 3003 is discussed above. One further token from the Oxford sampler,
KCJ 175, places yesterday before a report of getting up and seeing the
weather. The next speaker describes the rain. The date can frame the
speaker's thought or the larger episode, and the auxiliary in the embedded
sequence is hard to establish from the short signal check. I left this
token unresolved. [KCJ transcript](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/SpokenBNCdata/KCJ.html)

## Can a right-appended date explain the word order?

One word order gives a narrower architectural test. If a date intervenes
between a finite perfect auxiliary and its participle in a single clause,
the speaker could not have appended it **after an already completed perfect
clause**. I specified a development search for *have/has* or a contraction
immediately before *yesterday*, optionally with intervening *only* or
*just*. Inspect the following predicate, clause continuity and register
before treating any hit as evidence. Fronted discourse frames, dates
between two clauses, restarts and examples from prepared speech do not pass.
The test addresses a strictly right-appended date. A theory permitting a
parenthesis or a covert repair could still accommodate an internal date.

I screened the word tiers in all 3,272 divisions of the archived timing
release. After excluding windows that named a weekday within twelve words
on either side, 1,003 occurrences of *yesterday* remained. Four had the
specified preceding form. Two were store names ending in possessive *'s*
(KBK division 10 at 1200.670 s and KD3 division 27 at 1627.3525 s). One was a
question with *did* and lexical *have* (KP1 division 6 at 1073.605 s). The
fourth had *I'll have* before *yesterday* and an overt correction immediately
after it (KBE division 18 at 1023.2225 s). None had a finite perfect auxiliary
and a following participle in a continuing clause. The search covers the
specified adjacent forms within divisions and leaves other internal orders
and earlier planning open. This archive result tests the search for a witness.
It gives no estimate of how often speakers use integrated dates.

## Predictions for a past weekday

Take a single past outing in a particular car and an utterance ending in
*on* plus a weekday. Assume the hearer resolves the weekday to one past day.
Keep two questions separate: **which day contains the outing?** and **which
time is the subsequent conversation about?** The attested car and medication
reports motivate the first question. An evaluation of a car or a comment on
a dose does not answer the second.

| Account | Prediction for the outing | Prediction for the next turn |
|---|---|---|
| Integrated construction in which the adverb dates the event | The outing took place on the named day. The ordinary present relevance of the report can be preserved. | A present concern or a past-event question can follow. |
| Ordinary perfect with a separately supplied date | If the date picks up the outing just introduced, the outing took place on the named day. | The same two continuations are available. A hearer can use the outing as evidence now. |
| Reanalysis that assigns a past topic time | The outing took place on the named day. Its time also supplies a past topic. | A follow-up that independently inherits that topic should select the past anchor. An ordinary discourse shift can undo that prediction. |

The first two predictions are equivalent for any date that denotes a past day.
To see why, let the perfect introduce an event of taking the car out. An
integrated event modifier restricts that event to the named day. A later
date applied to the **same** discourse referent imposes exactly the same
restriction. Even with two salient outings, accurate identification of which
car went out on which day would establish that the hearer bound the date
to the right event. Either route can do that. A hearer who silently repairs
the perfect to a preterite can also recover a past topic. The earlier
listener calculation shows exactly where independent evidence about topic
shift would be needed. [Listener comparison](listener-anchor.md)

Two tempting transfer predictions fail. First, KCS begins a possible time
phrase before the long hesitation. In the reserved family, *on* is part of
the expression under either account, so its presence cannot diagnose a
restart. Any future prosodic comparison must locate the onset before *on*
and record hesitation **within** the phrase separately. Second, the absence of a pause would
only exclude an account that **requires** silence before every separate date.
The principal repair account makes no such requirement, and the corpus supplies
no independently calibrated relation between pause duration and date attachment.

**Decision.** The development cases establish that speakers can explicitly
date a completed event while using the English perfect in conversation. They
support a testable descriptive claim about what the date picks out. With the
same event referent, the two routes predict the same weekday reading. The
past-topic variant has a distinct conditional prediction, but none of the
observed follow-ups supplies its required independent topic diagnostic.
Keep the weekday outcomes reserved. This case supports narrowing the
manuscript's categorical-judgment claim. It supplies no estimate of
community prevalence or of a change in grammar over time.

## Selection and replay

The earlier Baby screen required *I* before *yesterday* and recovered
preterite comparisons. Here I checked the published positive cases directly
and separately screened the 12 files listed under demographic conversation
in Oxford's audio sampler. I kept the sentence numbering, excluded units
mentioning a weekday, and retrieved every remaining unit containing
*yesterday* and one of *have*, *has*, *'ve* or *'s*, in either order.
Of 37,121 numbered units, 88 contained
development *yesterday* and 24 passed the form screen. The script prints
all 24 identifiers. The rule also retrieves possessives, copulas and forms
belonging to a different clause. It misses untranscribed auxiliaries and
dates supplied in separate sentence units. The two searches overlap and
have different inclusion rules. [Original Baby screen](english-perfect-contrast.md) · [Oxford sampler index](https://www.phon.ox.ac.uk/SpokenBNCcontents)

KCS 2556, KCM 137, KCJ 175 and the two-clause control KBT 321 are discussed
above. The remaining 20 candidates received the following development codes.

| Reason for setting aside | Numbered units |
|---|---|
| A different predicate or clause carries the date or the retrieved form | KCL 3626, KD1 562, 3821, 4582, KCM 129, KCX 7886, KBU 1077 |
| The match comes from lexical *have*, a possessive or a copular form | KCL 5397, KD1 792, 3393, KCX 2991, 7610, KBM 116, KCJ 940, 1322 |
| A modal or past perfect occurs in the surrounding material | KCX 2841, 2917 |
| Hypothetical quoted complaint with a preterite | KBM 2067 |
| Fragment or uncertain predication | KCL 3755, KCX 5233 |

The local source cache contains the 12 Oxford HTML files, the archived
TextGrids and the short KCS recording excerpt. The command below replays both
searches, recovers the word times by lexical context and checks energy in
10-ms blocks of the waveform. Times are seconds from the start of the tape
side. A zero gap means adjacent annotated word intervals. Timing and energy
alone cannot certify prosodic integration. [Archived word alignments](https://reshare.ukdataservice.ac.uk/851496/) · [Analysis script](spoken_perfect.py)

```sh
data="$HOME/Corpora/bnc1994/audio-bnc-development"
python3 experiments/compatibility/spoken_perfect.py "$data" "$data/AudioBNCTextGrids.zip" \
  --kcs-wav "$data/KCS-0630B-1433.5-1442.wav" --clip-start 1433.5
```

For another machine, obtain the HTML files named in the script from the
Oxford sampler and the ZIP from the UK Data Service. The waveform option
also accepts the original Oxford tape 0630 side B with the default start
time of zero. All corpus materials stay outside the repository.
