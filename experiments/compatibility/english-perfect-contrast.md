# The English perfect with a definite past-time adjunct

## Scope fixed before inspecting test outcomes

The target is an episodic affirmative declarative in spontaneous British English conversation. A speaker reports one completed event and explicitly dates it to an interval over before speech time. The perfect competes with the simple preterite. Use *yesterday* to develop the contrast. Reserve the family *on* + bare weekday name, denoting one contextually identified past day (for example, *on Monday*). Exclude future, habitual, quoted and multi-event readings. Treat tokens with uncertain adjunct attachment separately. The reserved outcomes are form choice and hearer interpretation in this register. No outcomes for the reserved family have been inspected in developing this comparison.

Keep the event, speaker, addressee, time of report and facts about its consequences fixed. Vary the addressee’s question between a present decision and reconstruction of a past episode. Compare the perfect and preterite in both contexts. Withhold the inflected verb group when judging whether the discourse supplies an opportunity. Record unresolved cases. The reserve is for a future independent test and this local specification has no external registration.

For development, the local BNC Baby conversational component supplies 30 texts. Screen sentence units for *I* followed within the next ten word tokens by *yesterday*, with either verb form eligible; set aside units mentioning a weekday. This deliberately narrow screen checks the availability of comparable examples. It misses preposed adjuncts, other subjects and longer dependencies. Review candidates with the target verb group masked where a simple affirmative clause can be located. Keep the screening count, eligible subset and unresolved cases distinct. Treat the corpus follow-up turn as evidence of conversational uptake only where its interpretation is explicit.

## What the listener would have to distinguish

Two flatmates know that a spare key was posted the previous day. Neither knows if it arrived. In one version the speaker’s goal is to decide whether to send another key now. In the other it is to reconstruct the order of events for a diary. Use parallel goal cards so an auxiliary in the prompt cannot prime the answer. Compare *I posted the spare key yesterday* and *I’ve posted the spare key yesterday*. Elicit the choice between the two descriptions with the date equally salient. In a separate listening task assign the utterance. Asking a listener who saw a goal card to repeat that goal would make the card itself sufficient.

One sharpened OVMG candidate lets the newly licensed perfect introduce a past topic time, like the preterite in this episode. A dual-anchor candidate dates the posting in the past but keeps the present decision as the topic. Bondar motivates the possibility of this second interpretation. A continuing current consequence and a present **topic time** are distinct commitments. Neither the manuscript nor Bondar requires a unique time for a subsequent elliptical question. [Manuscript claim](../../grammaticality-de-idealized.tex) · [Bondar (2026), publisher abstract](https://doi.org/10.1075/eww.25038.bon)

Give the listener a world where the postal rule changed overnight. At posting a signature was optional. Today it is required. After the target they hear *Signature required?* and indicate which rule would answer the question, with an explicit “either time is possible” response. Reverse the direction of the rule change across items. Under the linking assumption that the follow-up inherits the utterance’s topic time, the past-topic analysis points to *optional* and the present-topic analysis to *required*. Both put the posting on the same day. Use explicitly time-anchored follow-ups as comprehension controls and the preterite as a comparison for ordinary shifts of attention. A listener can also recover the posting time directly from *yesterday* or select the rule relevant to the current conversation. Those routes defeat inference from a single answer to the semantics of the perfect.

To quantify the limit, take the probability of choosing the present rule, treating “either” as a separate recorded outcome. Let the two explicit-time control probabilities be $a$ for past and $b$ for present. If those response laws carry over and the target is a mixture of just those two readings, its probability $p$ implies a present-reading share of $(p-a)/(b-a)$ when $b$ differs from $a$. The same share must predict the “either” rate using its two control probabilities. A target outside the control mixture challenges that interpretation. A target inside it can tell us how often listeners chose each anchor while leaving open how it became available.

## How the rival fares

Claude developed a constructional competitor in a fresh session. It learns an effective penalty for a clause-integrated past-time adjunct from occasions when speakers chose the preterite. Its production equation gives the perfect probability as the logistic function of a general preference, a discourse-goal effect and an integration penalty. The two constructions compete over the same verb and event. Claude separately proposed recording the same words as a complete clause followed by a detached date. Werner explicitly distinguishes postponed time specification in conversation from an integrated adjunct (p. 9, note 2). An audio boundary manipulation is consequently motivated on independent grounds. Treat observed boundary strength as evidence about attachment, allowing uncertain cases. [Original rival and prompt](english-perfect-rival.json) · [Werner (2013)](https://www.sdas.edus.si/Elope/PDF/ElopeVol10-1Werner.pdf)

Two corrections matter. The rival’s equation gives the same decision-versus-reconstruction **odds ratio** for each attachment type. Removing the integration penalty makes its afterthought and no-adverb probabilities identical at a given goal. Its illustrative table violates both restrictions. A hard account also permits ordinary discourse effects on the choice among available forms. We retain the equation and leave the table as a record of a failed prediction. The raw proposal expects a smaller penalty for a weekday. The proportions of past and future uses cannot establish how often a preterite preempted a possible perfect in past-event opportunities.

**Fixed comparison.** For each discourse goal, let $r$ be the perfect share in a matched control where all routes are available. Estimate $r$ separately and check whether it transports across the date manipulation. A stable reanalysis available to a fraction $h$ gives integrated perfect use $hr$ if speakers with that route choose between forms as in the control. The rival multiplies the control odds by a fixed $w$. Given development counts, each parameter can be learned from the binomial likelihood of the observed choices on eligible occasions. Both versions pool the closed-past feature and transfer their development parameter to the reserved family. This shared-feature version of the rival is our explicit restriction. The rival’s family-specific version needs an independent transfer estimate. A simple logistic baseline using the verb, discourse goal and an adjunct feature can also implement the odds equation. Successful production prediction by that equation alone therefore leaves its learning interpretation open.

For a numerical witness only, set the reconstruction and decision control shares to .20 and .55. Match both accounts to .01 integrated-perfect use in reconstruction. The fitted constants are $h=1/20$ and $w=4/99$. The held-out **context** prediction within this illustration is 2.75% versus 4.71% choosing the perfect in the decision context. These numbers use illustrative inputs from the consultation. Neither English data nor the reserved weekday family supplied a fit.

A separate listener who does not see the goal card could infer the speaker’s goal from an assigned utterance. If the two goals have equal prior probability and listeners invert the speakers’ actual choice probabilities, the perfect gives 73.33% versus 82.47% probability of a decision goal. A preterite gives 49.55% versus 49.05%. This is a test of pragmatic inference under a specified listener model. It supplies no independent semantic reading if the listener simply inverts production. The postal-rule question above probes a different commitment.

**Recovery and stopping decision.** With known control shares and 500 independent choices per goal, the repository’s simultaneous 95% binomial-interval procedure accepts both accounts in 98.56% of count tables by probability under the stable gate and 98.60% under the graded cost. More fundamentally, an unchanged categorical grammar can route an utterance through repair, with effective access $1/20$ in reconstruction and $16/187$ in the decision. Multiplying these by the control shares exactly reproduces both graded probabilities and, with the same listener rule, both goal inferences. Appending a detached date can likewise be available under either grammar. A classifier trained only on form choice and task cannot settle where that access originates. Park the two-goal form-choice comparison as an architectural test. The English evidence worth seeking is a clearly integrated perfect and a hearer response that resolves a changed temporal rule, with the ordinary tendency to shift topic independently estimated. The present corpus screen supplies comparison tokens and no such observation. The weekday outcomes remain reserved.
