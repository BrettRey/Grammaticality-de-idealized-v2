## Part I. Charitable reconstruction and points of agreement

### The strongest version of the paper

Read charitably, the manuscript makes three moves that deserve to be taken seriously together:

1. **A level-separation thesis (§§1–2).** Acceptability ratings, judgments, corrections, formal derivations, and public linguistic status are bearers at different levels; conflating them produces pseudo-explanations. The six-way division — status constitution, token application, semantic/pragmatic consequence, target attribution, response causation, epistemic justification — is a genuine contribution. The observation that "a black-box classifier of the word 'grammar' wouldn't thereby supply the constitutive explanation" (§2) is correct and important, and it applies with full force to current LLM-based acceptability modeling.

2. **A constitutive ontology (§3).** Public grammatical status consists in "socially sustained form--value organization internal to recurrent constructional tokens" within a scoped repertoire. Normativity comes from practice; grammar-specificity comes from a relation's construction-internal role rather than from substance, frequency, or institutional labeling.

3. **A methodology of independent profiling (§6).** Candidate relations must be individuated from production, substitution, contrast, and distribution *before* grammar labels and repairs are consulted; held-out prediction constrains but does not constitute status. The explicit defeat conditions in §6 are commendable and rare in this literature.

### Where I agree

- **The selective-classification problem is real and well posed.** The opening observation that *\olang{le hiver}*, *\mention{goed}*, and agreement errors are interpretable yet classified as grammatical failures, while other equally interpretable deviations are not, genuinely requires explanation. The paper correctly notes that neither meaninglessness nor expressive substance marks the boundary.

- **The source-ambiguity constraint is properly deployed.** Using \textcite{hofmeisteretal2013source} and \textcite{leivadawestergaard2020} as constraints on *evidence* rather than as arguments about worldly status (§2) is the right dialectical placement.

- **The critique of Miller-style stability-as-normativity is apt.** The concession that "stable clustering can't by itself supply normativity" (§3.1) shows the author sees the central danger — though see Objection 1 below, where I argue the proposed remedy does not fully escape it.

- **Separation of value-error types (§4).** The tripartite distinction among internal value incompatibility, external value misapplication, and message-selection error is clean and does real work. The claim that "only internal incompatibility establishes a grammatical fault by itself" follows from the ontology and is defended, not merely asserted.

- **Anti-deflationary treatment of information theory (§5.2).** "The codebook is an input to the calculation, not its output" correctly blocks a common move whereby entropy measures are mistaken for discoveries about grammatical architecture.

- **Honesty about constructed stimuli.** The admission about the Amele comparison — "constructed from Roberts's paradigm and haven't been validated" (Table 5 caption) — is exemplary practice, though it leaves §5.2's empirical program resting on sand until validated.

## Part II. Central objections

### Objection 1: Has the paper redescribed stable coordination as normativity?

This is the deepest problem, and it bears directly on your first framing question. The manuscript criticizes Miller because stable clustering cannot supply normativity, then offers "constitutive reliance" as the bridge:

> "Participants rely on a candidate relation when predicting one another's behaviour and when producing and interpreting new material as an instance of the same construction. Conformity to the relation is part of what counts, within the practice, as successfully tokening that construction."

But consider what this amounts to in the vocabulary of convention theory. Mutual reliance on a regularity for coordinating expectations is precisely Lewis's definition of convention. Conventions are self-enforcing solutions to coordination problems; they sustain conformity through convergent expectation, not through any deontic force. Crucially, a Lewisian convention does not make deviation *incorrect* — it makes deviation *costly or surprising*. The driver who drives on the left when everyone else drives on the right has not violated a norm in any robust sense; she has simply failed to coordinate, and will suffer the consequences.

The manuscript wants more than this. It insists that a token can be defective even when "the absence of correction doesn't remove it" (§3.1), that "a respondent can be wrong rather than merely outvoted" (§3.4), and that judgments are "fallible responses rather than truth-makers" (abstract). But for these claims to be grounded, the practice must contain something over and above convergent reliance: a standard whose violation is possible *while the practice continues undisturbed*. The paper gestures at such a thing — "conditions of token identity and success" — but never explains why meeting-or-failing conditions of successful tokening confers **normative** rather than merely **classificatory** status. A chess piece placed off the board fails to instantiate a legal move; that failure is definitional or classificatory, not normative, unless we add stakes, accountability, or sanction. The paper explicitly denies that sanction or institutional enforcement is required ("A norm needn't be... prescribed by an institution, morally obligatory"), which strips away the usual mechanisms that distinguish norms from conventions in the social-scientific literature.

Two possible repairs suggest themselves, and the paper should choose and defend one:

(a) **Deflationary honesty:** concede that public grammatical status is convention-relative status in something like the Lewis sense, and drop or weaken the normative vocabulary ("standard," "correct," "defective," "accountable"). This would cost the paper some rhetorical force but would be internally consistent. Note, however, that the paper's own motivating data — schoolroom correction, stigma, repair initiation — involve sanction-rich environments, so the deflationary route sits awkwardly with the phenomena.

(b) **A substantive account of linguistic normativity:** specify what additional feature of linguistic practices (accountability structures, repair systems as sanctioning institutions, higher-order expectation hierarchies, participant-observable deviation-marking) converts reliance into genuine normativity. Interactional research on repair — which the paper cites in §5.2 — is actually the natural resource here: other-initiation of repair is a publicly available, systematically organized sanction-like mechanism. The paper cites this literature but does not use it constitutively.

As written, the transition from "recurrent practice" to "supplies normativity" (abstract) is a constitutive proposal asserted rather than argued, and it is the load-bearing wall of the building.

### Objection 2: Population structure, dynamics, and the gerrymandered repertoire

From a population-dynamics perspective, the account is underspecified in ways that matter constitutionally, not just empirically.

**Scope-fixing threatens circularity.** The norm exists "relative to a scoped repertoire," where scope is "specified by community, activity, medium, and period" (§1). But nothing fixes the scope independently. Consider *\olang{le hiver}*: French speakers disagree about *h aspiré* words; agreement varies by region, generation, register, and medium; the relevant facts have shifted historically and continue to shift. Given any pattern of disagreement, the account can dissolve it by declaring the disputants members of different scoped repertoires, or by declaring the repertoire "unsettled at the declared scope" (§3.2). Without independent criteria for scope individuation — who counts as a participant, weighted how, interacting through which networks, in which activities — every potential counterexample is absorbed by re-scoping. This is exactly the kind of move that renders a constitutive account unfalsifiable in practice despite the admirable defeat conditions of §6. The defeat conditions govern *what evidence would tell against assembly-role analysis given a fixed scope*; they do not govern scope fixation itself.

What would help: an explicit account of how repertoires are individuated — e.g., by interactional connectivity, by shared metalinguistic institutions, by mutual intelligibility of corrections, or by statistical clustering of judgment distributions — together with an acknowledgment that borderline scope cases yield borderline status claims, and a demonstration that the *le hiver* analysis survives under reasonable variation of scope.

**No dynamics.** The paper's figure caption concedes that "recurrent practice and response can feed back into norm architecture over historical time" (Fig. 1), but the feedback is never modeled or even schematized. Yet the maintenance question is not incidental: what sustains a realization norm like elision before *h muet* when learners receive variable input, when prestige varieties exert pressure, when orthography stabilizes some variants and not others? An account of norms owes at least a sketch of the equilibrium structure: Is conformity self-enforcing (Lewis-style), sanction-maintained, prestige-driven, or frequency-reinforced? Different answers predict different behavior under perturbation — contact, schooling changes, media shifts — and hence different diagnoses of "world-side gradience" (§3.4). The paper's third empirical prediction ("independently layered or changing relations should produce variable or moving boundaries," §6) implicitly presupposes a dynamic story it never tells.

**Partial sharing.** "Participants rely on a candidate relation when predicting one another's behaviour" — all participants? In reality, reliance is distributed unevenly across a population: specialists rely heavily, lay speakers partially, children developmentally, L2 users differently. If norm constitution requires uniform reliance, no large-scale norm survives; if it requires only some threshold or aggregate, the threshold is unspecified and doing hidden constitutional work.

### Objection 3: The synchronic/diachronic distinction is asserted, not secured

Question 3 of the brief presses here. Can a norm be response-independent at the token level while historically constituted by practice and response? In principle, yes — this is the standard constructivist package (institutions constituted diachronically by collective acceptance can nonetheless impose synchronic constraints independent of any individual's acceptance). But the manuscript owes an account of the **constitution relation**, and it does not give one. Candidates:

- Dispositions of the population at t (then individual responses are neither truth-makers nor non-truth-makers — they sample the disposition);
- Aggregate patterns of higher-order expectation (Lewis again);
- A historical lineage of transmission events (reproductive account);
- Institutional records and metalinguistic artifacts.

Each yields a different verdict on hard cases. On a pure-disposition reading, a single idiolect deviating from the population is simply wrong; on a lineage reading, innovations that propagate are becoming-correct even before widespread uptake; on an institutional-artifact reading, dictionary codification changes status. The paper's claim that "that diachronic feedback doesn't make a particular response the truth-maker of the norm to which it reacts" (§2) is compatible with all of these and rules out none. Until the constitution relation is specified, the response-independence claim floats free: it is not false, but it is not yet established either. The distinction between "an assembly-role profile [as] evidence about a norm, not the norm itself" (abstract) presupposes that we know what kind of entity the norm is such that profiles can misestimate it.

### Objection 4: Does construction-internal assembly do the discriminating work?

The grammar-specificity engine is the appeal to a "relation's construction-internal role." Three pressures threaten it.

**(i) Circularity via "construction."** In the Goldberg/Sag frameworks cited, constructions are conventional form--meaning pairings of any size and any content type — including discourse-functional and information-structural ones. If the inventory already contains pragmatic and social-indexical constructions, then "internal to a recurrent construction" cannot by itself distinguish grammatical from pragmatic or social norms; the discriminating work falls entirely to the notion of "assembly," which is characterized only negatively and metaphorically ("organizing those resources into recurrent expression types," §3.1). The four-part justification offered is better than nothing — the counterfactual test in particular ("Hold lexical identities, the intended constructional values, and the situation of use fixed...") is a genuine diagnostic — but the test presupposes we can fix "intended constructional values" independently of knowing whether the relation is grammatical, which in mixed cases (valence, honorifics) is precisely what is at issue.

**(ii) Honorifics and the instability of the deployment row.** The paper concedes that Japanese polite/plain style "can move toward grammatical assembly when a repertoire organizes it as a recurrent constructional choice with a domain, alternatives, and conditioned selection" (§6.1, cf. Table 2 rows 1 vs. 5). Addressee honorifics in Japanese are bound morphology occupying syntactic positions; addressee-reference honorifics index social relations. Under the account, the *same morphological substance* can occupy row 1 or row 5 depending on "recurrence, constructional domain, alternative set." Fine — but then the original selective-classification problem is answered with: *the same deviation can be grammatical or social depending on finely structured facts about the repertoire*. That may be true, but it means the account delivers graded, repertoire-contingent classifications rather than the crisp grammar/style/social sorting the opening problem promises. The paper should confront this consequence directly rather than treating mixed roles as a footnote ("These intersections don't show that all linguistic norms are grammatical," §3.1).

**(iii) Pure phonotactics exclusion.** Excluding pure phonotactics from grammar (Table 2, §6.1) is a substantive and contestable choice. Many linguists treat phonotactics as core phonology, hence core grammar; the paper's own criterion — "conditioned by, or when realizing, a grammatical choice" — makes grammaticality parasitic on prior grammatical choices, which will strike many readers as getting the dependency backwards for cases where phonological well-formedness is autonomously organized. The paper needs an argument, not a stipulation, and should acknowledge that on rival classifications of the norm-types the *same* surface string receives a different fault locus.

### Objection 5: Mixed analyses and the retreat to grain-indeterminacy

The treatment of *\mention{depend on}* is instructive and troubling. The account concludes that the higher links are schematic assembly relations while the final head-specific restriction "remains close to lexical knowledge" (§6.1) — and warns that positing layers merely because respondents divide their labels is forbidden. Good. But notice the resulting shape of the overall answer to the title question: whether a deviation counts as grammatical depends on the comparative support for relational versus whole-sign analyses, and "if the evidence supports both analyses equally, the grain is epistemically indeterminate" (§3.2). Combined with Objection 2's scope-relativity, the account now has *three* routes to non-verdicts: unsettled grain, unsettled scope, and mixed role. Each individually defensible; jointly, they raise the suspicion that the ontology predicts determinate grammaticality only in easy cases — strict paradigms and clear misspellings — where everyone already agrees. The hard motivating cases (*\mention{depend of}* sits between valence and collocation; heavy-rain-type collocations sit between construction and lexical combination) land in the indeterminacy zone. A theory of selective classification that is silent or indeterminate on the hardest selective cases has not fully discharged its burden. It should say explicitly what proportion of the motivating phenomena it expects to classify determinately, and why the remainder is acceptable residue.

## Part III. Concrete case tests

**Case 1: *\olang{le hiver}* (§§1, 6.1).** The analysis — excluded realization of the article construction rather than pure phonotactics — is plausible and nicely conditionalized ("It counts as a grammatical-form error only if that constructional relation... is what fails," Conclusion). Test: French speakers' spontaneous attributions for elision errors before *h aspiré* nouns (where lexical grouping is at stake) versus before vowel-initial nouns (where the pattern is fully general). If attributions track lexical class membership, the construction-specific analysis gains; if they track a general hiatus-dispreference, it loses. The paper sets up exactly this test (§6.1) but does not run or cite it. Also note: the claim that the analysis "is neutral between deriving \olang{l'} through vowel deletion and treating it as an allomorph" is fine, but the paper should engage the fact that elision is lexically restricted to a small closed class of function words — a fact that cuts both ways: restricted classes look paradigmatic, but item-listed realizations look lexical on the paper's own whole-sign criterion (§3.2).

**Case 2: *\mention{goed}* (§6.1).** Handled well. The dual characterization — productive tense realization failing an item-specific pre-emption condition, "makes the case partly lexical" — is exactly right, and pre-emption literature could be invoked to ground the item-specific restriction independently. Minor pressure: the paper says the item-specific restriction "also makes the case partly lexical," but under Table 2, a lexeme-specific word-form realization of a grammatical value looks like row 1 (realization of a selected value), not row 2. Which is it? The machinery permits both readings; the text should commit.

**Case 3: Amele switch-reference (§6.2, Table 5).** The internal-conflict candidate *Hina h-u-me-g...* is described as "licensed 2sg SS form conflicts with the distinct represented subject of the following clause." Two worries. First, the paper itself flags that SS/DS choice in Amele is sensitive to "agentivity, clause level, time, place, world, and pragmatic deixis," so whether the constructed SS form genuinely conflicts is unknown without validation — conceded, but then the entire downstream repair-locus illustration is hypothetical scaffolding presented inside an empirical section. Second, and more fundamentally: switch-reference "conflict" may not be an *internal value incompatibility* in the paper's sense at all, since the "conflict" is between the marker and discourse-level referential continuity rather than between two linguistically supplied values within the token. Compare *\mention{These books is useful}*, where both conflicting values are linguistically supplied. If SS/DS conflict turns out to be external misapplication or discourse incongruence rather than internal incompatibility, the flagship illustration of the discourse-consequence prediction collapses. The paper needs a principled account of where referential continuity information "lives" — grammar, semantics, or pragmatics — before using switch-reference as its paradigm case.

**Case 4: Misspelling and graphic realization (§6.1, Table 4).** The claim that orthography "normally maps graphic forms to linguistic expressions" but can come to realize constructional values is right and underexploited. English *read*/*read* (present/past) is a case where graphic contrast realizes a grammatical value and spelling errors therefore have grammatical consequences in writing-specific repertoires. The account handles this; a worked example would strengthen the medium-relativity claim considerably.

**Case 5: Register mismatch (Table 4, §6.1).** The paper says a register mismatch "can likewise preserve the proposition and the form's conventional social-indexical value while deploying that value in an unsuitable situation" — assembly succeeds, deployment fails. But consider a speaker who uses plain form where honorific form is obligatory in the local repertoire. Many communities treat this as a *mistake*, not merely a faux pas — correctable, attributable, accountable. The paper's own normativity vocabulary ("sharply sanctioned," §4) suggests these cases feel normative in exactly the way grammatical errors do. The account's resources permit saying the honorific choice is grammatically organized *and* the deployment failed; but it should explain why the felt fault character differs (or doesn't) across communities, since that difference is data for Objection 1's question about what makes faults normative.

## Part IV. Claim taxonomy: constitutive proposals, causal explanations, empirical conjectures

Applying the paper's own discipline to itself:

**Constitutive proposals (asserted, insufficiently argued):**
- "Recurrent practice supplies normativity" (abstract); the "constitutive reliance" bridge (§3.1) — Objection 1.
- "A relation's construction-internal role supplies grammar-specificity" (abstract) — Objection 4(i).
- Exclusion of pure phonotactics (Table 2, §3.1).
- The claim that internal value incompatibility alone "establishes a grammatical fault by itself" (§4) — follows from the ontology, but only as strongly as the ontology is argued.

**Causal explanations (appropriately deferred):**
- Processing, schooling, stigma, authority effects on response (§2, Fig. 1, §6) — correctly treated as response-level causes, not status determinants. This is one of the paper's strengths.

**Empirical conjectures (properly flagged as predictions):**
- Held-out attribution prediction (§1, §6, first consequence).
- Discourse-profile → repair-locus prediction (§5.2, §6, third consequence).
- Boundary-movement prediction (§6, second consequence).

**Unsupported transitions to repair:**
1. From "production and interpretation converge on a relation" to "such a relation supplies a standard against which novel tokens conform or fail" (§3.1) — the normativity gap (Objection 1).
2. From "socially sustained" to "response-independent" without a constitution relation (Objection 3).
3. From independently identified grammatical paradigms to the specific repair-locus prediction (§5.2) — the inference assumes internal-conflict errors are identifiable in advance of the discourse profile, which the Amele case shows is nontrivial.
4. From "scoped repertoire" to determinate scope in each worked example (Objection 2).

## Part VI. What revisions would answer the objections

1. **Normativity (Obj. 1).** Either defend a deflationary convention-theoretic reading explicitly, or supply a substantive mechanism converting reliance into normativity. The repair-system literature the paper already cites is the most promising constitutive resource: characterize other-initiation of repair as a publicly organized accountability structure and show how it grounds the possibility of "wrong rather than outvoted."

2. **Scope (Obj. 2).** Give independent criteria for repertoire individuation; state how partial and unequal reliance aggregates; add at least a schematic dynamic story about equilibrium maintenance (self-enforcing vs. sanction-maintained vs. prestige-driven), and show the *le hiver* analysis is robust across reasonable scope variation.

3. **Constitution relation (Obj. 3).** Specify what constitutes the norm at a time — dispositions, higher-order expectations, transmission lineage, or institutional record — and trace the consequences for innovation and change. One honest paragraph choosing among these would transform the paper's rigor.

4. **Construction notion (Obj. 4).** Disambiguate "assembly" so it does not silently inherit whatever the construction inventory contains; address the phonotactics exclusion with an argument; confront the graded-honorific consequence for the original selective-classification promise.

5. **Indeterminacy budget (Obj. 5).** State explicitly which motivating cases the account classifies determinately and which fall to grain-, scope-, or role-indeterminacy, and defend the residual zone.

6. **Amele (case test).** Validate or clearly demote Table 5; clarify whether SS/DS conflict is an internal value incompatibility given where referential continuity is encoded.

7. **Verification pass** on all flagged literature, with page-specific claims checked.

## Verdict

**MAJOR CONCEPTUAL REVISION.**

The level-separation framework, the independent-profiling methodology, and the explicit defeat conditions are genuine contributions, and the paper is admirably disciplined about keeping causal and evidential questions apart. But the two constitutive centerpieces — the reliance-to-normativity bridge and the construction-internal-role criterion — are currently asserted where they need to be argued, the scope-fixing mechanism invites gerrymandering, and the synchronic/diachronic settlement is asserted rather than specified. None of these problems is fatal; each has identifiable repair paths sketched above. The paper's own §6 standards are stringent enough that, applied to itself, they demand the revisions listed. With those in place, this could become a serious and unusual contribution at the intersection of philosophy of language and social epistemology.
