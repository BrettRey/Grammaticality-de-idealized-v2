# Review: "Why some interpretable deviations count as ungrammatical: linguistic norms and levels of explanation"

## 1. Main claim and charitable reconstruction

**Main claim.** A token counts as ungrammatical, rather than semantically anomalous, ill-worded, misspelled, mispronounced, or socially improper, exactly when it fails a socially sustained norm whose function is to relate already-individuated signs, exponents, or values within a recurrent construction in a scoped repertoire: recurrent practice supplies the normativity, and the relation's construction-internal role supplies the grammar-specificity.

**Reconstruction.** The argument, at its strongest:

1. The explanandum is selective classification. The same degree of interpretability and the same expressive substance receive different fault classifications (*le hiver*, *goed*, register mismatch, misspelling), so neither meaning-failure nor substance can be what makes a fault grammatical. An asterisk records a classification without explaining it.
2. Six questions are habitually compressed into one: what constitutes a grammatical norm, whether a token conforms, what consequence follows, what a response targets, what causes the response, and what warrants the analyst. The source-ambiguity literature (Hofmeister et al., Leivada & Westergaard) already shows that judgment data underdetermine cause; the paper generalizes this into a five-bearer architecture in which no level is a proxy for another.
3. On a constructionist ontology (Goldberg, Sag), words and schematic patterns alike are form–meaning pairings, so conventionality and meaningfulness cannot themselves mark the grammatical. The needed differentia is functional: grammatical norms govern the filling of constructional positions, value selection and combination, obligatory contrasts, and the realization of selected values, and these are conditions internal to whether material tokens the construction at all. Lexical, phonotactic, orthographic, and deployment norms occupy different functional roles, and the roles can intersect in one token.
4. Normativity comes from constitutive reliance: conformity to the relation is part of what counts, within the practice, as successfully tokening the construction. Hence a token can fulfil its communicative intention and still be defective, and a judgment can misfire without removing or creating the defect.
5. Candidate relations are individuated without the labels to be explained: recurrence, contrastive opposition, substitution, and held-out predictive distribution. Mixed analyses need independent attestation for each layer; where evidence supports unitary and relational analyses equally, the grain is indeterminate rather than "partly grammatical."
6. Downstream theories then earn their keep at the right level: Abrusán-style semantic repairability explains a subset of anomaly classifications; a discourse-consequence profile predicts repair locus (reference-directed for internal value conflicts, form-directed for value-preserving realization failures) without either becoming a membership criterion.
7. The whole package states defeat conditions: the ontology fails if its roles can't place the boundary cases without recycling labels, and its empirical reach fails if the profiles add no held-out expectation beyond rivals.

## 2. Desk-editor impression and reader takeaway

**Ten seconds.** Careful, dense, taxonomy-forward, and honest about its limits, but forbidding: five tables before the first fully worked example, and a citation profile (Danish functional grammar, constructionist syntax, typology) that reads oddly for *Linguistics and Philosophy*, where the natural interlocutors on norms, conventions, and public languages (the Lewisian and normativity-of-meaning literatures) are absent. A desk editor will ask whether this is a philosophy paper or a theoretical-linguistics position paper wearing philosophical dress.

**Sixty seconds.** A reader will take the paper to contribute (i) a disciplined disentangling of what "grammaticality" is predicated of, which genuinely dissolves several standing confusions about judgments as evidence; (ii) a constitutive proposal, the assembly-norm account, positioned against strict-paradigm and Boye–Harder discursive-status accounts as a third live option; and (iii) an unusually self-exacting methodology: individuate norms without grammar labels, test attribution on held-out data, and accept stated defeat conditions. The reader will also notice that all empirical constraint is promissory, and that the philosophical load-bearing joint, how recurrent practice yields genuine normativity, is asserted in roughly one paragraph.

## 3. Three strongest contributions

1. **The level architecture (§1–§2).** "Treating these as measurements of one latent yes–no property hides the dependence relations that need explanation" (§2). Distinguishing target attribution from response causation as two questions about the same public response is a real advance over the acceptability/grammaticality distinction as usually drawn, and Table 1 makes the separation usable.
2. **The functional counterfactual test (§3.1).** "Hold lexical identities, the intended constructional values, and the situation of use fixed" (§3.1). This is a genuine constitutive proposal, not a relabeling: it says what would have to differ for a norm to be grammatical rather than phonotactic or orthographic, and it correctly makes the French case conditional rather than stipulated. The companion three-way split of value errors in §4 ("only internal incompatibility establishes a grammatical fault by itself") is the sharpest single analytical tool in the paper.
3. **The anti-circularity methodology with defeat conditions (§6).** "A study can't identify a grammatical norm from the same grammar labels it later claims to explain" (§6). The insistence that profiles be fixed before responses are examined, that correction corpora are selection-biased, and that some outcomes are "inconclusive rather than favourable or fatal" is a model of how to make an ontology answerable to evidence.

Points of agreement, including non-obvious ones: I agree that responses are not truth-makers; that substance can't mark the boundary; that mixed labels can't rescue an indeterminate grain; and I learned something from the observation that a construction-directed response isn't automatically grammar-directed because lexical constructions sit inside the disputed boundary.

## 4. Three strongest objections

**Objection 1 (conceptual defect): the normativity bridge is one paragraph deep, and it faces a determination problem it doesn't address.** The paper's central plank is "practice supplies normativity by supplying conditions of token identity and success" (§3.1), built from Miller (2021) plus "constitutive reliance." Two gaps. First, reliance on a relation for prediction and production is behavioral regularity plus expectation; the step from that to a *standard* a token can fail is precisely what the philosophical literature on convention and the normativity of meaning disputes, and none of it is engaged (Lewis's *Convention*, Millikan on conventions and proper functions, Glüer & Wikforss on whether meaning is normative at all — all cited here from memory and **requiring verification** before use). Second, the gerrymandering worry: when all production and interpretation to date is consistent with relation R and with an extensionally deviant R′, what fact about the practice makes it sustain R rather than R′? "Participants rely on a candidate relation when predicting one another's behaviour" doesn't answer this; it restates it. Relatedly, the paper presupposes that "public, repertoire-relative" status is a legitimate target without confronting the standing skepticism about public languages as objects (Chomsky's E-language critique; Davidson's "A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs" — again from memory, **requiring verification**). An L&P referee will raise all three within the first read. What would answer it: a section that states the determination conditions (pre-emption structure, counterfactual robustness of substitution behavior, or a Millikan-style lineage story) and defends the public target against the individualist alternative rather than merely setting it aside as a "related target."

**Objection 2 (conceptual defect, surfacing as an evidential gap): the account can't yet draw its own line between constructional licensing and collocational preference without smuggling in frequency or labels.** See §5 below for the *depend of* / *strong rain* pair. The paper disclaims frequency ("isn't identical to frequency, an explicit rule, a majority vote") and withholds judgments, yet the operative difference between a licensing failure and a dispreferred-but-licensed combination looks like exactly one of those two things. The stated projection criterion doesn't separate the cases (see below). What would answer it: an explicit statement of the practice-level fact that makes a restriction a *tokening condition* rather than a *preference within tokening*, demonstrated on this pair. Pre-emption asymmetries or categorical-vs-gradient substitution behavior might do it, but the paper must say so and show that the criterion isn't opportunity-normalized frequency under another name.

**Objection 3 (evidential gap plus exposition).** Every empirical constraint is promissory. The abstract's "held-out prediction constrains the account" is, on the page, "held-out prediction *would* constrain the account": the two predictions of §1 and three consequences of §6 are designs, and the one worked empirical showcase (Amele) is explicitly "a source-grounded template that requires validation, not an empirical result." That candor is creditable, but the paper's answer to the circularity charge leans on tests not run. Under **source claims needing verification**: the Amele forms and the vowel-harmony behavior attributed to Roberts (1987: 239–240, 298–300, 371) must be checked against the grammar, since one candidate form is author-constructed by applying a harmony rule; the Boye–Harder summary (focusability and addressability as diagnostics, obligatoriness as incidental) and the Nørgård-Sørensen & Heltoft paradigm criteria should be verified against the sources, as should the Coseriu characterization, which is filtered through two secondary sources. Under **exposition**: "value" is never glossed despite carrying the account's Saussurean weight in its very name ("form–value assembly"); a fresh reader can't tell whether it means semantic content, structuralist oppositional value, or both, and the difference matters for whether "internal value incompatibility" is a semantic or a distributional notion. The first fully worked example arrives in §3.2, after roughly a third of the paper. What would answer it: run even one corpus-based pilot of prediction 1, or reframe the predictions as a research program and stop claiming present constraint; define "value" at first use; move a worked case into §1 or §2.

## 5. Boundary cases tested against the account's own machinery

1. ***Le hiver* (§6.1): derived, conditionally — pass.** The paper does what it promises: the grammatical classification follows only if the elision relation is tied to the article construction rather than general phonotactics, the deciding evidence (restriction to a function-word class, *h muet* vs *h aspiré*) is specifiable without labels, and a defeater is stated ("if independent evidence shows that respondents are reacting only to a general phonological constraint, the grammatical analysis loses"). This is the account working as designed.
2. ***Goed* (§6.2): mostly derived, with a gap in the machinery.** The mixed classification (realization relation plus item-specific restriction) follows from the individuation criteria. But notice what the violated norm is: *goed* satisfies the productive V-*ed* relation perfectly; the fault is that the repertoire supplies *went* for this lexeme. The account therefore needs pre-emption or blocking, a relation *between* an item-specific realization norm and a productive construction, and while §3.2's "item-specific pre-emption" names it in Table 5, the ontology never says what kind of norm a blocking relation is or how it meets the counterfactual test. The classification is right, but one step is gestured at rather than derived.
3. ***Depend of* vs *strong rain* (§3.2, §6.2): the classification is assumed, not derived.** This is the case that fails. Both involve a socially sustained, item-restricted selection between independently recurring signs inside an independently supported construction. The paper's discriminator is projection across derived forms: *dependence on*, *dependent on* preserve the preposition, so the restriction sits in an assembly chain. But collocations project the same way: *heavy rain*, *heavy rainfall*, *it rained heavily* preserve the lexical pairing across category shifts, so projection can't be what puts *depend–on* in the licensing chain and *heavy–rain* outside it. The remaining difference, that the *depend of* restriction is categorical while *strong rain* is preferential, is either a frequency fact (disclaimed as constitutive) or a judgment fact (withheld by the method). Table 5's hedged locus, "valence or collocation," quietly concedes the problem. Since placing exactly such cases without labels is the account's stated success condition ("The constitutive account fails if its functional roles can't place... mixed constructional cases without relying on the responses to be explained"), this is the case on which the paper's own defeat condition bites.
4. **Register/honorific mismatch (§6.3): derived, conditionally — pass.** Assembly-success-plus-deployment-failure follows from the roles, the grammaticalization path is handled correctly (present role, not historical origin, decides), and the account rightly refuses to let social seriousness relocate the fault.
5. **Amele (§6.4): a template, self-flagged.** The internal-conflict vs realization-failure contrast is exactly the right test shape for the discourse-consequence hypothesis, and the paper's own caveats (the disharmonic stimulus is constructed; SS/DS choice is conditioned by factors beyond subject identity) are the caveats a referee would otherwise write. But as it stands the section illustrates the ontology's expressive power, not its truth.

## 6. Strongest rival and a distinguishing observation

The strongest rival on the page is **Boye & Harder (2012)**: grammatical status as conventional ancillarity and discursive secondary status. The paper treats the comparison with exemplary fairness (§3.3) but leaves it abstract, "possible cross-classification... requires independent evidence for both properties."

A concrete distinguishing observation is available in the paper's own best tool: **value-preserving realization failures.** *Goed* and *went* carry identical content with identical discourse status; nothing ancillary or secondary differs between them. A discursive-status account therefore has no resources of its own to classify the *goed* fault as grammatical rather than lexical, whereas the assembly account locates it in the realization relation directly. Conversely, personal-pronoun paradigms (obligatory person/number/case choice, yet fully focusable and addressable material) would pressure the assembly account's rival in the other direction if Boye & Harder indeed classify focusable items as lexical, a characterization I hold from memory and **mark as requiring verification** against their 2012 paper. Adding one such worked contrast would convert §3.3's table from a map of positions into an argument.

## 7. Ranked revisions

1. **Build the normativity bridge.** Expand the constitutive-reliance paragraph into a section that (a) engages the convention and normativity-of-meaning literature, (b) answers the gerrymandering/determination problem, and (c) defends public repertoires as legitimate objects against individualist skepticism, rather than bracketing it. This is the paper's foundation and its thinnest point for this venue.
2. **Derive the licensing/collocation line.** State the practice-level fact that makes a restriction a tokening condition rather than a preference, without appeal to frequency or withheld judgments, and demonstrate it on *depend of* vs *strong rain*. If the honest answer is that this pair is indeterminate at current evidence, say so explicitly; the account's indeterminacy verdict is available and would be more credible than the current hedge.
3. **Supply the blocking machinery.** Say what kind of norm pre-emption is and how an item-specific realization norm outranks a satisfied productive construction, so the *goed* classification is derived rather than described.
4. **Recalibrate the empirical claims.** Either run a pilot of prediction 1 (a small corpus-based attribution study would do) or rewrite the abstract and §1 so that held-out prediction is offered as a program, not a present constraint; verify the Amele material against Roberts (1987) and either validate the constructed forms or move the section to an explicitly programmatic register.
5. **Sharpen the rival comparison.** Add the *goed*-type distinguishing case (and, if verification supports it, the focusable-paradigm case) to §3.3, so the Boye–Harder dispute has at least one observation that would move it.

## 8. Verdict

**MAJOR CONCEPTUAL REVISION.** The architecture is sound, the self-imposed discipline is unusual and welcome, and nothing here requires reconstruction. But the two joints that carry the whole account, the practice-to-normativity step and the ability to place item-restricted licensing without recycling frequency or labels, are respectively underbuilt and not yet working, and both sit exactly where this journal's referees will press first.
