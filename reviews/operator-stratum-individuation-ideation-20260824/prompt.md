# Adversarial ideation brief: individuating grammatical assembly relations

We are revising a philosophical linguistics paper that proposes this constitutive
account:

> Within a scoped public repertoire, a grammatical norm is a socially sustained
> standard that relates already individuated signs, exponents, or values within
> recurrent constructions. A token is grammatical or ungrammatical relative to
> whether it conforms to that relation. Judgments and repairs are fallible evidence,
> not truth-makers.

The live objection is that *construction*, *assembly*, and the grain at which a
relation is described may covertly recover the desired grammatical/lexical
classification. Under a Construction Grammar inventory, words, idioms, partly
filled patterns, and schematic constructions are all signs. Almost any apparent
counterexample might be decomposed into a grammatical relation plus a neighbouring
lexical, phonological, orthographic, or usage relation.

Develop a discriminating, non-circular answer to these questions:

1. How can sign and construction types be identified without first classifying
   them as lexical or grammatical?
2. What evidence warrants analysing a pattern as a relation among independently
   identified signs, exponents, or values rather than as part of the identity of a
   larger lexical sign?
3. When is a mixed analysis independently required, rather than introduced to
   protect the ontology?
4. Which residual cases should remain genuinely indeterminate rather than being
   called partly grammatical?
5. How should the paper compare a head-specific complementation restriction such
   as English *depend on NP*/*depend of NP* with an ordinary collocational contrast
   such as *heavy rain*/*strong rain*? State what evidence could reverse the initial
   analysis.
6. Does a public-repertoire account need a limitation concerning individual
   competence, innovation, emerging patterns, and formal idiolect grammars?

Constraints:

- Do not conduct a literature search or rely on grammaticality labels as evidence.
- Do not solve the problem by declaring all constructions grammatical.
- Pure phonotactics should remain outside grammar unless a phonological relation is
  conditioned by, or realizes, a grammatical choice.
- Mixed cases are allowed only under independently stated criteria.
- Prefer an honest indeterminate result to a protected classification.

Return:

1. your strongest proposed individuation rule;
2. the two best counterexamples to it;
3. a worked *depend on* versus *heavy rain* comparison;
4. a 250--400-word candidate subsection suitable for a philosophy-of-linguistics
   article; and
5. a verdict: whether the constitutive account survives this objection, and on
   what condition.
